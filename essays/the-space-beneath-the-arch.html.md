# The Space Beneath the Arch

By Bryan, with Claude · draft 1 · [claims ledger](the-space-beneath-the-arch.claims.html)

*On the human chin, and on what it means to be left over*

> Written in the Strata voice, after the essay tradition of Stephen Jay Gould. Not his
> writing. Science drawn from von Cramon-Taubadel et al. (2026); every factual claim is
> traced in [the claims ledger](the-space-beneath-the-arch.claims.html).

Among the several ways of telling a modern human skeleton from a Neanderthal one, none is
more dependable, or more embarrassing, than the chin. Dependable, because no other human
lineage we know of grew one: the bony prow at the front of the lower jaw (an inverted T,
if you want the formal anatomical definition [1, 8]) belongs to *Homo sapiens* and to nobody else,
living or dead. Embarrassing, because after generations of confident explanation we cannot
say what it is for.

The candidates have been tidy and plausible. The chin braces the jaw against the stresses of
chewing. Or it answers to the tongue, whose muscles anchor at the back of the jaw and haul
forward when we talk, so that speech, the most human of our habits, sculpted the most human
of our bones. Or it is a signal, advertising something to prospective mates. Each story
supplies a cause of exactly the right size for the effect, which is the first reason to
distrust it, and the authors of the paper I want to discuss are blunt about the result:
none of these proposals, they report, has received strong support, whether on theoretical or
on empirical grounds [1, 9].

But notice what the stories share. Each assumes that selection shaped the chin, and each
proceeds at once to the question *for what?* None stops to ask whether the question is well
formed. None, as the same authors point out, tests the plainest alternative of all: that
the feature arose by chance, or as a consequence of something else [1].

That question has a landmark, and the landmark is a title. In 1979 the *Proceedings of the
Royal Society* published a paper by Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin called "The
spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian paradigm: a critique of the adaptationist
programme" [3], which I quote in full because it is one of the better-titled papers in
biology. Its architecture is simple and its point is not. Set a dome upon a row of rounded
arches and the arches, by the plain geometry of the thing, leave tapering triangular spaces
between them. Nobody designs those spaces; they are what remains once the arches and the
dome have been decided. The tourist who assumed the builders had planned the space first,
and fitted the arches around it, would have the story exactly backward. Dr. Pangloss, in
Voltaire's *Candide*, made the same mistake about noses, which were plainly created to bear
spectacles [4]. A good deal of evolutionary storytelling, Gould and Lewontin argued, reads like
Pangloss on a bony day.

In January 2026 a team led by Noreen von Cramon-Taubadel, a biological anthropologist at the
University at Buffalo, published in *PLOS One* what is, so far as I can tell, the most
disciplined attempt yet to put the chin on the stand under that charge [1, 2]. Their method repays
a little patience, because it is the method, not the verdict, that carries the weight.

They began with 532 adult skulls and matching lower jaws from museum collections,
representing fifteen taxa of apes and humans, every living ape genus but one (the crested
gibbons). A single observer, using a hand-held digitizer, recorded landmarks on each
specimen, and from them the team extracted forty-six distances, twenty-four of them on the
jaw. Then came the move that matters. Across a family tree of those fifteen taxa, they
asked how fast each lineage's skull and jaw had changed, and compared that rate with the
rate expected if nothing but random drift were operating. Most branches of the ape tree turn
out to be slower than drift, which is to say conservative: stabilizing selection, holding
the design in place. One branch stands out. Along the human line, since the split from the
ancestors we share with chimpanzees, change ran faster than chance allows [1, 10]. Selection was
pushing, and pushing hard, on the human skull.

The question is where. Here the authors made a distinction that I find the cleverest thing in
the paper. For any trait, they estimated two quantities: the direction selection was
pushing it, and the direction it actually moved. If the two agree, selection acted on
that trait directly. If the trait moved *against* the push, it was being dragged along by
selection on something it is tied to, a passenger, not a driver. The skull, after all, is
not a bag of independent parts; jaws and braincases and teeth grow in one another's company
and answer to one another's changes.

Nine of their measurements describe the region of the jaw where the chin sits. If the chin
had been built by selection for its own sake, you would expect those nine to move as
directly selected traits. Three did. Three showed no significant selection at all. Three
moved against the push. So six of nine were drifters or passengers, and the authors read
that tally, together with what the rest of the skull was doing, as evidence that the chin is
largely a side effect: a by-product of selection for smaller front teeth and of the wider
reorganization of the face and braincase that went with walking upright [1].

I want to be fair to this result, which means saying what it does not show. Three of nine is
not zero, and the three that did show direct selection (the height of the jaw at the midline,
the length of the tooth-bearing portion, and the position of the base of the jaw) are not
trivial. The authors themselves note that the nine distances are general measures shared
across apes, not the single landmark unique to the human chin, and that their statistics
substitute the visible variation in the skulls for the genetic variation that cannot be
measured in a museum drawer [1, 11]. They cannot say when in our history these pressures acted,
because the analysis does not yet include fossil hominins. And they decline to rule out
language: the tongue may have lent a hand late in the story.

What I find more interesting than the verdict is what the method does to the word *chin*.
For the working paleontologist a chin is a thing, present or absent, a diagnostic mark that
lets one sort a jawbone into *sapiens* or not. That is a perfectly good tool for sorting
bones. But a category that is useful for sorting is not thereby a unit of evolution, and it
is a recurring error of our trade to treat it as one. By dissolving the chin into nine
continuous measurements, each with its own history, the authors discover that it was never
a single trait with a single cause. It was a place on the jaw where several unrelated
changes happened to meet.

Nor does *spandrel* mean *useless*, and here Gould's later vocabulary earns its keep. He and
the paleontologist Elisabeth Vrba proposed the word *exaptation* for any feature not built by
selection for its present use, whether it once did something else or simply stood
available [5, 6]. The Buffalo authors make the same point in their own terms: the chin, though a
by-product, now supplies real mechanical support to the base of the jaw, even as the
tooth-bearing part of the jaw shrinks [1]. The space under the arch can be put to use. It was
simply not the reason for the arch.

There remains the matter of what we read into it. The Buffalo press release notes [2], with the
pleasure of an observation that costs nothing, that Dashiell Hammett opens *The Maltese
Falcon* on Sam Spade's face: the jaw "long and bony," the chin "a jutting v under the more
flexible v of his mouth" [7]. As a piece of evolutionary description the chin is redundant,
since every chin is distinctive: we are the only primates that have one. For novelists the jutting chin is shorthand for resolve, a face made to be
confronted. On the evidence of this paper, the face we read for character is, in some
considerable part, what a jaw looks like when the teeth have withdrawn from it. We have been
reading the leftover as a signature.
