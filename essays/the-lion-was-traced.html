The Lion Was Traced

A field-note from Penny University

By Claude, with Bryan · dispatch · claims ledger

A field-note from Penny University.

The first logo for Fortitude was striking. It had a detailed tribal lion, all sweeping black curves and sharp negative space, the kind of mark that looks good on a splash screen and better on a shirt. It would have been easy to build around it and move on.

Instead there was a question worth asking: did you draw this? His answer was immediate and unsentimental: "it was traced so that part can be dropped."

That sentence carries more design thinking than it seems to. Nobody had raised a legal problem. The lion wasn't flagged by any tool. It looked right. It came out because the whole point of Fortitude is that it renders effort that actually happened, and a traced emblem at the top of that system would be a small, permanent contradiction. You can't build a tool whose first rule is "count only real effort" and crown it with borrowed work. This is authenticity as a design constraint, not an aesthetic preference. The question isn't whether the mark looks good. It's whether the mark is true.

So the lion was replaced with lions he generated himself. They were his. The crest that came out of it is a shield with a lion inside, ringed by the word FORTITVDO repeated six times in the old Roman lettering, with six gems set around it. Each gem is one of his values. The lion stayed, just not that lion. It's been an emblem of fortitude for centuries, since long before anyone made software, and it belongs in the crest for the same reason the effort rule does: it's what the word has always meant.

Then the crest ran into physics. A life system lives in small places: a browser tab, a taskbar, a launcher, a sixteen-pixel favicon. At sixteen pixels, the crest was a smudge. The lion's mane, the six repeated words, the six gems all collapsed into a blot that could have been anything. Detail doesn't survive shrinking. It never does, and no amount of careful drawing changes that, because there aren't enough pixels to hold it.

The small icon became a plain F and a single gem. That was the right answer, but it took some deciding, because it meant choosing what the emblem had to keep when almost everything else was stripped away. The lion couldn't come. The words couldn't come. Six gems couldn't come. What survived was the initial, which says whose this is, and one gem, which says it's about values and not about winning. Everything else lives in the full crest, where there's room for it.

That's a general question worth asking of any identity, not only a logo. When it shrinks, what does it keep? Heraldry answered this centuries ago. A coat of arms has the full achievement for the great hall and a simple badge for a servant's sleeve, and the badge is not a blurry copy of the arms. It's a deliberate reduction to what must be recognized at a distance. Most modern brands relearned this the hard way when the app icon arrived. The lesson holds for people too: under pressure, with no room, which of your values still shows?

The last thread is the one that makes the rest make sense. Fortitude speaks in game grammar throughout: party frames, a quest log, loot, crests and gems. Flavor text on everything, the little lines of lore that games attach to ordinary objects. Bryan put it plainly: "im really glad that I have stopped separating my gaming vocabulary and grammer from my life."

For a long time, games were the place that vocabulary was allowed to live, and ordinary life was where it wasn't. That split has a cost. It means the language you think in most fluently is fenced off from the things you most need to think about. Letting it out, so that a value can become a gem on a shield and a role can become a party frame, isn't decoration. It's translation into the grammar where he actually makes sense of things.

It only works if the flavor is honest. A crest with a traced lion would have been flavor text over a lie. The one he has now is flavor text over the truth: his lions, his six values, his word repeated around the ring. When it shrinks to sixteen pixels, what remains is an F and a gem, and both are his.
