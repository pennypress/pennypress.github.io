The Tools to See the Options

By Bryan, with Claude · draft 2 · claims ledger

Grows from this sentence of the root essay, Judgment

We have the chance to reflect on the stimulus before us, and we have more tools than Bigger ever had for seeing what our options are.

The paragraph answering the first objection. Read it in place · see the whole bush

Bryan, with Claude. Made by co-thinking: Bryan set the question and steers the argument; Claude drafted from his earlier papers and the sources in the claims ledger, and Bryan decides what stays.

In 2018 Bryan sat, on paper, as the judge of Bigger Thomas. A philosophy course on compassion asked him to decide whether the young man at the center of Richard Wright's Native Son deserves it. He decided Bigger should live. Answering the prosecution's claim that only the fear of execution protects the public, he wrote the sentence at the top of this page, almost in passing: we in the courtroom can stop and think before we act, and we have more help in that thinking than the defendant ever had.

Read eight years later, the sentence says more than it meant to. If the judge has tools for seeing his options and Bigger did not, those tools are not simply part of being human. They can be handed over or held back. The space where a person stops and chooses can be built, shared, or denied.

This essay tests an idea that grows from that: that compassion includes giving another person the tools to see their options, and that this is also what intelligence augmentation is for. By intelligence augmentation (IA) we mean a person steering while a tool helps, as opposed to a machine built to think in the person's place.

A sentence with no source

Most people know a version of the thought behind Bryan's sentence: between stimulus and response there is a space, and in that space is our power to choose our response. It is usually credited to Viktor Frankl, the psychiatrist who survived the Nazi camps. We credited it to him ourselves in an earlier paper, and have since corrected it.

It is probably not his. Quote Investigator, a site that traces quotations, looked for it in Frankl's works and did not find it. The words were popularized by the self-help writer Stephen Covey, who said he read them in a library book in Hawaii and could never recall the book or its author. Years later he went back and found the library building gone. The best candidate for what he half-remembered is a 1963 article by the psychologist Rollo May:

"Freedom is thus not the opposite to determinism. Freedom is the individual's capacity to know that he is the determined one, to pause between stimulus and response and thus to throw his weight, however slight it may be, on the side of one particular response among several possible ones."

A line passed on without a source is a line nobody can check. May's real sentence also settles a problem in Bryan's 2018 essay.

Could not have done otherwise

"Judgment" makes two claims that pull against each other. Early on it says Bigger "lacked free will, in the sense that he could not have done other than what he did." Later it says "I see Bigger as capable of growth, while Buckley would have us see him as fixed." A man who could never have done otherwise does not sound like a man who can grow.

A later paper of ours, "The Genie and the Gap," worked out a view of free will that removes the conflict, and May says it in one breath: freedom is not the opposite of being caused. In that paper, agency is a capacity that comes in degrees: taking in what is happening, weighing options against reasons you would stand behind, and letting the weighing change what you do. It is caused, it can be trained, and it can be lost. Roughly, it is the width of the gap between impulse and act times the number of real options visible inside it. In the Daltons' house on the night of the killing, Bigger's gap was almost nothing and held almost no options. "Judgment" already says so: "his actions were scripted rather than chosen" (84-85), and "Had his inner dialogue been developed enough to show him more than one option, we would not be in this court today."

So I would put the old sentence differently now. In 2018 I wrote that Bigger "lacked free will, in the sense that he could not have done other than what he did." Today I would write that he could not have done otherwise, given what he could see. The first sentence closes the case on him. The second points at what was missing, and at who could have supplied it.

"The Genie and the Gap" adds the point the rest of this essay turns on: nobody widens the gap alone. The inner voice that deliberates is, as the paper puts it following the psychologist Lev Vygotsky, an internalized conversation with other people. A self in a vacuum has an empty gap. "Judgment" gives a whole paragraph to Bigger's vacuum: a father dead when he was young (74), a community he held himself apart from (114), a mother and a girlfriend he could not respect (240), and models of conquest taken from the wider world instead (115).

Compassion as handing over the means

Martha Nussbaum, in Upheavals of Thought, says compassion rests on three judgments: that something serious has happened to someone, that they did not bring it on themselves, and that their good matters within my own scheme of what is worth pursuing. Bigger did harm others, and Nussbaum allows for that. Compassion can attach to suffering "out of proportion to the fault," what she calls "the nonblameworthy increment." Her example is a teenager arrested for drunk driving: the fault is hers, and yet adolescence brings "a certain blindness." Bigger's blindness was not a phase of life. It was manufactured, and that makes the increment large.

The Dalai Lama, in Ethics for a New Millennium, describes a virtue called sö pa, usually translated as patience, whose literal sense is closer to "able to bear": "a deliberate response — as opposed to an unreasoned reaction." Bryan wrote on sö pa for the same course in 2018 and put it his own way: "Self-control tempers afflictive emotions, giving a person more room to exercise free will." That is the gap again, seen as a skill. Skills are taught, and the means of teaching them can be named. In 1962 Douglas Engelbart, whose lab later gave us the computer mouse, said a person's capability comes from artifacts, language and methods, plus the training that makes them "operationally effective," and that augmenting it "does not imply any attempt to increase native human intelligence." Nothing in "Judgment" says Bigger was stupid. He lacked the means: words for what he felt, ways of thinking a situation through, and anyone to train him in either. The judge was given all of them over a lifetime.

That is also what IA is for when it is done well. "The Genie and the Gap" reduced the difference between the two kinds of AI to one question: who holds the setpoint? Bryan works as an HVAC technician, and that is where the word comes from for him: in a building's controls, the setpoint is the number the system steers toward, and whoever sets it owns the result. A tool can surface options a person could not see, make consequences visible, and hand the decision back. The same tool can instead decide, narrow your choices to the ones it predicts you will accept, and train you to defer. The machine is identical. What differs is whose goal it steers toward.

So the thesis at full strength: compassion includes giving people the means to see their options, and a tool that widens someone's gap and hands the decision back is compassion built into an object.

Who decides which options a person gets?

The deepest objection is paternalism. To give someone "the tools to see their options" assumes the giver knows what the options are. The helper decides what help looks like, and the person helped becomes a project.

Our own sources say this first. Frankl, in a line he did write (in Man's Search for Ultimate Meaning): "we cannot 'give' meaning to the life of others." "The Genie and the Gap": "you cannot give a person agency; you can only offer the conditions and let them author." Nussbaum puts it as a distinction between capability and functioning: the aim is the real opportunity to do a thing, not being made to do it. The Amish can support everyone's right to vote while believing voting is wrong for them; compulsory voting would violate them, and the unused right does not.

Paternalism also has a larger form, and Native Son is built around it. Bigger's deepest problem was not that he could not see his options. It was that other people had already decided, for his whole group, which options there would be. "Judgment" names the pieces: the separation of the Black Belt (173-174), a jury that could not set its prejudice aside (384), the law's protection withheld from the people the prosecution had dehumanized (374). Wright puts the type in one man. Mr. Dalton gives money to the NAACP and buys ping-pong tables for a Black youth program, and a real estate company he controls rents the Thomas family their one rat-infested room at a high rent. At the inquest in Book Three, Bigger's lawyer, Max, gets Dalton to admit that he will not rent to Black tenants outside the Black Belt. His reason: "it's an old custom." The same man chooses the help and the walls.

That is what the cage is: paternalism done by institutions, deciding for a group which options it gets, while handing out tools inside the limit it has set. Nussbaum's own approach names the trap. Resources are not justice; people need the conditions to turn them into a life. A tool for seeing your options, handed over without those conditions, is a window in a locked room. The objection cuts at IA in the same place. "The Genie and the Gap" warns that the same technology, wired the other way, gathers millions of people's setpoints into a few hands.

Structure and agency

This is the same tension as free will and determinism, one level up. Free will asks whether a person could have done otherwise. The structure question asks whether a person's life is their own doing or the doing of the institutions around them. Each has two easy answers. One says it is all structure, so the individual is excused, or erased. The other says it is all individual choice, so structure is a fiction and the stuck have only themselves to blame.

Both are wrong for the same reason the two easy answers about free will are wrong. May's sentence works here too: freedom is knowing that you are "the determined one" and still throwing your weight. Institutions really do favor some groups over others. Mr. Dalton's custom is a structure, and so are the jury and the Black Belt. And people inside those structures still choose, and can be helped to choose better. The sociologist Peter Callero argues that people cannot change their conditions without understanding the structures shaping them. On that view, seeing the structure is itself one of the tools for seeing your options.

Right now, it seems to us, the argument over this swings toward its extremes, each side treating the other's half of the truth as a lie. The structure is real and the person is real, and a politics that can hold only one of them will keep failing people like Bigger from one direction or the other.

It also answers a line in Bryan's own 2018 paper on sö pa: "Rather than changing the world, we need to start with ourselves," and only after gaining "some inner strength" take on outside challenges. For someone in Bigger's position, waiting until the inner work is finished means waiting forever. His answer now is the combination: the inner work and the outer work at once, neither one first.

The contract we owe each other

If neither the lone helper nor the institution can be trusted to decide which options a person gets, then the deciding has to be done together, and on terms nobody would refuse. That is the old idea of a social contract, and we think it is where this argument has to land.

John Rawls gave the best-known modern version. Imagine choosing the rules for a society without knowing where you will end up in it: your class, your race, your talents, your place. Behind that "veil of ignorance," he argued, you would choose equal basic liberties, fair equality of opportunity, and inequalities only where they benefit the least advantaged. The veil exists because contracts written by people who know their own advantage have built cages before. Rawls's account also holds that outside society our capacities, even for reason and language, stay undeveloped, which is the Genie paper's point about the gap put in political terms.

Nussbaum adds two things. She objects that the veil runs on prudence ("that could be me") more than on compassion, so it can raise the floor without anyone's good becoming part of anyone else's. And she gives the floor content: a list of central capabilities that a decent society guarantees to everyone at a threshold. Three of them are this essay's subject. Practical reason is the ability to form a view of the good life and reflect on your own plans. Affiliation includes "the social bases of self-respect and nonhumiliation," and protection from discrimination by race. Control over one's environment includes taking part in the political choices that govern your life. The first is the tool for seeing options. The other two are the conditions for acting on what you see. And because the list names capabilities, not functionings, the choosing stays with the person.

A contract like that answers paternalism at both scales. Nobody decides alone which options another person gets; we decide together, without knowing which of us will need them, what everyone must be able to see and do. Then each person chooses. Working out the terms is the job, and it is never finished.

What survives

The thesis that comes out of the test is this. Compassion does include giving people the tools to see their options, but given by one person to another it slides toward deciding for them, and given by an institution that also sets the walls it becomes the cage with a window in it. The honest form is a shared one: a social contract that guarantees everyone both the means to see their options and the conditions to act on them, and leaves the choice to them.

The judge in "Judgment" shows what an individual does inside that. He does not hand Bigger a book on patience. He turns his own gap on the court, sees his part in the cage, and refuses to add a death sentence to it. Whoever has the tools was given them, often by the same arrangement that kept them from someone else, so having them is closer to a debt than a credit. The debt is paid in both directions: on yourself and the structures you sit inside, and by passing the means along without deciding what they are for.

An IA tool can be held to the same terms. After using it, can the person see more of their own options, including ones the tool's owner would rather they missed? Do they hold the setpoint? Does it show them the walls as well as the paths? A tool that passes is doing, in a small way, what the contract does in a large one.

Near the end of "Judgment," Bryan asks the court to see that "one person drowning in a sea of fear and hate does not act alone." Nor does the person who gets out. The famous sentence about the space between stimulus and response came from a library book nobody can find anymore, half remembered and passed on to millions. What was passed on was real even though its source was lost, which is how most of us got our tools. The work is to pass them on more carefully than they reached us: with the source named, the decision left to the person, and the walls in plain view, and to sit down together and write the terms on which everyone gets them.
