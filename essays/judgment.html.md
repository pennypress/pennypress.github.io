# Judgment

By Bryan · 2018 third draft, edited 2026 with Claude · [claims ledger](judgment.claims.html)

> **About this text.** Bryan wrote this in December 2018 for a philosophy course on compassion. The
> assignment: sit as the judge in Bigger Thomas's trial in Richard Wright's *Native Son*, and decide
> whether he deserves compassion. This is the third draft, the last one in his hand alone, lightly
> edited in 2026 with Claude for typos and clarity. The argument and its order are his, unchanged. Page numbers
> refer to *Native Son*. It is the root of a set of essays on compassion that branch from it.

---

As the judge of this trial, I have been given the task of deciding whether compassion is warranted toward the defendant, Bigger Thomas. The lack of premeditation and the accidental nature of the original crime call for mercy. Bigger lacked free will, in the sense that he could not have done other than what he did: given the violent, racist environment that formed him, it was only a matter of time before he acted the way he had been primed to act. Several parts of his life call for compassion, and taken together they lead me to conclude that he should be given a chance to live. Bigger was denied a chance at life by a society that oppressed him and his whole people. That oppression came because of the color of his skin, not because of any defect in his merit or his conduct. If we give Bigger a chance at life, we declare that he matters, and we give ourselves a chance to recognize how much we need all of our fellow citizens in order to live full and meaningful lives.

The defense has raised several facts about Bigger's life that must be weighed. The first is that he does not stand equal under the law. No jury of white people will be able to set aside its racial prejudice and consider the facts of the case (384). In their minds, a black man is guilty before any evidence is presented, and the atmosphere around this trial makes it worse (382). Buckley opens the window and lets the chant of a mob calling for death flood into the courtroom (373). Then, having used sentiment to sway the court, he argues that sentiment should play no part in deciding the case (374). His argument against sentiment is undone by the sentiment underneath every part of his call for death. If we tried to decide this case by reason alone, we would rip out the very parts of it that allow us to decide at all. Our laws tell us which of Bigger's actions are not tolerated, but the question before us is one of discretion. We have to interpret the law, not merely enforce it, or we end up with unequal treatment. The protection of the law should also protect citizens from the state. When Buckley says he is protecting the lives and property of the people, he excludes the people he has dehumanized (374). One of the starkest examples of this inequality is Bessie's body, brought into the courtroom and used as an object (331). No one cares about a crime until it crosses the line between black and white.

Bigger Thomas has admitted his guilt, and still the prosecution insists on a parade of witnesses (381). What do they add to our knowledge? Mostly, the prosecution's showmanship adds more hate toward a target it has already dehumanized. Buckley is the embodiment of white America's hatred of its black population, and the fear that drove Bigger to act against Mary is a direct result of that hatred. Bigger was not acting against a person; he lashed out against a storm of hate (387). Neither side of this terrible event could see the other as human, because of the distance between them, physical and mental.

For the first time in his life, Bigger is thrust into an alien white world. He does not know whether to knock at the front door of the house or go around to the back (44). The line between white and black is crossed again when he is made to shake Jan's hand (66-67). Both times, his response is the same: fear and hate. That such ordinary moments between equals should provoke this shows how segregated his life was before he met the Daltons. The physical separation of the Black Belt is a large part of what keeps him trapped (173-174). In this foreign land he is unsure how to behave, or what the behavior of others means. Later he wonders whether he would be struck dead for imagining himself the equal of others (362). He is not beyond redemption, if only he would let hope into his inner dialogue.

Bigger is emotionally stunted, unable to relate to the world outside him except through violence. He would rather fight his own friends than cross the line and commit a crime against white people (36-40). Later he cannot form clear thoughts about what he has done, which exposes how instinctive his actions were. A person with an immature, underdeveloped inner life cannot be condemned in the same way as a rational agent capable of reflection; someone who is not fully self-aware calls for our pity. In Mary's bedroom on the night of the murder, Bigger was present, but his actions were scripted rather than chosen (84-85). Had his inner dialogue been developed enough to show him more than one option, we would not be in this court today. This young man had been trained from childhood to fear exactly that situation (114); it is as if he had been brainwashed. Bigger had killed before, but what he had killed was the life inside himself (284). Because of that inner death, he never developed into a whole person. His existence and his human potential were denied by the society that trained him to see the world in the narrow way he does (402). Most of us have people who help us develop, but Bigger was so isolated from kindness and love that his character never had a chance to grow.

What role models did he have, as a child or since? His father died when he was young (74). He kept apart from the black community, which he saw as resigned (114). His mother and Bessie were both too passive for him to respect (240). The two people in whose company he might have found love were, for him, cold, with no positive influence on his life. I can see, too, that Bessie was forced to live other people's lives in order to survive, which made it hard for her to live her own. Bigger looked instead to violent examples in the wider world and took invasion and conquest as his models (115). The only direction in his life is a desperate instinct to be free of fear and hate (354). The criminal justice system could only condemn him; it could not protect him from his fears (395). With nowhere to turn, and with no moral bearings because he was not connected to other people, Bigger lashed out in desperation. He was acting against forces outside his human connections, inside him and around him.

I foresee three objections, involving fear, revenge, and common humanity.

The first, raised by the prosecution, is that we cannot protect the lives and property of citizens without the fear the death penalty instills (374). My answer is that each case asks us to consider it with as much good information as we can gather: the evidence in the case and the wider consequences of what we decide. Otherwise we go on mindlessly repeating the long history of violence that this case has brought to our attention. We have the chance to reflect on the stimulus before us, and we have more tools than Bigger ever had for seeing what our options are. If we grant that Bigger is human, we must recognize that what makes life worth living has been denied to him.

The second objection is that letting Bigger live denies us revenge. But does vengeance protect us, or does it cause more suffering? We fool ourselves if we think the death penalty can protect us from someone who has nothing left to lose. Executing him will not bring Mary back to life; sparing him can give this young man a chance at a life. In allowing him that chance, we recognize that we share his struggle to be fully human. The cost of his death is that we carry on the hate and violence that did so much to cause this crime.

The third objection holds that letting Bigger live is less compassionate than putting him down: he has tasted blood and is now ruined. To see him in this demeaning, animal way ignores his humanity. What matters is that Bigger is human, though not fully developed or mature. The prosecution's insistence on denying his humanity only shows how afraid we all are to face the ugly side of ourselves. Someone might ask how my view of Bigger's humanity differs from the prosecution's. My answer is that I see Bigger as capable of growth, while Buckley would have us see him as fixed, a static thing. A person who matures can come to terms with unavoidable suffering and bear its weight with dignity. Bigger has never had the tools to build a meaningful connection with his own inner world, let alone with the complex outer world he finds so overwhelming.

Having compassion for Bigger Thomas means that it would be unacceptable to sentence anyone to death. Having compassion for this murderer means caring about our future as well as his. The prosecution would probably prefer a different judge. But to remove a judge because he sees the humanity in a person everyone else is dehumanizing would be shortsighted and dangerous. Racial hatred, carried on, can only lead to more death and, in the end, to extermination. We should recognize that one person drowning in a sea of fear and hate does not act alone. Bigger had accomplices in Mary's death. Mr. Dalton is not on trial, yet he had as much of a role in his daughter's death as any member of the white society that created the conditions under which Bigger grew up (393). Taking a person's life, even a guilty person's, feeds a cycle of hate and revenge; it solves nothing. We need to bring something as powerful as compassion into this situation, because otherwise we are on a path to destroying ourselves.
