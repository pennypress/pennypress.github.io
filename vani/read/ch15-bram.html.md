The cloud starts about as high up the ridge as the meeting-house bell. Below it there's the ordinary world: wet grass, a few fur-trees, a scree slope, Hask. Above it there's nothing, a white wall leaning on the hill. And the trees go up into it and don't come out.

I lie on my back in the moss at the very edge and look up into it. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen, and I've seen a harvester kneel.

The trees here are taller than the dome. Their trunks go up grey and slick and furred with moss, ropes and beards and cushions of it, every colour of green there is and some that are nearly gold. Halfway up, the white takes them, and somewhere up there, inside the cloud, their crowns are living a whole life we can't see. Things hang down out of the white on threads: little gardens, roots and flowers in balls of moss the size of Tilly, turning slowly, dripping. The whole forest drips. Tik. Tik. Tikatik. Like the dome after a sprinkler day, only for ever.

On the left, a waterfall comes out of the white. It's falling already when you first see it, a long rope of water, and it falls, and falls, and about the height of Saul's barn above the ground it just stops being water. It thins and frays and turns into mist and drifts away sideways. It never lands. Nothing under it is even wet.

"Where does it go?" I ask Hask.

"Up," says Hask, after some thought. "It appears to be going up."

He's right. The whole cloud's climbing, the way steam climbs off Gran's pan: warm air off the Preserve's flat land, reaching the ridge and going up it, and getting cold, and turning white. I can feel it pass over my face going uphill. It tastes of cloud: cold, and a bit like licking a spoon.

And I can't smell anything from up there. With the air going up, anything up the hill blows away from me, and all I get is what's behind me. Wet grass. Hask's lichen. Biscuit, on my chest, asleep, river and lamp oil.

I put my palm flat on the moss and listen. The forest's talking, the way the Undercroft talked: Wet. Wet. Light up here. Wet. And over it, high and steady, a hum that isn't alive. Fans.

The towers. You see them when the cloud thins: white, smooth, windowless at the bottom, three or four of them standing up out of the forest like the bones of a hand. And at the very top of the nearest, above the cloud, where the evening sun still reaches, something glittering. Glass.

That's where they've gone.

* * *

Odile and Sedge went up at dusk, with the moon coming up full out of the Pale behind us, in a long grey skiff with a blue nose light that came in low over the grain and sat on the rise by the bone-coloured station as politely as a cat. Odile in her coat, brushed, which I've never seen her bother with. Sedge in a borrowed shirt that was too long in the arms, her sleeves rolled four turns like mine. On the inside of her wrist, very faint, a tracery of green-gold, like leaf veins, that wasn't there yesterday.

She kept looking at it. She kept putting her other hand over it and taking it off again.

"You said there'd be pudding," she said to Odile.

"I said there might be."

"If there isn't pudding, I'm coming straight back down."

Odile looked at me. She didn't say stay here. She'd already said it four times, and Hask had said it twice, and I'd said yes every time.

"Below the cloud line," she said instead.

"Below the cloud line."

Sedge turned in the skiff's lit doorway and did a stupid wave, all her fingers going separately, and then the door shut, and the skiff lifted, and went up the slope over the trees, and the white took it.

Hask and I walked up to where the grass gives out and the cloud begins, which isn't far: the station's rise sits right at the foot of the ridge. Below the cloud line. Like we said.

That was two hours ago. I've tied my knot, the twenty-second. I've eaten the last of Odile's crackers, which she left me in a paper twist, the way Gran does when she goes out.

The sky-grazers are here.

Their smell was in the moss as soon as we got near: hay and wet wool and honey gone to vinegar, and bread, faint, under it.

They come down out of the white at dusk, slow and pale, mouths open, grazing the low seed. Only here there are little ones. Half-size, fat, pale as milk, their back ridges round as rolls, piping so high it's nearly not sound, blundering about beside their mothers on nothing.

This is where they come to have them. Up in the crowns.

"Hask," I say. "Look."

"I am looking," says Hask. "Item two thousand and six is very much on my mind."

One of the big ones drops lower than the others, piping a soft question, low enough that I can see its eyes, big and dark and surprised, and the edge of its left wing.

There's a nick out of it.

It hangs a hand's breadth off the moss in front of my face, smelling of the strongest bread on the slope, the way it did on the shelf above the stream with the heat-hunter coming.

Then it bumps its soft nose on my forehead. Exactly where it did, the morning it left me.

I can't say anything. I put my hand on its neck, and it's warm, and the float in its back is full and tight as a drum.

It brought something. Behind it, lower and more anxious, comes a little one. Fat, pale, new. It hangs behind its mother and looks at me past her side, and pipes.

"You had a baby," I say. My voice goes stupid. "You had a baby."

The little one burps. It's very small. It smells of bread.

Hask makes the grinding noise in the ground.

* * *

But they don't go back up.

When they've grazed, the flock rises into the white towards the crowns, the way they always do at dark, and then they come back down. Piping, worried. They try again. They come back down.

I listen through the ground a long time before I hear it. Up in the crowns, among the drips, a long faint creak, like a rope on a boat when the tide pulls. Lots of them. Strung between the trees.

Net.

"They've netted the crowns," I say.

"Who has?"

"Someone with fans." I think of thirty-one roof claims. "To keep them off the glass."

The nicked-wing grazer settles on the moss beside me with its little one tucked against its side, both of them breathing slow. Above us, in the white, the creaking goes on.

* * *

The wind turns.

I feel it happen. For hours the air's been going up past my face, into the hill. Then it stops. Everything goes very still, and the drips sound louder, and the moss stops breathing upwards. And then, slowly, cold, the air starts coming down.

Out of the cloud. Down the slope. Over me.

The cold air off the top, Hask says later, sliding down at night like water down a roof, because cold is heavier than warm. I don't know that then. I only know that for the first time since we got here, I can smell the top of the hill.

Wet stone. Moss. Cedar.

Cedar and cooked ink.

My stomach goes over. It's the smell of the corridor outside the glass room, upstairs, under the dome. The smell of the grey shirt I threw in the sea.

And then a whistle.

High, thin, from up in the white. Highwater's whistle, the one Sedge does through her fingers. Grey boat. Grey boat. It comes from the left. Then the right. Then from all round, from everywhere at once, bouncing off the trunks and the hanging gardens and the cloud itself, so I can't tell where it is.

"Hask. Where?"

"Above," says Hask. His whole stack has turned up the hill. "The ground does not carry a whistle. I cannot say where above."

The whistle stops. Halfway through.

And on the cold air, so strong I taste it, a struck match. Sedge, frightened. And next to it, a lamp turned all the way up. Odile, furious.

And round both of them, all through them, cedar and copier toner.

Pell.

Not one. Lots of them, like coats on the hooks in the meeting-house porch. All the same coat.

It's coming from the right. Not from where the whistle seemed to be. From the nearest tower.

The whistle lied. The smell doesn't.

* * *

I'm on my feet. I don't remember getting up.

Hask has come right up to the edge of the white, his top stone tipped back as if he's looking up the slope.

"Hask, can you—"

"No." Very quiet. "I weigh three hundred and forty kilograms, less three leaves. I am not built for ascent. I have never needed to be." He turns his stack to me. "The Examiner is my bearer. For three hundred and eleven years I have been, every time, between the bearer and the harm. It is what I am for."

"I know."

"I cannot be between her and this," says Hask. "You can." A pause, as long as a breath. "This is not a thing I have ever had to ask. I do not know the form."

"You don't need a form."

"I am told that frequently," says Hask. "Go."

* * *

I go up the scree on my soles and my hands, fast, and at the top of it the cloud takes me.

It's like putting your head in a sack. White. Wet. Cold that gets straight into the soft little rooms in my chest, so the ache starts at once, and the cough with it, like Gran's. I can't see my hands. I go up the first trunk by feel, and it's moss over bark over wet, and my fingers can't find anything, and I come down hard on my back in the roots.

Biscuit puts its head out of my shirt, takes one look at the cloud, and goes back in.

I lie there. Somewhere above me, a long way up, Sedge has stopped whistling.

Bram, says the voice behind my eyes. Careful.

"I can't climb it."

No.

"I can't see. I'm freezing. My hands won't hold."

No. They won't.

"Then fix it."

I've never told it that before. Every other time, it's asked.

Which?

"All of it."

Bram. Name it.

There's no time to name anything. So I grab what I've got.

Hands first.

Gerald. Master Aldous's jar beast, with the hooked feet, who walked up the inside of the glass every lesson and hung from the lid looking smug. Tilly said he had sticky feet. Master Aldous said no, not sticky: hairs, too small to see, thousands on every foot, so fine they caught on the glass the way a burr catches on wool. "Look closer, Tilly."

"Gerald's feet," I say. "On my hands. And Sedge's. The way she goes up a tree like breathing. The long bit at the ends of her fingers."

That's a beetle and a girl.

"It's what I've got."

Cold next. And I'm in the Hale kitchen with the lamp lit at the wrong time of night, and Ma in the bed by the stove, white and done in, and Dr. Ommery sitting with something red and furious and new on her knee. Tilly. Born an hour. Ommery turned her over, gentle, and put my hand flat on her back, between the shoulders, and it was hot. Hotter than the rest of her. Like a little stove.

"Brown fat," said Ommery. "Babies have it. It burns to keep them warm without shivering, because they're too small to shiver properly. We lose most of it when we grow." She looked at me over Tilly's head. "What did you learn today, Bram?"

"Tilly's back," I tell the voice. "The stove between her shoulders. All over me."

That's not a name, that's a baby.

"It's what I've got."

Eyes last. A knee-deer at my pond, my first week, turning its head, and two green lamps coming on in the dark. Pim at Highwater, owl-wide, looking at me across a black platform as if it was noon. The Primer's page with an eye cut in half like an onion, and the bit at the back that catches the light.

"The shine," I say. "At the back. Like the knee-deer. Like Pim."

The voice doesn't answer for a moment.

That's four, it says. Four at once. And not one of them named properly. A beetle and a girl and a baby and a deer. It's not cross. It's frightened. I've never heard it frightened. I hold you by your names, Bram. Everything you've named, I can keep the shape of while I work. Everything you haven't, I'm holding by habit. If I do four, on these names, all at once, I'll need all of me to keep them straight. Something will slip.

"What?"

I don't know. Something you never named. I won't know till it's gone.

Up in the white, nobody's whistling.

"Yes," I say.

Bram—

"Yes."

* * *

It's like being a loaf when the oven door shuts.

The heat goes through me first, from between my shoulders, out, down my arms and legs and into my fingers and toes, and the cold I've been shaking with is just gone, as if someone took a wet coat off me. I lie in the roots and steam. Actual steam, coming off my skin into the cloud.

Then my hands.

They itch, then burn, then ache like after carrying the milk pails up from the gate. I hold them up and I can see them, which means my eyes are going too, and they're wrong. The fingers are longer. Not much. A knuckle. The last joints bend back too far, like Sedge's. And the pads have gone a dull grey, fine-ridged, like the inside of a mushroom cap, and when I touch my own thumb to my finger, they catch. Not sticky. Catch.

And the cloud goes from white to grey to silver, and then I can see.

Not like day. Like a drawing of the forest in silver pencil, every drop, every thread of moss. The colours have gone. But it goes on as far as I want to look.

I put my hand flat on the nearest trunk and it holds.

And I'm so hungry I could eat the hill.

Climb, says the voice, very small. I'm busy.

* * *

I climb.

My fingers go on the wet bark and catch, and peel off when I lift them, tk, tk, like a cat's paw off a rug. I go up faster than I've ever gone up anything, faster than the bell tower with Jory watching, with Biscuit clamped to my chest under my shirt like a hot water bottle with opinions.

And the forest opens up round me, silver, inside the cloud.

The crowns.

Up here the trunks open out and go everywhere, sideways, branches as wide as the lane, with whole gardens on them, ferns and flowers and little trees growing out of other trees, things I've no names for. Water runs down every branch in silver threads. There are pools in the forks. A frog the size of my thumbnail sits in one and looks at me and goes pip. The sound up here's all drips and wind in moss, and over it, very soft, all round, the grazers, piping to each other in the white, soft as owls.

And the nests.

Baskets, woven of moss and pale seed-down and grazer hair, big enough to sleep a family. They're not on the branches. They float, each one tied to a branch by a few twisted ropes of moss, lifting a little, like a boat at a mooring. I can smell the bread off them. I can smell milk, faint, and hay, and something warm and sleepy, like Tilly's hair when she's been in bed all day with a cold.

Every one is empty.

They float under the net.

Grey company mesh, strung through the crowns from tower to tower, a great sagging ceiling just above the nests. They're pushed down under it, squashed and tilted, nudging up against it like fish under ice.

Biscuit puts its head out and looks at the net with great interest. Biscuit likes anything that holds.

"Later," I tell it. "I promise."

Through the white, off to the right, the hum of the fans gets louder.

On the way past I see the fence. Down in a fork of the ridge, a pond, black and still, with reeds round it, and round the reeds old rusted wire. The reeds lean away from me as I go over. Nobody's home in it.

The one that left, says the voice, so faint I nearly miss it. She's at Highwater. In Lenna.

I don't stop.

* * *

The tower comes out of the cloud like a wall coming out of fog. Which is what it is.

White. Smooth. Cold and faintly wet, humming through my palms. No window, no door, no handhold.

Gerald walked up the inside of a jar.

I put my hands on it. They catch.

I climb it.

The fans get louder. The cloud thins. And then I put my head up out of the top of it, and I'm above the cloud.

A white sea in the moonlight, flat and silver to the end of everything, with the other towers sticking out of it and the tallest crowns breaking through like black islands. And stars like dust, all the way down to the white. The cold up here is clean and sharp and huge, and the stove between my shoulders burns on and I don't feel it.

Round the top runs a narrow gallery with a rail, and fans in round housings breathing out warm air that smells of cedar. And inside the rail, the glass room, lit gold, sitting on the tower like a lantern on a post.

I come up under the edge of the gallery and hang there by my fingers, with nothing under me but cloud, and look in.

Sedge is sitting on the floor with her back against the glass. Right there, a hand's width from me through it. Her knees up. Her borrowed shirt. Her wrist in her lap, glowing faintly green. Her face is wet and furious.

Odile's standing. Her back's to me. She's talking to somebody I can't see, very calm, with one hand flat on a long table, and on the table there's a white cloth and the remains of a dinner and, I notice, a pudding. Untouched.

And by the door there are three of them. Tall, thin, very neat, silver hair combed flat, company grey. Each with a little enamel pin.

I tap on the glass. Just with one fingernail. Tk.

Sedge turns her head.

She sees a face come up at the bottom of the glass, above a drop into the cloud, with eyes that shine silver in the lamplight like a knee-deer's at the pond. And she doesn't scream.

She grins.

It's the best thing anyone's ever done.

Then, on the gallery right above my head, the bootsteps stop.

"Good evening," says a pleasant baritone. "Nothing personal."

* * *

It doesn't hurt much. A slap on the side of my neck, like a horsefly.

He's standing on the gallery with his feet neatly together and a grey pistol with a brass cap in his hand, looking down at me with great interest. The pin on his lapel says 26. He smells of cedar and toner and, very faintly, of satisfaction, like a pan that's just been wiped.

"Settling agent," says Pell-26, and makes a note. "Draw-room strength. It's formulated for reference-line physiology, so you'll find the staff are quite unaffected. You should really be seated."

And then the smell reaches me, coming up off the place on my neck, and I know it.

In the white room under the Upper House, the spring I was called. The chair. A woman in green with cold hands, very kind, saying just a little something to settle you, sweetheart, and the sweetness. Sweet like violets. Sweet like a struck coin. And then everything slow and soft and far away, and the three vials, and nobody looking at me once.

Subject should be seated.

Everything goes slow.

* * *

My hands hold on.

Everything else goes. My arms go soft. My legs swing out over the cloud. The stars swim. But my hands stay on the edge of the gallery, flat, grey, caught, because they don't need me to hold. Gerald doesn't think about it. Gerald just hangs.

I named them that way.

I can hear my heart. Slow. Too slow. Thum. And a long gap. Thum.

And my breath. Going out. And not coming back in for a long time. And going out.

Bram, says the voice, and it's a long way off, it's in your blood. It's going everywhere. It's fitting into things.

"What things?"

I'll show you. There isn't time. There isn't time to show you. Come in.

* * *

The blood is a river at night.

Like the mill race in spring, too fast, too full: red loaves, thousands of them, soft and round with the middles pressed in, tumbling past each other down the dark like bread down the bakehouse chute on a feast morning. They're carrying the breath. I can see it in them, a brightness, and I can see it going dim.

And among them, the poison.

Little things. So small. Sharp, bent, all the same shape, like a key that only opens one kind of lock. Thousands of them, riding the river with the loaves.

The river goes past the houses. That's what the nerves are, here: long thin houses along the banks, lights going on-off, on-off down the street, passing messages. Every house has doors, and some of the doors are brakes. When they open, the house goes quiet for a moment. A house that's always talking is no good either.

The keys go to those doors.

They fit. They turn. And they hold the doors open. Not shut: open. And the houses go quiet, and stay quiet. One window goes dark. Then the next. Then a whole street.

That's the settling, says the voice. That's how they make you sit still in the chair. Just a little, and the houses go quiet and you go soft. They gave you the dose for a boy in a chair. Not a boy on a wall.

Down at the end of the river, there's a street I know without being told. It's the one that says breathe. Every few moments, its lights go on all at once, and a long way off, something enormous, my chest, pulls air.

Its lights are going on less.

Thum, says my heart, from somewhere above the river. A long gap. Thum.

"Take them out."

I can't take them out. I can only write. Things are falling out of it, it's going so fast. I can't change the doors, they're the old doors, the clean ones, and it'd take days. I can't make a new thing out of nothing. It has to be something that's already there.

The breathe-street flickers.

Breathe properly, says Ma.

But I can't. That's the whole—

And I remember the reef. The green dark under the Knuckle. Three hundred counts with no breath, and my heart slow as a church bell, and my body pulling all its warm into the middle and waiting. Not drowning. Waiting. My lungs know how to go without. I had that written into me at Highwater, and I named it properly, with Ommery's upside-down tree and Gran's paper chest.

"The sea," I say. "I can go without. I've got three hundred counts. More. Use them."

The voice stops.

Yes, it says. Yes. That's time. That's not much time.

My heart goes slower. Everything pulls in to the middle, the way it did under the reef. The breathe-street flickers, and holds.

One, I count. Two.

"Where's the dirty water go?"

What?

"Ommery. She said everything bad that gets into you goes to one place to be washed. The washhouse. Under the ribs, on the right." I can hear her, and Ma, and Gran, all in one voice. "The washhouse. Go to the washhouse."

Fourteen. Fifteen.

And the river turns, and we go with it.

It's a great dark wet place full of work, like the washhouse at home on a Monday, only instead of women up to their elbows it's the little busy machines, folded things of every shape, and each one, when something comes past that it fits, takes hold of it and cuts. Forty. Forty-one.

Seventy. Seventy-one.

The breathe-street, a long way off, goes dark. And stays dark. And then, slow, lights again.

Nobody in Firstfield has a name for a cutter in the washhouse under your ribs. But I've watched someone cut, and wipe, and cut again.

Saul, in the dark at the end of the east rows, with the candle wax running onto my fingers. One brown leaf. Cut. And the knife into the vinegar, and wiped, clean, ready. And the next leaf. Cut. Wiped. Never blunt. Never used up. Two weeks of evenings. Every cut the same.

"Saul's knife," I say. "Cut one, wipe, cut the next. It doesn't get used up."

And it's Gran's laundry. The cutter is a long chain of the little shapes, folded up on itself the way Gran folds a sheet off the line, corner to corner, in half, in half again, so it ends up a square that fits in the press. I can see it. Here. And here. And here. Three letters. Apple for Crock. Gran for Tilly. Crock for Apple.

Yes, says the voice. I see it. I've got it. I'm writing.

Hundred and twelve. Hundred and thirteen.

And then it's the bakehouse. The little ovens in every cell roar up at once, the way the bakehouse oven roars at five in the morning, and the new chain is made, and folds, corner to corner, in half, in half again, into a square I've never seen.

It takes hold of a key.

Snip.

In half. Two bits of nothing, harmless, washing away down the river.

And lets go. And it's ready for the next.

"More," I say.

And the washhouse makes them, Saul's knives by the thousand, cut and wipe and cut, the ovens burning everything I've eaten for a week, and the river goes in full of keys and comes out full of pieces.

Two hundred. Two hundred and one.

But it's costing something else. The voice has to hold every new fold straight while it's written, and it's already holding Gerald's hands, and the baby's stove, and the knee-deer's eyes, and the sea in my lungs, all on bad names. It hasn't got enough hands.

Two hundred and sixty.

The breathe-street goes dark.

"Drop it," I say.

* * *

My chest pulls.

Air. Huge, cold, with stars in it. I'm hanging off the gallery with my head back, hauling breaths like the mill bellows, my heart going like Biscuit's in the storm, and every window in every house in me lit.

And I'm so hungry I could eat Pell-26.

He's crouched on the gallery above me, writing. He's got a hook out on a pole, the kind you'd use to bring in a boat, and he's reaching it down for my collar.

"Ah," he says, pleasantly. "You're still conscious. That's very unusual. I'll make a note."

"Do," I say.

Except it's not my voice.

It comes out low. Too low. Rough and breathy, with a hiss on the edges of it like wind in a gill-slit, like someone talking from inside a different room. I hear it come out of my mouth and I don't know it.

Pell-26 stops writing.

"That is not the subject's voice," he says, to his ledger.

I don't let myself think about it. I swing my legs up, catch the rail with my soles, and come over the edge in one go the way Sedge comes up into a tree house, and I'm crouched on the walkway in front of him, steaming. Biscuit comes out of my shirt like a cork.

He looks at me.

He looks at me for a long time, and whatever he sees, he doesn't write it down.

Then he lifts the dart pistol again.

* * *

Biscuit bites him.

Not on the hand. On the ankle, through the company sock, hard, the way it bit the binder in the Undercroft, and Pell-26 says something that isn't polite at all and the dart goes into the fan housing with a tink. And I'm past him, along the gallery, round the curve of the glass, to where the net is tied off.

An iron cleat bolted to the rail, and the net's main rope round it in a knot as big as my head, wound and wound and set hard with wet. I can feel the drag through the rail: the long faint creak, all those empty nests pushing up from underneath.

"Biscuit."

Biscuit likes anything that holds.

It goes up the rope like a thrown stocking, throat lit amber, and chews through it like a pear. One strand goes, thung, like a harp string. Two. Three.

The fourth goes on its own.

And the rope jumps off the cleat and whips away over the rail into the cloud, and below us, a long way down in the white, there's a sound like a great sheet being pulled off a bed. A huge soft rushing tear, going away and away through the crowns, from tree to tree, from tower to tower, as the whole grey ceiling sags and slides and falls off the forest into the dark.

And then, under the cloud, everywhere at once, the flock pipes.

* * *

They come up through the white like bubbles in a pan.

First one, rising past the gallery so close I could touch it, the cloud coming off its back in threads. Then twenty. Then all of them, every one I ever chewed seed for and every one they've had since, rising into the stars, piping, and the babies blundering up beside their mothers. And the bread.

The bread.

It comes up off them all at once, warm, into the freezing clean air, and the whole top of the High Gardens smells like the bakehouse at six on a winter morning with the frost on the glass. I stand on the gallery with my hands on the rail and laugh. My voice is wrong. It's still laughing.

Pell-26 comes round the curve of the glass with the pistol up, limping. And a grazer rises up through the gap between the gallery and the glass, slow, enormous, all goat-weight and float and surprise, and takes him with it. Just lifts him. His feet go off the boards. He's sitting on a grazer's back with his ledger in both hands and his pin catching the starlight, going up into the night, saying, "This is— I must— This is irregular—"

The grazer reaches the top of its rise, considers, and drops him on the roof of the glass room from about the height of a hay cart.

Item two thousand and six, I think, and I'm not even sorry.

* * *

Inside the glass, Odile moves.

I've seen her move before, at Highwater. You don't see the middle of it. There's a Pell by the door, standing, and then there's a Pell by the door, lying down, and Odile's got his wand in her hand, and she's done something to the door, and it's open, and the cold's rushing in, and she says one word over her shoulder, and Sedge is up off the floor and out on the gallery beside me before I've finished turning round.

She stops.

She's looking at my face in the light from the glass room. And what comes off her isn't a struck match. Colder. A wire pulled so tight it's singing.

"Later," I say, in the voice that isn't mine. "Two each. The bread ones. Hold the strong ones."

Odile comes out of the door behind her. She looks at me once, the way she looks at a drawer that doesn't match its label. Then at the flock, hanging in the stars all round the tower, piping.

"I am not—" she says.

"Two each."

The nicked-wing grazer comes down to the rail with its baby tucked under its side. Behind it come the strongest, the high riders, the ones that smell most like home.

Odile takes a breath. Then she takes hold of two grazers by the loose skin at the base of their necks, very firmly, as if they're a form she's about to file, and steps off the gallery into nothing.

Sedge goes after her with a yell like a gull.

Biscuit pours down my collar. I take the nicked-wing one and its neighbour, and look back once.

Up on the roof of the glass room, Pell-26 is sitting up, still writing. And past him, on a terrace at the very top of the tower, in the gold light from a door, a man in a long coat is standing. He's not shouting. He's just watching us go. And his coat, while I look, goes slowly from gold to the silver-white of the cloud, as if it's trying to match where we've gone.

He doesn't raise his hand.

I step off.

* * *

We go down into the cloud with the whole flock round us.

The stars go out, and my eyes turn the white to silver. The grazers sink slow, heavy with us, floats tight, piping to each other the whole way down like a hymn in the next room. The crowns go past, and the empty nests rising free on their moss ropes like boats when the tide comes in.

A waterfall comes out of the white beside us, falling and falling, and we sink beside it and it turns to mist on my face before it ever gets anywhere.

Left, I hear Sedge shout. Bram, it's left—

"It's right," I say. "Sound lies up here. Smell."

And I smell for it. Down there, under everything, through the falling cold air: wet stone, lichen, a mite or two, and a leaf. Green and new and unlikely, the smell of the joint between a seventh and an eighth stone.

We come out of the bottom of the cloud like coming out of a sack, into the ordinary dark, and the scree and the moss are right there under us, and Hask's right there on the moss, with his top stone tipped back, looking up.

The grazers let us down slow. My feet touch. My knees go.

Somewhere above, in the white, a dart pistol goes tik. Tik.

Hask comes down over the three of us like a roof falling in slow, stone by stone, with a sound like the meeting-house door closing. It's dark under him and smells of lichen and old rain. Something bounces off his top stone. Then something else. Then nothing.

"I have checked," says Hask, over our heads, "and only one of you is my bearer." A pause. "I have decided not to mind."

* * *

There's a pool at the bottom of the waterfall.

A lower one, that does land, in a black round pool in the ferns. I don't remember getting there. I remember Sedge's hand on my arm, and being so hungry I ate moss, a fistful, and it tasted of nothing but wet.

The grazers settle round us on the slope, folding their legs. The babies sleep in a heap. The sky's going grey behind the ridge, and the towers' lights are going out one by one.

I kneel at the edge of the pool to drink.

And the water's still, and it's getting light, and my eyes still see in silver, and there's a face.

* * *

When I was small, Gran washed my face at the pump every morning before school. Cold water, and her thumb, and no nonsense. She'd hold me by the chin to keep me still. Her fingers smelled of woodsmoke and old wool.

"That's a Hale chin," she said, one morning, holding it. Turning my face to the light like a jug she was thinking of buying. "That's your da's. That's mine. My mother's before me. Stubborn as a gatepost." She gave it a little shake. "You'll not lose that."

* * *

I look for it.

The face in the water is long, and narrow, and wrong. The cheekbones have come up high and sharp, like Davin's. The skin's gone pale and mottled, grey and green, like the moss on the trunks. The eyes are too big and there's a shine at the back of them, silver, like a knee-deer's at the pond. The mouth's the right size. The nose isn't.

The chin is narrow. Pointed. Somebody else's.

I touch it. In the water, a long grey hand with fingers a knuckle too long comes up and touches a stranger.

I'm sorry, says the voice behind my eyes. Very small. You never named it. You never needed to. It was just there, like a floor. When I let go, it went where the new names pulled it.

I don't say anything. I can't.

Behind me, Sedge says, "Bram?"

I turn round.

She's standing a few steps up the bank with her arms wrapped round herself. Odile's beside her, and Hask behind, and Biscuit on Hask's ledge with its throat dark. The grey light's on all of them. And Sedge is looking at me the way you look at someone at the door at night when you were expecting someone else.

"Bram," she says again. Not quite a question. Then it is one. Very quiet. "Who are you?"

* * *

You say whose you are.

That's how you say it in Firstfield. Son of. Brother of. Of the house of. I said it to Davin. I said it on the bank of a dying pond in the Pale.

I open my mouth.

Bram, I think. Son of—

And the voices come, the way they come, when I need them.

They come wrong.

Hands first, says Ma, in Gran's voice. Look twice, says Jory. Bet you won't, says Saul. Name it and you can hold it, what did you learn today, count to five hundred, not dead, only nearly, breathe properly, all on top of each other, all in the wrong mouths, like a meeting house saying the hymn at different speeds. I reach for Ma and get Ommery. I reach for Gran and there's only the words, no face, no woodsmoke, nobody holding my chin.

They're all there. I can't sort them into people.

I can't sort them into mine.

"I don't know," I say, in a voice that isn't mine.

Sedge's face does something I never want to see again.

I shut my eyes.

* * *

And then, in the middle of it, a voice that doesn't belong.

It's not from the village. It comes up through the ground, through my knees, slow and very serious, as if it's thought about this for three hundred years.

This is not enumerated in the Third Schedule.

The meeting house goes quiet.

And another one. Dry. Tired. Patient, the way you're patient with a drawer that's stuck. I've never once heard her say it out loud. I've only watched her do it, in the Pale with her slate on her knee, at Highwater by the light of her own nails. I hear it now.

Write it down.

They don't push the others out. They come in among them, and the others make room. Ma. Gran. Saul and Jory and Master Aldous and Ommery and Tilly's terrible joke. And a gate-creak laugh off a landing saying Ada Hale's chin, and a man with a gutting knife saying breathe through your nose, and Sedge saying bet you won't to me first.

Not fewer voices. More.

I open my eyes.

Odile's kneeling in the ferns in front of me. I didn't hear her come down the bank. She's holding out her pad of blank paper forms, the ones that don't need charging, and a pencil. She doesn't say anything at all.

I take the pencil. My new fingers catch on it, and peel off, tk, and catch.

I write.

It's my writing. The hand's wrong, but the letters come out the way Master Aldous taught us with his watch ticking, the B with the bottom loop too big, the way he always told me off for.

Bram.

And under it, whose.

Wenna's. Ada's. Tilly's.

Saul's. Jory's. Master Aldous's. Dr. Ommery's.

Lenna's. Teasel's, he gave me a pear.

Hask's.

Odile's.

Sedge's.

I stop. Biscuit's watching me from Hask's ledge.

Biscuit's, I write, probably.

I read it all the way down, twice, the way Saul says, and the second time it's the same. It doesn't fit on the form. The last ones go up the side.

"That's whose," I say. It's still the wrong voice. "That's who."

Odile takes the form back. She reads it once, all the way down and up the side. Her face doesn't do anything. Then she folds it in half, and in half again, corner to corner, like a sheet off the line, and puts it in the inside pocket of her coat, the one over her heart, where she keeps the things that are evidence.

"Filed," she says.

* * *

The nicked-wing grazer comes down the slope.

It drifts down to the pool with its baby against its side, and hangs in front of me, and looks into my new face with its big surprised dark eyes for a long time.

I wait for it to pipe and go. Everything's been going.

It bumps its soft nose on my forehead.

And then it burps.

Right in my face. Enormous. Like the mill sluice opening in spring, warm, fresh bread, and the baby, not to be outdone, does a little one too, hup, and it's so exactly home and so exactly ridiculous that it gets out of me before I can stop it.

I laugh.

It comes up out of the bottom of me like it did on the lip of the ditch in the Pale, helpless, wheezing, with a hiccup in the middle and a stupid high bit at the end that Jory always said sounded like a donkey being surprised.

The voice is wrong. The laugh isn't.

Sedge makes a noise. Then she's down the bank and on her knees in front of me in the ferns with both her hands on my wrong face, holding it the way Gran held my chin at the pump, turning it to the light.

"There you are," she says. Her face is wet again. She doesn't seem to mind. "That's you. That stupid donkey bit. That's you."

Biscuit comes down off Hask's ledge and sniffs me, all over, slowly, my hands and my neck and my new chin. Its throat stays dark a long time.

Then it goes amber, and Biscuit climbs into my shirt and goes to sleep.

Hask makes the grinding sound in the ground. It goes on for quite a while.

"I have heard that laugh through the ground," he says, "since the orchards. Stone hears stone. It is the same."

* * *

The sun comes up behind the towers and lights the top of the cloud gold. Up in the crowns, the nests are rising on their moss ropes, and the grazers going up to them in twos and threes, piping.

I'm sitting on a rock by the pool with Odile's coat round me and a mouthful of chewed seed, which tastes, through the noise, of bread.

I can try to give it back, says the voice. Your face. Some of it. Later. If you name it.

"I don't know what it's called."

No, it says. Nobody ever does.

Sedge sits down on the rock next to me, so her shoulder's against mine. She looks at my face sideways for a while.

"It's horrible," she says.

"Thanks."

"I'll get used to it."

I look at her.

"Bet you won't," I say.

She knocks her shoulder into mine, hard enough to nearly put me in the pool. "Bet I will."

Out on the slope, the nicked-wing grazer burps again, softly, in its sleep.

I laugh. It's mine.
