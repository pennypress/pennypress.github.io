What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 11 of 24

Chapter Ten: Population: None

RECOVERY TENDER RT-6 · CAPACITY: 2 (TWO) ASSOCIATES OR 400 KG RECOVERED MATERIALS · OPERATE IN CHARTED WATERS ONLY · ALL WATERS ARE CHARTED

— instruction plate riveted inside the bow of a Varro-Tandem Bioreference recovery tender; someone has scratched a small question mark after the last line

The boy's footprints went out onto the mud and stopped, and for about a minute I believed the sea had him.

I'd found the first sign of him at noon on the nineteenth, on the last hill above the coast, after a night and a morning walking north-west from the Hiveplains. The grass up there was grey and tough as wire, combed inland by the wind, all but one oval at the brow, flattened as if it had been sat on and had not yet decided to get up.

I knelt and put my face to it, because that is the kind of Examiner I have become, and smelled salt, and crushed stems, and under them, so faint I might have been remembering it, warm bread.

"Hask. He sat here."

Hask came up the slope behind me, three leaves in his joint and a skin of Hiveplains gold dust still in the carving, and stopped dead at the brow.

Below us, the world ended.

I had seen the sea once before, in a lightning flash from the top of a hive. That had been a picture. This was the thing itself. It began at the foot of the hill and went out flat and grey and moving everywhere at once, all the way to a straight line where the sky came down to sit on it. It breathed. A long hush up the sand and a long draw back, so slow and so big that after a while you stopped hearing it and started feeling it in the soles of your feet, the way you feel a ship's engine through a bunk.

Between the hill and the sea lay miles of shining mud, slate-grey, cut by wandering silver channels; and out on the mud stood the forest I'd seen from the tower. Trees on legs. Every trunk held up a man's height clear of the ground in a cage of arching roots, dozens to a tree, so that the whole wood seemed to be wading out to sea on tiptoe, holding its skirts up. Its green roof ran along the coast north and south until the haze ate it.

And out of that roof, a long way off, a single thread of grey smoke still went straight up into the clean air.

Hask said nothing for so long that a gull landed on his top stone, looked at me, barked once like a small offended dog, and left.

"Hask?"

"I have crossed between stars eleven times," said Hask, "in the holds of ships. I had not seen this." A long grinding pause. "Is it enumerated?"

"The sea?"

"Drowning is enumerated. For the bearer." Another pause. "Not for the Proxy. I have looked. I cannot drown." "It is very large, Examiner."

"Yes."

"I find I wish to stand in it."

◆ ◆ ◆

The prints went down the hill, and I went with them.

On the sand at the bottom they were easy: broad, ridged, toe-knuckled, the plated feet I had first seen at the 40-K stream ten days ago, a boot tread with no boot. And beside them, weaving in and out of them like a dog that cannot walk in a straight line to save its life, a second, smaller track: two little forefeet, then four behind, webbed between the toes, with a drag-mark of tail. The same print I'd found beside a replanted seed under a skylight in the Undercroft.

"His animal's still with him," I said.

"I am glad," said Hask, which surprised us both.

The prints went out onto the mud. The mud took them and kept them, deep and clear, every ridge of plating printed in it like a seal in wax. They went a hundred paces towards the trees, detoured to a pool where something with one enormous claw was still waving it at nobody, and went on.

And then they went into a channel, and did not come out the other side.

I stood at the edge of it. It was as wide as a village lane, and it was full, and it was running: inland, past my boots, quick and silver and full of turning weed, the wrong way for any river I'd ever met. While I watched, the water came up over the toes of my boots. I looked back. There was water between me and the hill, spreading over the mud like milk over a tabletop, coming from everywhere at once, and the last of my own footprints were filling.

"Examiner," said Hask from the sand, in the voice he keeps for things that are about to be enumerated.

I went back fast, with the sea round my shins. By the time I reached him the mud was a lake, and the forest was a forest standing in a lake, and every print I'd followed was under a metre of moving water.

I stood on the sand and dripped and thought: he didn't know. He came down this hill at dawn yesterday and walked out to look at a crab, and the sea came in behind him from everywhere at once, and he had never in his life been in water over his head.

I made myself look again.

Twice, the Survey teaches you. Once for what you're afraid of, and once for what's there. And what was there, on the sand at my feet where I'd come back up, was the smaller track, the six-footed one, coming out of the water. Wet and new, from this morning's low tide, wandering up the beach to a dead fish, and turning round, and going back towards the trees as purposeful as a postman.

An animal doesn't come back to a place where its boy drowned and go foraging. It goes home.

And the smoke, out there on the drowned forest, went straight up.

◆ ◆ ◆

That evening I did the thing I'd come to do, which was watch.

I sat on the sand with my back against Hask and my slate on my knee and wrote down the sea. By dusk the tide was going out again, hissing away between the trees in a hundred channels. I counted from the turn: Survey habit. Count, write it down, count again. Twice a day, a little over six hours each way. Tomorrow's high water would come just after dawn.

At the edge of a pool left by the ebb I watched a small fish flip, silver, on the mud. Something came out of the water at it so fast I didn't see it arrive, only the shape after: long, dark, a mouth like a pair of shears. The fish was gone. Beside the same pool, a crab walked slowly along the bottom, right under the long dark shape, and nothing touched it.

Slow was not cross. Fast was. I had learned that from a meadow of bees two days ago, and it seemed the sea had the same opinion. I wrote it down anyway. A rule you've only met once is a rumour.

On every root that the ebb uncovered, at the same height, ran a band of tiny blue-black shells, packed as close as cobbles, and above the shells a line of dried weed, and above the weed, bare bark. A bathtub ring round the whole forest. I thought about a crayfish shell in the fork of a tree on a brown river world, and Teague's tired kind voice, and then I made myself read it instead. The shells were where the water lived. The weed was where it went at the top of an ordinary tide. Where the roots were shell to the knee, the channel ran deep. Where a root was bare to its foot, the mud under that channel sat high, and the ebb would leave it first.

I sketched the nearest channels, marked the bare roots, and felt better than I had all day.

Out over the forest, against the last green of the sky, something small and grey with a blue nose-light moved slowly along the canopy, stopping and starting, like a heron fishing.

"Pell-4," I said.

"He is looking for warmth," said Hask.

"He's looking for a number."

◆ ◆ ◆

Night came down warm, smelling of salt and rot-sweet fruit and, very far off, woodsmoke.

I took my boots off and walked into the sea.

I'm not proud of the reason: I was a sandwich of gold dust and sweat and dried mud. But when I stepped into the shallows the water lit up round my ankle.

Blue-green, cold, the colour of the Undercroft and of my own fingernails. Where my foot moved it left a smear of light. I bent and swept my hand through the water and a comet came off my fingers, sparks streaming off every knuckle, falling away behind like embers off a blown coal. Each spark was a living thing too small to see, flashing when disturbed. Every wave broke in green fire along its whole length.

I stood in the sea to my knees, an Examiner of the Inspectorate with my trousers rolled, and wrote my name in light on the water with one finger, and watched it go out. Then I did it again.

Behind me there was a long grinding step, and another, and Hask walked into the sea.

The water lit round him, and he walked out into the dark on a pool of green light, the carved law on his lower stones glowing from beneath as if written in fire. The sea came up to his third stone, his fourth. He stopped with the water lapping at the lichen, and stood there, enormous and dark against the glowing waves, and did not say anything at all.

I didn't either. Some things you let a person have.

Later, from the wrack line where the storm had piled branches and weed and torn red-patched sailcloth, I pulled out a boat.

A punt, flat-bottomed, pegged, not a nail in it. It had a pole lashed inside it and a short frayed bow rope, and on its bow somebody had carved, small and with great care, a heron standing on one leg.

At about midnight a company drone came in low over the sea, a grey shape with its running lights off, and set something down on the sand a few hundred metres north. Something long and grey with a short mast. Then it lifted away.

Two thin figures came limping down the beach to meet it, carrying their shoes. Their voices carried over the sand.

"Twelve. That is my side."

"There are no sides, Seven. It is a boat."

"There are always sides."

◆ ◆ ◆

Halloran called at dawn, bacon frying.

"Pastern." Tock. "Your bulletin made the crawl."

I was sitting in the heron punt with my boots on, watching the tide come in. "Which one?"

"You know which one. STEADY to UNDER DISCREPANCY, between a soap opera and a toll rise, and by breakfast the Chancery had sent me a note with a seal on it the size of a dinner plate." Tock. "I've put it in the tin with the shells."

"Am I in trouble, sir?"

"You're always in trouble." A pause, frying. "Varro-Tandem filed a fresh recovery claim at four this morning. Coastal margin, north-west. Materials, various. Not specimen. Various."

"The Register says the coast's uninhabited," I said.

"It does." Tock. "Which means anything they find there is nobody's, and anything that's nobody's is whoever picks it up." He let that sit. "You've got three days, Pastern. Don't spend them all in one place."

"I wasn't planning to spend them anywhere, sir. I was planning to find him."

"Then find him," said Halloran, "before somebody numbers him," and the line closed like a log settling in a grate.

North along the beach, the grey tender had its motor running: a high, quick, eager whine, like a wasp in a jar.

◆ ◆ ◆

Hask towed me into the forest along the bottom of the sea.

A punt is a boat for one person and a pole. Hask in it would have gone to the bottom like an anchor with opinions. But Hask cannot drown. He has looked.

So I put the bow rope in his top stone's grip, and he walked into the channel, and kept walking, down the slope of the channel bed, until the water came up past his fourth stone, his fifth, his sixth.

He stopped there. "The leaves," he said.

"Salt?"

"Salt is not enumerated as a harm to leaves. I have checked." A pause. "I do not find this reassuring."

So he took the channels by their margins, where the mud shelved up, and kept his seventh stone above the water. And the bow rope went down into the sea at a slant, and the punt went forward with a little chuckle of water at its bow, steady and slow as a man walking to church, and ahead of me there was nothing in the channel but the upper half of a cairn, wading, with three pale leaves riding a hand above the water like a buttonhole.

Through the clear water I could see the rest of him. The dark stones walking on the bottom, the carved law on them wavering in the green light, tiny silver fish nosing at the lichen on his fourth stone. A crab had got on at the beach and was riding on his shoulder ledge, claws up, like a figurehead.

We passed in under the roof.

It was like going into a church from a hot street. The light went green and soft and dim, coming down through leaves in long slanting bars that lay on the water and wavered. The air was warm and wet and smelled of salt and fruit: windfalls bobbing in the slack water, purple-black plums with a white bloom on them, long knobbled figs hanging in ropes from the branches, and higher up, in the deepest shade, pale things the size of a fist that glowed. Faintly. A lamp turned right down. Roots creaked. Water sucked and clicked round ten thousand arching legs, and everywhere, small and high and quick, came whistles: one from the left, and an answer far off on the right, and another overhead, passing away into the leaves like a rumour going round a market.

"They are talking about us," said Hask, ahead of me.

"How do you know?"

"Everything is," said Hask.

A fish broke the surface ahead, quick and silver, and the shears came up out of the dark and took it, and the long shape slid back under, right past the punt. It turned its blunt head towards the bow, and the bow went on past at a church-going walk, and the eel lost interest and went down among the roots.

I sat very still with my hands in my lap and my heart going like a drum. Twice is a rule.

Behind us, the wasp-in-a-jar whine came into the forest, and the whistles all round us stopped.

◆ ◆ ◆

The net came out of the sky.

I didn't see Pell-4 until he'd fired. The little one-seat skiff was hanging in a hole in the canopy where a storm-killed tree had come down, blue nose-light on, and Pell-4 was leaning out of it with the net-launcher's bell mouth pointed at me. There was a soft cough, and the air above the punt filled with something spun and spreading and fine as cobweb.

I had time to get one arm up. The net came down over me and the punt together, light as a breath and tough as wire, and pulled tight; and up above, a small motor whirred, and the line from the launcher to the net went taut and began to reel in.

The punt's bow came up out of the water.

"Good morning, Examiner," called Pell-4. The bee stings had gone down; his face was nearly a face again. "I'm so sorry. Nothing personal." The launcher on its strap chimed. He glanced at its screen. "Oh dear."

I could see it from where I was, tipped on my back in a tilting boat with cobweb over my face. RECOVERED MATERIALS: 1 (one) EXAMINER, grade unknown. 1 (one) BOAT, ownership not established. Entered to company stock 6:41 am.

"I'll get you out at once," Pell-4 said, and pressed something, and the launcher made a sorry little two-note sound and said in a pleasant recorded voice, Release of logged stock requires disposal authorisation. Please contact your line supervisor.

He pressed it again.

Release of logged stock requires—

"Ah," said Pell-4.

The punt came up another hand's breadth. I was going to be reeled up out of the forest like a fish in a bag and filed somewhere in a company warehouse under Boats.

Then Hask let go of the bow rope and turned, and came back down the channel in three long strides with the sea breaking white off his chest, and rose.

He came up all of a piece, the way he rises, the water pouring off every stone in sheets and streaming out of the carving, weed in his joints, the crab still on his shoulder with its claws up. He rose until he towered over the punt, three wet leaves trembling in his middle, and his top stone came down onto the net and held it, and the punt, flat to the water.

The line sang tight. The skiff above dipped its nose.

"Item eighty-eight," said Hask. "Unwelcome hands."

"It's a net," called Pell-4, sounding genuinely interested.

"A net," said Hask, "is a great many hands."

The skiff's motor climbed. The line hummed between the sky and the stone like a bowstring. Pell-4 was trying to lift an Orrun Proxy out of the sea with a craft built for one thin man and his ledger case.

I lay under the cobweb looking up the line, and thought about Rook on a limestone world saying walk up the inside of the world.

I had my knife out of my boot before I'd finished thinking it.

The net gave to the blade one strand at a time with a sound like plucked wire. I cut a slit, wriggled out, and stood up in the tipping punt with Hask's top stone beside me and the taut line going up from the net into the sky.

Ten metres. Maybe twelve. A single grey filament no thicker than a bootlace, humming.

I put the coil of my honeyed rope over my shoulder. I wrapped the launcher line round my right forearm, twice, and put my boots against the trunk of the nearest tree, and walked up it.

Hand over hand. The filament cut into my palms through the dried honey. Below me the green water spread out like a floor; above me the skiff came closer, dipping and straining, and Pell-4's face over the side looking down at me climbing his own line towards him with an expression of the purest courtesy and dismay.

"Examiner," he said, when my head came level with his beetle legs. "You're on my line."

"Yes."

"I really must ask you to—"

I got a boot onto the skiff's left leg and swung, and the whole craft rocked like a cradle, and Pell-4 grabbed the edge of his canopy with both hands. I didn't go for him. I don't hit men who say nothing personal; it only encourages them. I went for the bough of a rope-fig a metre from the skiff's tail, thick as my waist.

I took the end of the launcher line, where it ran into the skiff's belly through a little brass eye, and I took a turn of my own rope round it, and round the bough, and round the line again, and pulled it down tight into the hitch Rook had made me tie fifty times on a cold morning until I could do it in the dark. I cut the line below the hitch. Then I let go and dropped.

Two metres into a crook of branches, which hurt, and three more down a curtain of hanging fig-ropes, which hurt differently, and a short fall into the channel beside Hask, which didn't hurt at all.

I came up spitting salt.

Above us, the skiff hung in its hole in the sky, nose down, motor whining, tied to a fig tree by its own net line like a grey kite on a string. Pell-4 leaned out, looked at the hitch, looked at the launcher's screen, and pressed the release once more, without much hope.

Release of logged stock requires disposal authorisation.

"I'll contact my line supervisor," said Pell-4, with dignity, and took out his ledger.

From the trees all round us, somewhere up in the leaves, a whistle went up. Short, short, long. And then another, from further off. And then, I could have sworn, something that was not a whistle at all, but a child, laughing.

◆ ◆ ◆

The tender came round the bend at a speed it had no business doing in a forest.

It was long and grey and low, the colour of a filing cabinet, with a high quick motor at the stern like a wasp in a jar, and two thin men in grey sitting in it, one at the tiller and one in the bow, both very straight, very far apart.

"Examiner Pastern!" called Pell-12. His face was still faintly pudding from the bees. "Good morning! We have a claim."

"I heard."

"Coastal margin. Materials, various." "The coast is listed uninhabited, Examiner. Anything we recover here is, by definition, nobody's." He smiled, pleasantly. "It really is nothing personal."

I thought of the whistles, and the child laughing.

"Hask," I said quietly, "how fast can you walk on the bottom?"

"As fast as I walk on the top," said Hask. "Which is to say, Examiner, with deliberation."

"Good."

I climbed back into the punt, still trailing its cut net like a bridal veil, and Hask took the bow rope, and we went.

Not fast. Never fast. Away from them along the channel at a church-going walk, Hask wading ahead, the punt chuckling. Behind us the wasp-whine rose, and the tender came after us in a wake that slapped the roots and woke the whole channel up.

"Examiner," called Pell-7 from the tiller, in a voice like a door closed very gently on a very cold room. He was wearing company shoes. They were plainly two sizes too large. "I don't wish to be rude, but you are going extremely slowly."

"Yes," I said.

The net-launcher in Pell-12's hands coughed. I ducked, and the cobweb went over my head and wrapped itself, with great efficiency, round the low bough of a fig tree hanging over the channel. The launcher chimed. Pell-12 glanced at it, and his face did something complicated.

"What does it say, Twelve?" said Seven.

"It says we have recovered a tree."

"What kind?"

Twelve read it. "Fig, rope. Ownership not established."

"I shall be filing about that."

"Of course you will."

Ahead of me the channel split round a big tree with roots like a cathedral. To the left it ran on wide and obvious, the channel you'd take in a hurry; to the right, narrow and dark. The roots on the left were bare to the foot, every one, weed and grey bark going down into the water. On the right they were crusted with blue-black shells to the knee, all the way along.

The water round the punt had stopped running upstream. The weed on the roots, a minute ago streaming inland, hung slack now, turning, thinking about it. High water. Just after dawn, as I'd counted.

"Hask. Right, please. Into the dark one."

"Is it enumerated?"

"No. It's arithmetic."

We went right. Slowly.

And the tender, which had been chasing an Examiner in a punt for a quarter of an hour and feeling foolish about it, saw the wide clear water on the left, and a chance to cut round ahead of me, and opened its throttle with a great eager howl.

It was the right decision for a boat in a hurry. The weed on the roots was already turning, beginning to stream the other way, seaward. The ebb had begun. And under that wide clear water, where not a single root carried a single shell, the mud sat high.

I heard it before I saw it. The motor's note went up, and up, a scream, as the little propeller came near the surface and bit into the churned water and spat foam, quick as a fish flipping on mud. And something long and dark came up out of the roots at it the way the shears had come up at the fish.

And another. And three more.

There was a thump and a sound like a wet sack fed into a mangle, and the motor's scream went to a choke, and then to nothing. The tender sat in the middle of the wide channel, stern down, festooned with long dark furious shapes that had their teeth in the propeller and were not letting go. Pell-12 had his compliance wand out, its brass cap fizzing blue, and was leaning out over the stern towards the water.

"Twelve," said Seven, quite calmly. "Salt water."

"I know what water is, Seven."

"Then you know where it conducts to."

Twelve looked at the wand, and the water, and the bottom of the tender, and his own feet in their good shoes, and put the wand away.

The ebb took the water out from under them. You could watch it go. It drew away down the wide channel, quick and shallow, and the tender settled, and tilted, and sat down on a shining bank of mud with a long contented squelch, like an old man lowering himself into a chair. The eels stayed where they were, among the roots round the stranded boat, in the pools, waiting for something to move quickly.

The Associates sat in their tender on the mud and did not move quickly. They are not fools. They are only Associates.

"Examiner!" called Pell-7.

I had Hask stop. We were thirty metres off, in the deep crusted channel, still afloat.

"I should like it noted," said Pell-7, "that I am filing a grievance against the sea."

"Noted."

"And against Twelve, for the route."

"Against Seven," said Twelve, "for the shoes."

From somewhere above, faint and tinny, a third voice drifted down through the canopy from a grey skiff tied to a fig tree. "I should like it noted that I was here first."

I tore a Form 9 off the pad in my coat, wrote on it Assistance available on request. Tide back in about six hours. Move slowly. The eels bite what's quick, folded it into a dart, and threw it. It landed in the mud a metre short of the tender. Neither of them reached for it.

They're learning.

◆ ◆ ◆

Highwater did not so much appear as let me see it.

One moment I was gliding under the roof with the ebb running and Hask wading ahead; the next I looked up and the roof was full of people. On platforms lashed across the branches, on sagging rope bridges, in doorways of woven houses roofed with sailcloth patched red: children, mostly, but not only children. Nobody spoke. They watched me the way the guard bees had watched me on the hive doorstep, and every few heartbeats somebody whistled, softly, and somebody else, further off, whistled back.

Their hands were wrong for the Register. A woman gutting fish had fingers as long as my hand. A boy on a ladder had webs between his. An old man in a doorway had forearms scaled fine as a fish's. Pickers. Divers. Climbers. Lines I'd seen in catalogues, under discontinued.

The Register said there was nobody here at all. I half-closed my eyes and did what Fallow taught me, and for one breath walked it: the long hall of the coast in my head, drawer after drawer, every one labelled none. And standing in the aisle between them, in the dark, more people than I could count, with nowhere to put their names. That would want a proper Reading, later, sitting down.

The water had gone out far enough to bare the mud round the foot of the biggest tree in the village, a grey trunk as smooth as a wall with notches cut up its side from the mud to the lowest platform: dozens, each with little marks beside it. Most of them were near the top.

Hask walked up out of the channel onto the mud and stood at the foot of the trunk with the sea running out of his joints, and the whole village leaned back a little, all at once, like a field of barley when the wind changes.

A ladder came down from the low platform. A man came down it, not fast. Tall, long-fingered, grey in his beard, a white scar puckered down from one ear. A gutting knife on his belt that he didn't touch.

He looked at me in the punt, and at Hask, and then he leaned towards me, not close, and breathed in through his nose. I saw his face go puzzled, as if he'd opened a cupboard expecting plates and found a bird's nest.

"You smell," he said slowly, "of a goat. And a hive. And tar."

"Tea."

"That's not tea."

"It's very strong tea." I held out the bow rope. "This came ashore on the beach in the storm. I think it's yours. I'm sorry about the net. That's not mine either."

He looked at the heron on the bow. Something in his face went very still. He took the rope, and turned the punt in the shallow water with one hand, and looked at the knot I'd tied in its bow ring last night, by fingernail-light, in the dark.

"Who tied that?"

"I did."

He looked at me with a sort of tired pity. "Who wrote you?"

I know a form when I hear one. Which company. Which line.

"Nobody," I said. "I'm the plain kind."

"There's no plain kind," said the man, and went up the ladder with his boat's rope over his shoulder, leaving the punt for somebody else to haul, and me standing in a foot of ebbing water, being looked at by a whole village.

I climbed the ladder after him, because nobody had said I couldn't.

◆ ◆ ◆

The low platform was wet to the ankle still, the boards dark with the tide,, the houses round it marked a hand high inside their doorsills. It smelled of woodsmoke and fish and, from somewhere, aniseed.

The aniseed came from a cut-down barrel at the platform's edge, full of dark silt, with a feathery herb growing in it.

I'd been looking at the ground since a stream in the Spill, and I knew the shape before I knew I'd seen it.

Two small dimples in the silt, beside the herb. First knuckle deep, and not a hair more.

I crouched by the barrel, and put out my finger, and didn't touch them.

When I stood up, he was there.

He was on a rope bridge at the far side of the platform, coming down it with his arms full of pale glowing fruit, talking over his shoulder to a girl behind him. He stopped dead with one foot on the boards.

I'd been carrying his face in the Register for eleven days: the yearly portrait, taken in a white room under the Upper House the spring he was called, a boy with a village haircut and a clean neck, looking at the lens the way you'd look at a dog you weren't sure of. It was the face. It was not the boy.

He was shorter than me, as I'd known from a bolt-plate in the Undercroft. Thinner than his portrait, brown from three weeks of sky, in a sailcloth shirt far too big for him with the sleeves rolled up in four hard turns and a red patch on one elbow. His feet were bare, and they weren't feet any more, not as the Register would have them: grey-green, ridged like bark, the plating grown up round his heels and over the knuckles of his toes, gripping the bridge's rope the way a hand grips a rail. At his left wrist, under the skin, lay a tracery of fine green-gold lines like the veins of a leaf held up to the sun, and it was faintly lit, as if something under there were awake and paying attention. Under each of his ears, three pink slits, one above another, the edges fine as the edge of a mushroom's gill. And under his right ear, just above the slits, stuck to his skin like an earring nobody's grandmother would allow, a single small shell, blue-black, on threads of gold.

Something on his shoulder lifted its head. Brown-black and sleek, two short legs in front holding on to his collar, four behind, a blunt whiskered face, and a throat that glowed, as I watched, from nothing to a slow, wary amber.

He looked at me. He looked down past me, over the edge, at Hask standing on the mud with the sea running out of his carving.

And all six slits on his neck slammed shut.

I saw it happen. Like little doors closing in a wind, snap, three each side, all at once. I'd never seen a body say I am frightened so plainly, or so far outside anything in the Register.

"Bram," I said. "Bram Hale. My name is Odile Pastern. I'm an Examiner, I'm not with—"

He ran.

Back up the bridge the way he'd come, the moonpears going everywhere, glowing, bouncing off the planks and dropping into the water below like lamps let fall. The girl behind him swung aside without seeming to move to let him past, and stared after him, and then at me.

And halfway across the bridge he stopped.

An old man was there, coming the other way with a basket of plums on his hip, and in getting past him Bram had knocked it, and the basket had tipped, and purple-black plums were rolling all over the planks towards the gaps.

With an Examiner on his trail and a pile of walking stone below him and every reason in the world to keep going, he went down on his knees on a swaying bridge in front of the whole village and picked up the plums. Quickly. One at a time. He put them back in the old man's basket, and set it straight on the old man's hip, and said something to him I couldn't hear.

Then he went up a ladder and into the leaves like a squirrel up a wall, and was gone.

◆ ◆ ◆

I didn't chase him.

I could climb; I'd climbed a net line that morning. But nobody ever turned round to answer a question for the person behind them.

My first year at the Inspectorate, on a wet world whose name I've let myself forget, Fallow and I had a witness run from us. A boy of ten who'd seen who set a mill fire went out of the back of his mother's house like a cat off a hot stove the moment we came in at the front. I was twenty-seven and fast and I went after him, and lost him in three streets, and came back an hour later with my knees torn and my temper worse, and found Fallow sitting on the boy's mother's front step in the drizzle, eating an onion pasty out of a paper bag. The boy was sitting next to him. He had half the pasty.

"How," I said.

Fallow wiped his fingers on his coat. His knees were bad that winter; he hadn't moved off the step in an hour. "Nobody," he said, "runs from a man eating lunch."

I sat down on the edge of the low platform at Highwater, with my boots hanging over the mud beside Hask's top stone, and took out my flask.

The steel was warm through my coat where it had ridden all morning. Four dents in it now: three from long before I met any of this, and one from a heat-hunter in the Spill, with two tooth holes next to it. I unscrewed it, and the steam came up into the green light, tar and burnt raisins, and every head on every platform turned to look.

"Item fourteen," said Hask, below me, very softly.

"I know, Hask."

I took out my slate, and laid it on my knee, and opened the case file, and began to write up the morning. 1 (one) Associate, grade 4, tethered. 2 (two) Associates, grades 7 and 12, aground. 1 (one) tree, fig, rope, recovered, ownership not established. My palms stung where the launcher line had cut them. I drank my tea. I wrote. Nobody runs from a woman eating lunch, and I hadn't got any lunch, so it would have to be paperwork.

The village watched me do it.

After a while, the whistles started up again, low and conversational. After a longer while, the woman gutting fish went back to gutting fish. A baby in a net above me woke, and grizzled, and was handed down a ladder from person to person.

And a small girl came and stood in front of Hask.

She was perhaps five. She had long fingers like the woman with the fish, and a smear of plum on her chin, and she stood on the root at the foot of the big tree and looked up at him, all the way up, at the carving and the crab and the three leaves, and she didn't say a word. She just stared, with her whole face.

Hask looked down at her for a long time. Then his top stone turned, very slightly, the way it had turned once to look at a girl in a kitchen under a dome with her spoon halfway to her mouth.

"Yes," said Hask. "I am from beyond the Shining."

The girl's mouth fell open. "What's the Shining?"

A long grinding pause.

"I do not know," said Hask. "I was asked once. I have been waiting to answer correctly."

She thought about this. Then she reached out one long finger and touched the crab on his shoulder ledge, and the crab raised a claw at her, outraged, and she shrieked with joy and ran up the ladder, and came back with three more children.

◆ ◆ ◆

The girl from the bridge came down about an hour later.

She came down out of the leaves like a dropped scarf, touching every branch on the way, and landed on the boards beside me without making them shake. Long fingers, long toes, freckles in dark patches across her nose and shoulders like the bark of a plane tree, hair in a wet dark rope. His age, near enough.

She squatted on her heels an arm's length from me and looked at my flask, and my slate, and my fingernails, which were glowing a little in the shade, and then at my face.

"Are you the grey boat?"

"No," I said. "I'm the thing the grey boat files against."

She considered this. "Teasel says you tied his boat up with a knot like a wet dog."

"Is Teasel the man with the scar?"

"Teasel says," she went on, "you hung one of them in a tree."

"Only by his net."

Something went across her face that might, in a better light, have become a laugh. She shut it down hard.

"You followed him," she said. "All the way from the dome."

"From a stream in the Spill. He planted tomatoes by a pond. And four at the bottom of a hive. And two," I nodded at the barrel, "there."

She looked at the barrel for a long time. Then back at me, and her eyes had gone narrow.

"What happens," she said, "if you catch him?"

Every answer I had was on a form, and none of them was good enough to say out loud to her.

"I don't know yet," I said. "That's the truth. I know what happens if the grey boat catches him." I drank my tea. "So I'd like to get there first."

She looked at me a while longer. Then she reached over, without asking, and took the end of my honeyed rope, and tied it to the platform's rail in a knot I had never seen in six years of Survey and eleven of Inspectorate. One movement. Three heartbeats. It sat on the rail as neat as a bird on a twig.

"Show me that," I said.

"No," she said.

Then, after a moment, very slowly, with her long fingers held where I could see them, she untied it and did it again.

I tried. It came out like a wet dog. She snorted, and untied it, and did it again, slower.

Across the platform, in the shadow of a doorway, an old woman had been watching us. I hadn't seen her until she moved; she'd been leaning against the woven wall and her skin, I would swear under oath, had gone the same brown-grey as the wicker. Small, white hair cropped short, one eye clouded white and the other black and very bright. She looked at me the way a good Examiner reads a document: all of it, and then the signature.

"Sedge," she said. "Fetch the woman some water. She's drinking tar."

The girl, Sedge, rolled her eyes and went. The old woman leaned back against the wicker and let it have her again, slowly, the way a stone takes on the colour of the rain, and looked away over the drowned orchard as if I were already filed.

◆ ◆ ◆

He came back in the middle of the afternoon, with the tide on the turn.

I'd seen him a dozen times before that and not let myself look. A pale shirt in the leaves. A glow of amber in a fork of branches where something sat with its throat lit, watching me with great suspicion, and then my flask with great interest. Once, a pair of grey-green feet hanging from a branch right over my head, toes curled round the bark, for the length of one page of slate, before they were pulled up out of sight.

Then a moonpear came down out of the leaves and landed on the boards beside my flask with a soft thump, and rolled, and stopped against my knee, glowing faintly.

I didn't look up. I picked it up and bit it. Cold water with honey in it, and a smell like rain. I hadn't eaten since the Hiveplains.

A ladder creaked. Bare plated feet on the boards, slow. He sat down an arm's length and a half away with his knees up, and the animal came down off his shoulder and walked straight across the boards to my flask and smelled it, sneezed, and smelled it again.

"It likes that," he said. His voice was hoarse, with a dry catch at the bottom like a cough he was keeping in his pocket. "I don't know why. It likes all the worst smells."

"Good," I said. "So do I."

He wasn't looking at me. He was looking down at the mud, where Hask stood at the foot of the big tree with five small children sitting on his bottom stone and a sixth trying to get onto his shoulder ledge with the crab.

The six slits on his neck were open, fluttering a little in the air. As I watched, one on the left side began to close, and then thought better of it.

"At the stream," he said. "In the Spill. When the grazers came down." He swallowed. "There was a pile of stones, and they turned round, before they stood up. Like they were looking at me. And then they were a roof."

"Yes," I said.

He looked at Hask a long time. The child on the shoulder ledge fell off into the soft mud, and laughed, and climbed up again.

Then the boy turned his head and looked at me properly for the first time, and asked the only question he'd come back to ask.

"What is he?"

Below us, Hask's top stone turned, slowly, in the green afternoon light, until it was facing up at the two of us on the edge of the platform, and the children on his feet went quiet.

"I," said Hask, "am a discrepancy."
