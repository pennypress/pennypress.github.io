What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 5 of 24

Chapter Four: Evidence Under an Open Case

← Chapter Three: Nothing Floats for FreeContentsChapter Five: Keep Moving →

There are six ways for a record to be wrong, and an Examiner will meet every one of them before her first grey hair. There is only one way for the world to be wrong, which is never. When the two disagree, do not ask which of them is lying. The world cannot lie; it has not the imagination. Ask instead who last held the pen, and what they were afraid of.

— Manual of the Inspectorate of Discrepancies, Preface to the eleventh revision

I woke under a roof that was thinking about something.

The skiff had come back for us the night before and gone away again with nobody aboard but the driver, who had called me several things, of which mad was the politest, and promised to return at ten with a groundsheet, a hamper and a bill. So I had spent my first night in the Spill in the mud by the stream, with Hask leaned over me as a lean-to of carved stone, the way he had leaned over me when the sky fell. Towards dawn I surfaced to a sound from deep in the stack: a slow, faint grinding, like a millstone turning a hard idea over and over and not liking any side of it.

Light came through the gaps between his stones in thin gold bars, and the carved script printed itself in shadow across the backs of my hands, tiny lines of law moving very slightly as he thought. The mud had dried on my sleeves into a grey crust. Everything smelled of wet stone and crushed grass and, faintly, of frightened sky-grazer, which is a rich, warm smell, like a hay barn with a wet dog in it.

"Good morning, Hask."

"Examiner." The grinding stopped. "I must report a change in my condition."

I crawled out. The night sounds had stopped, and in their place was a low, steady seethe, like a kettle a long way off, that seemed to come out of the ground itself: the warm grass, I think, waking up. Mist lay on the stream in a flat white sheet. And the air tasted, on the back of my tongue, green and sweet and very slightly of iron, as if I'd licked a leaf that had been lying on a gate.

Hask rose back into his column, stone settling on stone, and stood there with the patience of a mountain that has been asked a personal question.

The crack across his third stone, with its smear of pale down, had not got worse. That wasn't the change.

Between his seventh stone and his eighth, out of the joint where for ninety years a seed had sat and minded its own business, there was a leaf.

One. Pale, the green of the inside of a celery stalk, no bigger than my smallest fingernail, and folded down the middle like a ticket somebody meant to keep. It trembled when he spoke.

"It was damp," Hask said, "in the night."

"It's lovely, Hask."

"It is undeclared." A pause, long enough for the mist to move a metre down the stream. "At the spaceport, I declared a seed. The officer entered a seed. There is a form, with an attachment, and the attachment says seed. It is no longer a seed." Another pause. "The record is in error, Examiner. I am a discrepancy."

I considered him: three hundred and eleven years on mandate, four thousand one hundred and twelve enumerated harms, one cracked stone, one leaf.

"Do you want me to open a case on you?"

"I should like time," said Hask, with enormous dignity, "to consider."

I left him considering and went to look at the grazer.

◆ ◆ ◆

It was still on the bank where it had sat up after hitting him, sixty paces downstream, and it had not gone anywhere, because it couldn't.

In daylight it was bigger than I'd thought and softer than I'd feared: as long as a skiff bench, pale cream fading to grey along the spine, covered in a fine down that lifted in the breeze like the nap on velvet, its wings folded along its sides like sails put away wet. The face was blunt and gentle, with a soft upper lip that it was using, with great concentration, to strip the seed heads off the grass. Its eyes were dark and wet and set wide, and every few mouthfuls it lifted its head and looked at the sky the way you look at a door you've been locked out of.

Along its belly, from the forelegs nearly to the tail, hung the bladder. It was empty. It lay against the mud in slack folds, like a wineskin after a wedding.

Its prints went into the mud nearly to the dewclaws, two neat toes and a deep heel, because it weighed what a goat weighs and nothing was holding any of it up. I leaned in and breathed. Mud, crushed grass, warm animal. And from the slack bladder, a sort of nothing: a flat, closed-room smell, like a cellar where nobody has kept anything for years.

The grass interested me more.

It had been leaning at my boots since I crossed the cut, the way a cat leans into a hand, as if I were a small stove. When I set the flask down beside me, the grass round it leaned harder. I moved the flask a foot away to be sure. The grass went after it, a little ring of blades bowed towards that dented steel like a congregation towards a pulpit.

But round the grazer, which was warm, which was a great soft hot-blooded animal lying right there in it, the grass stood up straight and ignored it completely.

I wrote that down. Then I unscrewed the flask, because it was dawn and I had slept in a stream, and drank.

My flask has never once in eleven years delivered tea at a temperature suitable for a human mouth; the steel is double-walled, by somebody who hated the cold personally. Fourteen hours after I'd filled it, the tea came out smoking, took the skin off the roof of my mouth, and woke me from the scalp downwards.

"Item fourteen," said Hask, from sixty paces upstream.

"You can't even see me."

"I can hear you," said Hask, "wince."

I was halfway through the second mouthful when the flock came back.

They came from the north, over the ridges: a long loose drift of pale bodies very high up, glowing gold on their undersides in the early sun, like lanterns being carried home after a party. The ground grazer lifted its head and made a small, thin sound, half a pipe, and none of them answered.

And the smell came down to me on the cool air, faint and absolutely unmistakable.

Bread.

One loaf, just out of the oven, in the next room, with the door ajar: warm crust and yeast and something faintly sour underneath. My stomach made a noise the grazer turned its head at.

I watched them go over, high and easy, smelling of breakfast, and looked down at the one on the bank, smelling of an empty cellar, stripping grass because it could no longer reach the seed that floated.

Two kinds. Same animal, same flock, same sky. One kind went up and smelled of bread. The other kind fell and smelled of nothing.

On the slate I wrote: High ones: bread. Low ones: nothing. They fall. And then, after a moment, one word with a question mark after it, which is the closest an Examiner comes to swearing: Drifted?

◆ ◆ ◆

The skiff came whining down the farm track at ten past ten, and the driver climbed down with a hamper on one hip and the face of a woman who has brought bad news and resents carrying it.

"Morning, Examiner. You look like something the Tobins pulled out of their tub." She set the hamper down. It smelled, gloriously, of cheese. "They've filed."

"Who's filed what?"

"Service. Last night." She jerked her chin at the valley behind me. "Clearance order. Crew's coming from the depot at Ancot day after tomorrow with the torches and the tender. Wide break along the cut, and they burn the valley back to the ridge-foot, where the grazers roost." She shrugged. "Farms'll be glad. No roost, no grazers coming down on roofs."

"And the grazers?"

"Go somewhere else. Or don't." She looked at the one on the bank, still stripping grass, and something went across her sunburned face and was put away. "I'm not sorry about the roofs. I'll be sorry about the smell. You never get the smell of a burn out of grain. You taste it in the bread for a year."

I called up the Register. It answered the way it always answers, instantly, without the faintest shadow of a doubt.

CLEARANCE ORDER. Filed: Moriel Heritage Service. Area: Spill margin, sector 40, and drainage to the ridge-foot. Method: prescribed burn. Mobilisation: 48 hours. Grounds: damage to registered property (roofs, 31 claims). Parties notified: none. Objections received: none. Approved.

Filed at nine minutes past eight in the evening; approved at twenty past. Eleven minutes to agree to set fire to a valley, and most of that, I suspected, finding the right form.

I tapped the valley itself. The Register thought about it for no time at all.

SPILL. Status: UNRECORDED.

That was the whole entry. Eight generations of everything Moriel had ever made and stopped bothering to keep, and the Register had it down not as nothing (there are forms for empty land) but as not yet looked at. Unrecorded life belongs to no one. You cannot notify no one. No one cannot object.

I tried anyway, because you do. I drafted an objection in my own name, on my own warrant, the Inspectorate notes that the area covered is the subject of active inquiry, and sent it, and the Register sent it back so fast the screen barely flickered.

OBJECTOR LACKS STANDING: SUBJECT UNRECORDED.

"It isn't worried," said the driver, reading over my shoulder with no embarrassment at all. "Is it."

"It never is."

"Can you stop it?"

I could register the Spill, in theory, and give it standing. Teams take seasons over a biome: survey, census, a description that would stand up in front of a Chancery clerk with a grudge. I had forty-eight hours, a hamper, and a Proxy who was deciding whether he himself needed declaring.

"Arson," Hask observed, coming down the bank in a series of long grinding steps, "is enumerated."

"For valleys?"

"No," said Hask. "For the bearer."

"Then it won't help."

"No," Hask agreed. The leaf between his seventh and eighth stones trembled. "I mention it because I find I am displeased, Examiner, and I have nowhere to put it."

The driver looked at the leaf, and at me, and then very hard at the far ridges, the way people on Moriel do when they're trying to keep something none of their business.

"Groundsheet's in the hamper," she said. "Bill's under the cheese."

◆ ◆ ◆

I followed the stream up.

The plated footprints went that way, with their fine ridged tread like the sole of a good boot. I lost them on the stones, found them on a sandbar, lost them, and after a while stopped looking for prints and just looked.

The valley narrowed. The stream got louder and colder and talked to itself like gravel shaken in a tin. When I put my palm to a fur-tree, purely for the record, it was warm, and something under the fur shifted towards my hand. I took my hand back. The fur rippled, offended. A thing with too many knees, deer-tall, its legs folding three and four times like a carpenter's rule, stepped out of the ferns and regarded me with about half of the ring of eyes around its head. It decided I wasn't interesting. I have been dismissed by Chancery officials with less grace.

I had come to find a boy, and I'll say it plainly: I kept stopping. The flowers did it.

They grew along the bank in a loose row, one every few paces, each the size of a dinner plate on a stem as thick as my thumb, and as I came up to each one it turned its face to follow me. That much I could account for. Half the plants on any windowsill follow the sun, and I had just been told by a tree that things out here run warm. A warm thing walks past; a flower that likes warmth looks at it. I wrote follows heat? in the margin and was pleased with myself for about four steps.

At the fifth, the flower ahead of me turned before I reached it.

I stopped. It stayed turned. I took one step back, and the one behind me, which had let me go, came round again to watch me leave; then the one ahead swung back to meet me, a little late, like a clerk looking up from a ledger. I am an examiner. I did what examiners do with a thing that won't explain itself, which is to stand very still and annoy it.

I unscrewed the flask, which fourteen hours after filling was still too hot to drink, set it steaming on a stone in the middle of the row, and walked ten paces up the bank into the shade.

Not one flower looked at the flask. Every one of them looked at me.

So, not heat. I crossed out the margin note. Then, because counting is what I have instead of courage, I walked on at an even pace and counted. The flower at my shoulder; the one beyond it; the one beyond that. Three ahead of me, then four, then more than I could see: the turning ran up the bank faster than I could walk, plate after plate lifting its face towards a place I hadn't reached yet, until it went round the bend of the stream without me, the whole valley turning one flower at a time to look down it at the woman coming.

They weren't following me. They were passing me along.

I stood there a while with the pencil not moving. The moss sighed under my boots, a long hhhhh. Far up the ridge something went tok... tok..., patient as a clock, and I had the entirely unprofessional thought that it was taking the minutes.

Hask came up behind me, step by grinding step, and stopped when I stopped, and did not hurry me. When I had stood there long enough, he said, "Examiner, there is a gap in my mandate for harm by wonder," and I said, "Is there?" and he said, "It was not thought necessary," and I went back for my flask, and we went on.

The ravine was where the hillside cracked open and the stream dropped through it in a white rush. Below the ravine, the water slowed and spread and widened into a pond.

It was the colour of old glass, green-gold at the edges, so still the sky lay in it without a wrinkle. For a moment, kneeling at the edge, I had the odd sense of being looked at, and by now I knew what that meant out here: I had been announced. I cupped a handful and drank. It was cold enough to ache in my teeth and tasted of stone, and of something faint and clean, like mint that had been near the water once and gone away.

The reeds round the edge leaned away from me.

When I put out a hand they bowed back further, like courtiers from a draught. Everything else in the Spill had leaned towards me since I crossed the cut; these were the first living things on the planet that wanted less of me rather than more.

I wrote that down, under the grass and the flask.

His camp was in the lee of a big fur-tree on the pond's east side, where the roots came out of the bank and made a sort of hollow, and when I found it I stood very still for a long time before I let myself go into it.

Chair first.

So: plain facts.

The hollow under the roots was lined with the tree's own soft shed fur, like a nest, and pressed flat in the middle in the shape of a person curled on his side with his knees up. A person not as tall as I am. Tucked into a crack in the root was a reed stem tied in a row of small hard knots. I counted eight. One for each day, I thought, and then had to stop thinking for a moment. Round the outside of the hollow, close in, in a ring, the earth was printed all over with grazer feet, two neat toes and a heel, dozens of them, overlapping, going round and round.

They were light. The fallen grazer's prints went into the mud to the dewclaws; these barely dented the earth, set down as delicately as a cup on a saucer, and there were so many of them, so close in, that I could only read them one way. They had lain down round him. Pressed up close in a ring, in the cold. The Spill gets cold at night. Baseline humans, as the warm dead voice in the dome would no doubt point out, are inefficient at retaining heat.

They had kept him warm.

On a flat stone by the water there were crusts: grey-brown patties dried hard in the sun, full of the husks of the floating seed. It had been chewed; you could see the crush of molars. I held one to my nose. Sour, yeasty, cool, like the back of a bakehouse before anyone has lit the oven. And under that, faintly, the same warm loaf that had come down out of the sky at dawn.

Further along the bank, in the warm grass, there were flattened patches. Long, grazer-shaped, the grass crushed flat where a big body had lain heavy on it, and at the head of each one, a spatter of the same chewed paste. I counted them, walking slowly, and then again, because I didn't believe it. Eight.

And this was the part that made me sit down on the stone.

Not one of the eight had tracks leading away from it.

The bank between them was soft, and I walked it twice. I found the boy's plated feet going from patch to patch and back to the stone, a beaten path. I found the deep, dragging prints of a downed grazer going into two of the patches. I did not find a single print going out. Nothing the weight of a goat leaves a place without leaving tracks.

Unless it leaves upwards.

I sat on the stone with a crust in my hand and let the logic come.

The grazers were falling, and the ones that fell smelled of nothing. A boy who had eaten bread from one village oven all his life had sat on this stone and chewed their own food into a mash and fed it to them, like a bird feeding a chick. Afterwards they did not walk away. Afterwards there was a flock that floated higher than the rest and smelled, faintly, of a baked loaf.

I did not understand why. But I knew what I was looking at, because I had seen the other half of it in a museum with a gift shop at one end. The Upper House had wanted to draw from him until he was no use. He had come out here and given it away, a mouthful at a time, to animals that weren't his, that no one had registered, that the Register had down as a blank.

The file had called him Reference 12. The director had called him an honour. The shirt in my evidence pouch called him property. I put down the crust and wiped my fingers on my trousers and I thought: no. You're the sort of boy who stops.

It's a terrible quality in a runaway. You'll never get anywhere.

I liked him enormously already.

◆ ◆ ◆

The seedlings were on the pond's south edge, where the bank caught the most sun.

I nearly walked past them, because they did nothing.

Between the last of the reeds and the first of the grass, somebody had pushed seeds into the black soil one at a time; you could still see the pinched dimples where a finger had gone in, first knuckle deep and no further. In a row, there were seven tiny plants, each with two soft seed leaves and a second pair just beginning, crinkled like a fist opening. Their stems were red. A dull, dark, earthy red, the colour of a brick that has been rained on.

Everything in the Spill moved. I had been leaned at and turned towards and sighed over for a whole day, until I'd stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a clock.

The seedlings didn't lean. They didn't turn. When I crouched over them they didn't bow towards me or away. They were small, and red-stemmed, and they just sat there, in a place where nothing else on earth just sat there.

I knew them. I'd looked down on them from a glass bridge the day before: long rows of something low and leafy studded with red. Their cousins at the market stop had been blue and singing, and I remembered thinking the Firstfield ones looked, frankly, a little dull.

At the end of the row, pushed into the soil, was a stick, and somebody had cut a notch in the top of it with something not very sharp, the way a child marks a thing so he can find it again.

So he had meant to come back. Or meant someone to find them. Or he had just done it, because there were seeds in his pocket and soil at his feet and it seemed a pity not to.

"Hask," I said.

"I see them."

"Are they enumerated?"

Hask considered the seven red stems for a long time. The leaf between his seventh and eighth stones stirred.

"No," he said. "There is nothing in the Schedule about being left a garden."

I wrote down: Seven tomato plants, baseline (red), planted by the subject. Then I thought about Fallow's chair, and how often the most important thing in a room is the one that doesn't move.

◆ ◆ ◆

At dusk the flock came home.

I was sitting on his stone by the pond, my hands still smelling of the crusts I'd bagged, when the sky over the ridges filled with them: sixty or more, coming down in a long slow spiral, going pink and then violet as the sun went, and a soft breathy chorus of piping, like an orchestra tuning and never agreeing on the note.

They circled the pond, lower each time, their shadows going over me cool and soft. They came down over the hollow under the fur-tree and hung there, piping, and nobody came out, and they went round again.

One came down.

It landed ten paces off the way a dropped scarf lands, and folded its wings with a sound like a sheet being shaken out. Its bladder was full and taut, and the breeze brought me the smell of it: warm bread, and under it the grazer's own musk.

It looked at me with its wet dark eyes. I looked at it.

"Steady," I said, mostly to myself.

It came over in three delicate steps and snuffled my hands, a long whuffling breath, hot and damp and smelling of grass. Then it found my collar, and my neck, and my jaw.

And it leaned on me.

Under each of its eyes, I learned then, a sky-grazer has a little gland like a teardrop, and when it likes something it wipes that gland along it. It wiped one along my jaw, ear to chin, with the steady pressure of a cat marking a doorpost, and then did the other side for good measure. It was oily and warm. I didn't dare move. Its down was softer than anything I've ever touched, and it smelled, close to, of warm hay and wet wool and honey gone very slightly to vinegar, and faintly, faintly, of bread.

"Examiner," said Hask, behind me, with the slow gravity of a man reaching for a lever. "Shall I object under item eighty-eight?"

"It hasn't got hands, Hask."

"Neither have I," said Hask. "I have never found it a limitation."

Then it lay down beside me with a long contented sigh that went down its body like a wave, and put its chin on my boot. We sat together while the flock settled along the far slope, piping more and more softly, until the whole hillside was hung with pale shapes like washing left out overnight. The first stars came out over the ridge. Real ones. I thought, with no warning at all, of a boy in a loft under a glass sky that went dark blue and nothing else, and I had to look at the pond for a while.

When I got up at last, the grazer lifted its chin off my boot, looked at me as if I'd been rude, and went to sleep.

I walked back along the bank to the hollow, and that was when I noticed the grass.

It wasn't leaning at me.

All day the warm grass had tipped itself towards my boots like filings to a magnet. Now it stood straight up. I crouched and held my palm over it, and it didn't lift. It stood there exactly as it had stood round the fallen grazer that morning, as if I were just another soft warm animal of no particular interest. As if I were flock.

I put my wrist to my nose. Hay, wet wool, honey, vinegar, bread.

I went down to the pond and washed my face and neck in the stone-and-mint water until my skin was raw with cold. Then I smelled my wrist again.

Hay, wet wool, honey, vinegar, bread.

"Hask," I said. "Do I smell?"

Hask considered the question with the gravity it deserved.

"Yes," he said.

◆ ◆ ◆

I didn't sleep. I'm not sure why. The Spill at night, close to, was too loud for it.

Loud the way a forest must have been to the first people who ever lay down in one. The warm grass chirred, like a thousand people gently rubbing their palms together. The stream rang with its underwater bells. Now and then a sleeping grazer piped a single soft note, and another answered. Far up the ridge the tok... tok... went on, and across the valley two whistles went back and forth, trying to agree on a tune. The fur-trees, as the day's heat left them, purred very low, like cats on a hearth, and smelled like a cedar chest being opened.

It was cold. I sat up in the mouth of the hollow in the driver's groundsheet and my coat, the flask held in both hands against my chest, and Hask stood beside me, a darker dark against the stars. I didn't drink much. I wanted the heat in my hands more than in my mouth.

I heard it first as a change in the grass.

The chirring stopped down the slope. Just there, thirty paces off, a patch of silence, and then the silence came towards me, a ripple running through the dark grass like wind; only there was no wind, and the ripple was the grass leaning, blade by blade, all in one direction, pointing.

At me.

No. I held very still and watched it come, and it wasn't pointing at me. It was pointing at the flask.

Then I heard the clicking.

Low down. Steady. A dry, overlapping tic-tic-tic, like slates on a roof shifting in a frost.

It came out of the dark at the bottom of the bank, and I saw it in pieces: a long low body armoured in grey overlapping plates that clicked as it breathed; no eyes; a smooth wedge of a head, held low; and in front of the head, on a stalk of its own flesh, a little bulb of soft blue light, swaying like a lantern on a pole.

The bulb swung. Stopped. Swung back. And came to rest, like a compass needle finding north, on my chest.

On the flask.

"Hask," I said, very quietly.

"Examiner." His voice was very calm and very slightly strained, like a mountain trying not to clear its throat. "I must tell you that I am not yet able to act."

The thing came up the bank one slow step at a time, the whole slope tilting past it at my hands like a field of arrows. Its head split sideways a fraction with a wet click, and I smelled its breath: hot and meaty and rotten, like a butcher's drain in high summer.

"Why," I said, "not?"

"Item three thousand, three hundred and one. Predation by beasts of the larger sort. Appendix C." The words came out of him at their usual speed, which is to say geologically. "The notary was most particular. A beast is of the larger sort if, from the tip of the snout to the root of the tail, it exceeds two ells. And until it has been measured by the Proxy, it is not a beast of any sort. It is—" the faintest grinding pause "—weather."

"Weather."

"If it were a draught," said Hask, with real regret, "I could act at once."

"Then measure it."

"I am measuring it."

And he was. Out of the side of his column, with a long rasp of stone on stone, he was extending his top stone on a short ledge of others, like an arm, if you took a generous view of arms. The notary, I understood, had had it cut to the length of his own ell. Hask was going to lay the measure of a man three hundred years dead alongside a predator in the dark.

The thing took another step. The stone arm crept towards its snout.

"One," said Hask.

The thing moved its head.

"I must begin again," said Hask.

Ten paces. The grass between us lay flat, every blade pointing at the hot steel against my ribs, and I had the most absurd thought, with death clicking its way up the bank: it isn't me. The grass doesn't want me at all any more. It's the tea.

And then I wasn't on a riverbank on Moriel at all.

◆ ◆ ◆

Rain like gravel on a tarpaulin. My second season with the Survey, on a world past the Vessany lanes that nobody has bothered to name properly to this day. A drainage ditch at the foot of a cliff, at night, four of us crouched in it to our knees in cold water, and something walking along the top of the bank above us in the dark.

The crew called them kettle-dogs, because they came to the cookfires: long, pale and blind, with two pits in their snouts that saw warmth the way you and I see light. They'd taken a packhorse the week before. I could hear this one breathing, coming closer, and feel my own heat coming off my face like the glow off a stove door, and I knew it could see me shining.

Big Imre, our crew chief, had a coffee pot he carried everywhere, because he would rather have died than drink cold coffee, and he had just boiled it on the little burner in the bottom of the ditch. He looked down at it as if saying goodbye to a friend.

Then he flipped the lid open with his thumb and lobbed the whole thing, steaming, up over the lip of the ditch and twenty metres down the bank.

The kettle-dog went after it like a shot. We heard the pot go over, and then the most extraordinary noise I have ever heard an animal make, an offended, scalded hoot, and it went crashing away into the dark, sneezing.

"It doesn't want you, Pastern," Imre said, in the dark, already climbing out of the ditch. "It wants warm. Give it warmer, and keep yourself cold." Then, mournfully, to the night at large: "That was a good pot."

He drank cold coffee for the rest of the season, and complained about it, and I never once heard him say he was sorry.

◆ ◆ ◆

The thing on the bank was five paces from me.

I unscrewed the flask.

The steam came up into the cold in a white ghost, smelling of tar and burnt raisins. The bulb shivered on its stalk and swung fractionally upwards, towards the steam.

"Examiner," said Hask, "if you are about to drink that—"

I threw it.

Not at the beast. Past it, high and hard, uphill, end over end, the open neck spinning out tea in a black spiral that steamed in the starlight. It hit a flat stone twenty paces off with a clang like a dropped bell and lay there glugging, tea pouring over the stone in a smoking sheet.

The grass swung.

The whole slope, in one long rustling breath, bowed towards that stone. It hadn't been pointing at me since a grazer wiped its face on my jaw. It pointed at the hottest thing on the hill, and the hottest thing on the hill was my tea.

The bulb swung after it like a needle. The beast spun on its short legs and went for the stone.

"Item fourteen!" said Hask.

"It's not my scalding, Hask!"

"The Schedule," said Hask, extending his stone arm after the beast as it went, "does not specify whose."

It hit the flask with its whole hinged head. I heard the crunch of steel, and then the shriek: a thin, scalded, outraged whistle, and steam blew out of the sides of its wedge where the jaws met, and it reared back, shaking its head, plates clattering, its bulb whipping round and round in mad circles.

That was the moment. Imre had climbed out of the ditch, so I climbed out of mine.

I went into the pond in four strides, up to my waist, and the cold drove the breath out of my chest. I dragged the coat off under the water and came up out of the shallows with it bundled in my arms, sopping and icy, pond weed hanging off one sleeve. The reeds bowed away from me as I came through them, parting like a curtain. I was cold now. I was mud and pond and musk, and to that blue bulb I was nothing at all.

The beast had its head down at the stone, snuffling and sneezing at the spilled tea, clicking with rage. Its bulb hung over the steaming flask. It didn't know I was there. It couldn't. Everything it had to see with was pointed at a dented tin.

I came at it from its blind side, which was every side, and threw the wet coat over the bulb.

The light went out under three kilograms of soaked wool, and the beast went utterly, spectacularly mad.

It bucked. It spun. The plates on its back went off like a drawer of cutlery tipped down stairs. I threw my weight onto the coat, both arms round its head through the cloth, and it was like holding on to a sack of angry slate, much stronger than me. But it was blind, and it didn't know which way it was going, and I did. I set my hip against its shoulder and shoved, downhill, the way you'd shove a stuck cart, and it went where it was pushed, because every direction was the same to it now.

Downhill was the reeds.

They bowed away from its heat as it crashed into them, opening a lane in front of us, and it plunged down the lane with me hanging off its head, through the reeds and over the lip of the bank, and into the stream where it ran fast and cold out of the bottom of the pond.

The water hit us both. It came up over my chest and over the beast's back and over the coat on its head, and I let go.

For one moment I stood in the stream to my ribs, and the beast stood in front of me, half under, and threw its head up and shook the coat off into the current. Its bulb came up blazing blue out of the dark water and swung, wildly, left and right.

Left: cold stream. Right: cold stream. Ahead of it: a cold, wet, pond-soaked, goat-scented woman, standing perfectly still, with her teeth clenched to stop them chattering, and the whole Spill's grass behind her pointing politely the other way.

It couldn't find me. It couldn't find anything. Everything was water.

The bulb swung back towards the bank, towards the stone and the flask still faintly steaming on it, and stopped there, quivering. The beast looked at that hot little dot of tea for a long moment. I swear it remembered its mouth.

Then it turned, with a long, sulky, dwindling tic-tic-tic, and went away down the stream in the dark, splashing and sneezing, until I couldn't hear it any more.

I stood in the water.

On the bank above me, Hask stood with his stone arm stretched out at full length over the reeds, over the exact spot where, for about one and a half seconds, a blind beast had stood stretched from snout to tail in the shallows with a coat on its head.

"It is," Hask announced, "of the larger sort."

I breathed.

"Thank you, Hask."

"Two ells, and the better part of a third." The stone arm withdrew into his column with a long rasp and a click. "I may now act."

"It's gone."

He considered this.

"Then I shall act," said Hask, "retrospectively."

And when I waded out of the stream he leaned over me as a roof of carved stone, entirely unnecessarily, sheltering me against a beast that was half a kilometre downstream and still sneezing. I sat down in the mud under him, soaked to the skin, shaking from scalp to boots, and I laughed. I laughed until I had to put my forehead on my knees. Up on the far slope a sleepy grazer piped once, to ask what all the fuss was, and another one told it to go back to sleep.

When I could stand, I went and got the flask off the stone.

It was still warm. There were two neat punctures in the side of it now, and a great new crumple across the shoulder where the jaws had closed, and it smelled of tar and raisins and, faintly, of hot meat. Dented in three places, it had been, for longer than I'd held any posting. Now it was dented in four.

I've never had it hammered out. I don't intend to.

◆ ◆ ◆

The sensor pinged at dawn.

I'd tied my slate to the feed from 40-K out of habit, and I came up out of a short black sleep under Hask's roof to its chime.

FIELD SENSOR 40-K. One (1) baseline human genome, trace, unregistered, moving. Elevation: 22 m.

Twenty-two metres.

I crawled out and looked at the sky above the sensor post, and there, drifting over the rise in the first light, was a single sky-grazer, grazing the seed off the morning air.

The sensor was a faithful machine, built to taste the air for a baseline human and report what it tasted, and this morning it had tasted him twenty-two metres up, on a goat.

My grazer came down to the pond for a drink and let me run a swab along its soft lip, and then wiped its face on me again, as if it suspected I'd been washing. I ran the assay against the Register's own reference samples, the little vials sent up the long stair, labelled in neat handwriting, once a year since he was born.

MATCH. HOMO SAPIENS (BASELINE). REFERENCE No. 12. Location of record: Upper House.

Location of sample, I wrote underneath, in my own hand: a sky-grazer, floating.

I sat down on his stone and did a thing I have done perhaps six times in eleven years. An Examiner doesn't suggest that the Register is wrong; she says it, formally, and the saying goes up the line to the Chief Examiner's desk with her name on it, and if she's wrong it stays on her record for the rest of her life.

I opened a Discrepancy.

In the matter of Reference No. 12, of Firstfield. The Register holds the subject transferred to the Upper House and present there. The world holds the subject's living trace present in the Spill, sector 40, as of this morning, confirmed by assay. Class: Unrecorded. Further classes pending. Case open.

Then I began to enter evidence.

One scrap of grey knit shirt, labelled PROPERTY OF THE MORIEL HERITAGE SERVICE. One sleeping hollow under a fur-tree, lined with shed fur. One reed stem, knotted eight times. Thirteen crusts of chewed seed. Eight flattened patches of grass and the bank between them. Seven tomato plants, baseline, red-stemmed, and one notched stick. The plated footprints along the stream and the pond, wherever they could still be found. One fallen sky-grazer on the bank below 40-K, bladder empty, as a control.

And the flock.

Every sky-grazer in the valley bearing the subject's trace, entered as a living exhibit, in situ, together with the roost and forage they depend on for their condition. I counted them twice from the bank as they stirred on the far slope, and got sixty-three both times, and wrote sixty-three. The Register asked me, very politely, if I wished to enter sixty-three exhibits under one number.

I said yes.

The Register thought about it for no time at all.

EVIDENCE ENTERED. Under open case. May not be destroyed, altered or cleared.

"Hask," I said. "I need a witness."

He had been waiting for this, I think, for a very long time. I held the slate against his pale mandate stone, worn smooth by centuries of other people's thumbs, and the Register recorded an Orrun Proxy's attestation. And Hask made the small grinding sound deep in his stack that is, in an Orrun, roughly equivalent to applause.

◆ ◆ ◆

The burn crew came at nine. A day early.

I heard them first: the hum of a water tender along the farm track, and the clank of drip-torches coming off a truck, like saucepans having an argument. Somebody upstairs had heard overnight that an Examiner was sitting in a valley asking questions, and had brought the order forward. That was a plain fact too. I wrote it down.

I walked down to the cut to meet them. My coat, fished out of the stream at first light where it had snagged on a root, hung off me in damp folds with pond weed still in the pocket. My hair had dried in the shape of the inside of a stone. I smelled, quite strongly, of goat.

There were eleven of them in fire-orange, lined along the grain like a hedge of marigolds, and a crew boss, a lean man with burn scars like lace up both forearms and eyebrows that had been singed off and grown back thin and surprised. He had a drip-torch in one hand, and when he saw an Orrun cairn come out of the Spill behind me he looked from one of us to the other, working out which to address.

"Inspectorate," I said, and showed him the warrant. "Morning."

He read it. He sniffed, and tried to pretend he hadn't.

"Examiner," he said. "Order's approved. You'll want to stand back of the cut. We light at ten, wind permitting."

"You don't, I'm afraid."

"Ma'am, I've got a clearance order from the Heritage Service with the Register's seal on it—"

"So do I," I said. "A different one."

I held out my slate so he could see the case. He read it carefully, lips moving a little at the long words.

"Sixty-three exhibits," he said.

"Living."

"You've entered a flock of goats as evidence."

"Sky-grazers. In situ. Together with their roost and their forage." I pointed up the valley, where the flock still hung along the far slope like washing, beginning to stir in the sun. "The roost you're about to burn. Evidence under an open case may not be destroyed, altered or cleared. You can burn it, of course. And the Register will put your name on it, and mine, and the Service's, for longer than either of us is going to live."

He looked at the flock for a long moment.

"They're goats," he said, but not with much hope.

"They're the only place on Moriel where that boy is still on record as being anywhere at all," I said. "Look at your order."

He looked down at his slate: the same clearance order, the same seal, and under it a new line the Register had added on its own, without being asked, because it does not care which of us is right; it cares only that it agrees with itself.

SUSPENDED. Conflicts with open Discrepancy. Area contains evidence.

"Thirty years," said the crew boss slowly. "I've never once seen an order suspended."

"You've never had an Examiner sit in your valley."

Behind him, his crew had started to grin, the way crews do when the boss has been out-argued by somebody they'll never see again.

The crew boss handed the drip-torch to the man behind him without looking round. Then he looked at me, and his surprised eyebrows did something complicated.

"You do smell like a goat, you know," he said. "Ma'am."

"Evidence," I said.

He laughed. It came out of him unwillingly, like a cork, and then he couldn't stop it, and he shook his head and turned round and made a circling motion over his head with one scarred arm, and eleven people in fire-orange began, not at all reluctantly, to load the torches back onto the truck.

Hask stood beside me and watched them go, the tender whining off down the farm track in a long plume of gold dust.

"Examiner," he said. "I have been wondering what the Spill would have done, if it had been able to object."

"It doesn't get to. It's Unrecorded."

"Yes." The leaf between his seventh and eighth stones turned, very slightly, towards the sun. "I believe it has just done so anyway. Through you."

I didn't answer that. I unscrewed the flask, refilled that morning on the driver's spirit stove, and took a mouthful, and it was too hot, and I let it be.

"Item fourteen," said Hask, comfortably.

◆ ◆ ◆

That evening I sat by the tomatoes.

The flock had come home at dusk and gone round the hollow twice looking for a boy who wasn't there, and settled. My grazer had wiped its face on me on the way past and gone to sleep with its chin on my boot. I had washed in the pond three times that day. I could still smell hay and wet wool and honey and vinegar on my wrist, and under it, very faintly, bread.

"Examiner," said Hask.

"Yes, Hask."

"I have considered." The grinding settled into silence. "I wish to declare it."

"The leaf?"

"Under the attachment. As an amendment." He said the word amendment the way other people say sunrise. "I would not have it said that the record was wrong about me, and I knew, and did nothing."

So I opened the attachment the customs officer at the spaceport had filed, Seed, provenance unknown, lodged in Proxy, and under it, on the slate in the last of the light, with Hask dictating at the speed of continental drift, I added: Now leaf. One. Pale. Folded like a ticket. Hask said the last part was not strictly necessary. I left it in. He let me.

He made the small grinding sound again, deep in his stack.

The stars came out over the ridge, one and then another and then a great many. Down at the stream the bells began. The seven tomato plants sat in their row in the dark, small and red-stemmed and doing nothing, with a notched stick at one end, and I thought that there are worse things to be the evidence of.

My slate chimed.

Not the sensor. The Register: the soft double note it makes when a record I'm following has changed. I picked it up expecting a filing note, my Discrepancy going up the line.

HOMO SAPIENS (BASELINE). REFERENCE No. 12. TRANSFERRED: UPPER HOUSE.

And then, as I watched, the last word of the line below dissolved and reformed itself, letter by letter, very neatly, the way the Register does everything.

STATUS: RETIRED.

I looked for the next line. The one that tells you where a retired thing goes.

There was no next line.

The timestamp said it had been entered eleven hours after I opened my case. Someone upstairs, a long way from this valley and these stars and these seven small red stems, had signed a form saying the boy was finished; and the Register, which has never in its life been uncertain about anything, had read the form, and found it in order, and serenely agreed.
