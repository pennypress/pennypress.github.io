# Chapter Three: Nothing Floats for Free

"Okay," I say, and the warmth goes in.

Not up my arm. In. It sinks through the skin of my wrist the way rain goes into a dry garden, all at once and then gone, and where it went there's a mark left behind: a faint green-gold tracery under the skin, like the veins on the underside of a leaf. It itches. I want to scratch it, and I'm afraid of what I'd be scratching.

There, says the voice, from somewhere just behind my eyes now, instead of from the pond. Shall I fix it?

Up on the ridge, the clicking stops. Then starts again, nearer.

"Yes. Fix it. All of it."

All of what?

"You said. The leg. The cold. The —"

I said I could. It sounds faintly embarrassed, the way Tilly sounds when she's been caught with her finger in the honey. I didn't say I knew what they were. I can only hold a shape you can hold. You'll have to tell me what a leg is.

"It's a leg. It's the bit you stand on."

That's not a shape. That's a job.

I think of a jar held up to the schoolhouse window, and twenty children made to say every part of the thing inside it out loud, feelers, thorax, the little hooks on its feet, until the smallest ones stopped screaming and started pressing their noses to the glass. Name it and you can hold it.

So I name it. I shut my eyes and do it the way Dr. Ommery taught us in the medicine lessons, with the chart of the flayed man on the schoolhouse wall that Jory drew a moustache on. The calf. Skin on the outside, then the yellow fat, then the red underneath, which is muscle, Bram, and is not called meat while it's still on you. The cut runs down the outside of it, from the back of the knee nearly to the ankle bone, shallow at the top and deep in the middle, where the shale went in. I think it as hard as I've ever thought anything. I think it like reciting.

Oh, says the voice, pleased. There. Got it.

It is the worst itch of my life. I kick the water. I bite my own knuckle. When I look, the red thread I've been trailing down the ravine has stopped, and the edges of the cut are drawing together like a purse on a string, and where there was an open mouth in my leg there's a pink seam, puckered and shiny, already pale at the ends.

"Feet," I say, through my teeth. "Do the feet."

What are feet meant to be?

I know this one. I've heard it every morning of my life. Baseline feet, as you can see, lack the calloused plating of the modern. And I've watched the moderns for four years at the Window: the school group on hot days, kicking off their sandals in the skywalk, and the bottoms of their feet not pink at all but grey-green and close-grained, like the sole of Old Saul's good boot, if Saul's boot were alive and fond of him. Thick. Grippy. Something you could stand on a hot stone with and not dance.

"Plated," I say. "Like theirs."

The soles of my feet go hot. Then hotter. Then it's like standing in nettles, and then like standing on warm bread, and I make a noise I'm glad nobody in Firstfield is here to hear. When it stops I pull my right foot out of the mud and look at it, and the bottom of it isn't mine. It's grey-green, faintly scaled, the way a lizard is in the pictures. I press it with my thumb. It gives, like good leather. The blisters and the bloody places from my long walk are just gone, as if they were a rumour.

"I'm a modern," I say, and laugh, a bit wild. "Look at me. Tap the glass."

You're welcome, says the voice. Now for the bill.

The bill arrives in two parts.

The first part is hunger. Not the hunger of the half hour before supper. It opens in the middle of me like a trapdoor, so suddenly I put both hands on my belly to check it's still there.

The second part is heat. Mending is work, says the voice, a little apologetically. Work is heat. The cold mud on my arms has dried pale and is cracking and flaking off, and in the low gold light coming through the reeds I can see my skin steaming. Faintly. Like a pudding cloth.

"Oh, no."

I crawl out onto the bank, because I can't think in the water, and the grass leans in.

All of it. As far as I can see along the bank, every blade has bowed towards me, a ring of little green arrows pointing at the boy-shaped place where I'm lying. The moss on the stones has turned its tips towards me too. It's the way the crowd in the east skywalk leans on a Tuesday, all pointing down at the Window. There he is. There's the one.

Up on the lip of the ravine, the bulb appears. Soft blue. Swaying on its stalk.

Yesterday it swung back and forth across the stream, hunting. This morning it doesn't swing at all. It stops, steady as a needle, and points. Not at me, exactly. I'm half behind a boulder. It's pointing at the grass. At the ring of arrows, and at whatever the arrows are pointing at.

The clicking starts down the slope.

I can't do the mud again. I'm too hot; it would steam off me like Ma's washing in front of the stove. My legs are shaking. I've got no spoon. I look at the grass, and the grass looks at me, all of it, like a crowd of tattletales.

And then, because there's nothing else to look at, I look at the reeds.

They're leaning away. From me. Every stem in the bed nearest my boulder is tipped a little out over the water, as if I smell. I noticed that yesterday, kneeling to drink, and didn't know what to do with it. But I know the grass tells, and the reeds don't, and I know which side I want to be on.

I slide back into the pond on my belly, into the reeds, and the cold comes up my body like a hand closing.

It's deep here, deeper than the shallows. I push in among the stems until they're thick round me, and sink until there's only my nose and my eyes above the water, and the water is so cold it hurts in my teeth. Behind me, the grass on the bank is still bowed towards the place where I went in.

At the edge of the pond, the arrows stop. The reeds don't pass it on.

The thing comes down the bank. I hear it before I see it: the clicking of its plates, the hiss of grass under its belly. I smell it, too, which I didn't yesterday: hot iron and something sour, like a cheese cloth left too long in the sun. Then it's there, long and low and slate-backed, on the bank above me, and the bulb swings out over the water.

And every reed in the bed leans away from it.

Away from the warm thing on the bank. Which means towards the pond. Which means towards me. All at once, the whole reed bed bows over my head like a crowd stooping to look at something on the ground, and the stems cross and lock above me, and I'm under a roof. A green, ticking, rustling thatch, with sunlight coming through it in coins, and the blue light swinging back and forth beyond it, finding nothing.

I don't breathe. I don't think about breathing.

Something small and slippery mouths at my big toe under the water, tasting the new plating. I let it.

The bulb follows the arrows to the water's edge, and there the arrows stop, and it swings out over the reeds, and the reeds lean further away from it, smooth and flat as a combed head of hair. Nothing. No warm boy. No arrows. Just a pond with its hair brushed.

The thing puts one foot in the water. It doesn't like the cold any better than I do. It pulls the foot back with an offended rattle, and stands there a long time, clicking.

Then it goes.

I wait until I've counted to five hundred, which is how long Gran says you leave bread before you peek. Then I stand up in the pond, and the reeds lift off my head slowly, stem by stem, like people getting up after a prayer.

My stomach, which has been very patient through all this, picks that moment to make a noise like the Friday thunderstorm: all three crashes, in the right order. Somewhere two days south of here, Tilly is under the table.

Was that you? says the voice.

"That was me."

Shall I fix it?

"Don't you dare."

I tie a knot in a reed stem, so I'll know how many mornings there've been. It's the second.

* * *

I find out about hungry.

In Firstfield, hungry is the smell of Ma's onions through the floor of the loft, and a slap on the wrist if you go for the crust. Out here, hungry is a hole I carry about with me, and it's always the same size, however much I put in it.

The voice knows what's food. It tastes through my fingertips.

The fat white roots at the edge of the pond: Fine. Boring. Eat it. They taste like wet bread and pennies, and I eat eleven.

The glass-bell fruit on a scrubby little tree by the ravine mouth, in clusters that chime when the wind takes them: Sweet. Fine. You'll be sick after nine. I eat eight, and I'm sick after eight, and the voice says Huh in a way I don't like.

And a fat purple fruit, soft as a bruise, hanging low and warm and smelling of plums and honey. I'm already biting it when the voice says That one will make you hear colours. Don't.

"Now you say."

I said it during.

I spend that afternoon on my back in the moss. The pond is a long, low note, like the bottom string of the fiddle at harvest. The reeds tinkle. The patch of sky between the fur-trees is blue at me, very loudly, for three hours, and every time a cloud crosses it the blue goes up half a step, and my own hand, when I hold it up against it, hums pink. At one point I'm fairly sure the sun clears its throat.

I did say, says the voice.

"You said it during."

When the colours have worn off, I try asking it to fix the hunger. It seems only fair. It fixed the leg.

What's hunger?

"It's — here." I put my hand on my belly.

That's your stomach. Your stomach's a bag. The hungry isn't in the bag. Where is it?

I think about it. I really do. I can name the stomach and the gut and even the liver, which Dr. Ommery says is the most loyal thing in the body and the least thanked. I can't name the hungry.

Then I can't hold it, says the voice, quite cheerfully. You'll just have to eat.

* * *

At night the Spill gets loud in a different way.

The fur-trees purr as they cool. It's very low, lower than the dome's hum, and it comes up through the ground into your back. The moss sighs every time something small crosses it, hhh, hhh, so the dark is full of little sighs going off in different places like someone thinking about you. Out on the pond something rings, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass, and something else answers it from the far side. A long way off, up towards the ridges, the sky-grazers pipe to each other, soft as owls.

And it's cold. Proper cold. In Firstfield the dome keeps the nights at sleeping weather. Out here nobody's keeping anything.

Shall I fix that? says the voice, the second night, when my teeth are going.

"Can you?"

Tell me where warm comes from.

"The blood carries the heat," I say. I know that one. Dr. Ommery, at the flayed man. "From the middle to the outside."

Good. I could thicken it. Make it run hotter. That costs food. You haven't got any food. You'd wake up hungrier than you are, and colder, and then you'd die. A pause. I'm only saying. I'd do it, if you said yes.

"No," I say.

It's the first thing I've said no to out here, and it feels odd in my mouth. Like a stone you're not sure is a sweet.

Instead I find a fur-tree with a hollow between two of its roots, just bigger than me, and lined with old soft shed fur like a nest. I curl into it. After a moment, the tree shifts. The warm fur on its trunk leans down into my back, the way it did on my first night, when I snatched my hand away and it rippled, offended. This time I don't snatch. I lean back. The tree purrs into my spine.

"Thank you," I tell a tree.

It's then, lying in a tree's lap, that I look up, past the edge of the canopy where it breaks over the pond, and the cloud that's been sitting on the Spill for two nights slides off sideways, and I see the sky.

It isn't blue.

It's black, a black that goes back and back, the way the pond goes down. And it's full. It's full. There are more lights in it than there are people on Moriel, more than there are seeds in all Saul's tomatoes, put together, ever. Hard white ones, blue ones, one yellow one low over the ridge like a lamp in a window, and behind them all a long pale smear across the middle of the sky like flour somebody spilled and didn't wipe up.

The stars like dust.

That was the poem. In the schoolhouse library, in the old part, before Master Aldous took the book back to fix its spine. A kind of old weather, he said. It isn't weather. I don't know what any of it is and I can't stop looking.

All my life, Tilly said, I told everyone the night was wrong. I didn't know what I meant. I meant this. Someone put a lid on all of it and painted the lid blue.

"What are they?" I ask the voice.

It considers. Hot. Far. Not food.

Then, after a while, when I still haven't looked away, more quietly: You like them.

"Yes."

Hm, it says, and I feel it put that away somewhere, carefully, the way you put away a thing you mean to look at later.

I think: what do you call a sky that won't be blue? And I laugh out loud in the dark, so suddenly the moss sighs, because I know the answer. It's a sky. It always was.

* * *

On the third day I notice the grass is talking.

Sitting on my boulder chewing a root, I watch it move the way people move when they're talking: little ripples running through it, passing from blade to blade, never quite in time with the wind. When a knee-deer folds itself past, a lean runs through the grass ahead of it, like gossip down a lane ahead of a cart.

"What's it doing?"

Talking. The voice sounds bored. Everything out here is talking. Most of it is about lunch.

"I want to hear it."

That gets its attention. I feel it sit up, if a thing without a back can sit up.

Then tell me where you listen.

Not my ears. I know that much. I think of the Window, and the visitors who came through once arguing in smells, whose children sneezed all the way down the skywalk. I think of the tongue. Dr. Ommery's other chart, the old printed one by the schoolhouse door: sweet at the tip, salt and sour at the sides, bitter at the back. She took a pencil to it on our first day and wrote WRONG across it in letters as tall as my hand. Somebody a long time ago copied a careful drawing badly, she said, and then everybody copied the copy, and now half the worlds in the Tally think your tongue has neighbourhoods. It doesn't. Every bit of it tastes everything. She kept the chart up anyway, as a lesson about copying. And behind and above the tongue, up inside the nose, the place where smell goes, which she said was the oldest sense we've got and the one we use least.

"Here," I say, and touch my tongue, and the bridge of my nose. "Make it hear."

It costs me the rest of the day. I go hot and then cold, and fall asleep in the tree's hollow in the middle of the afternoon with my mouth full of pennies, and when I wake, at dusk, the world is shouting.

The air tastes of chatter. That's the only word. The grass is saying one thing over and over, sweet and quick and nervous on the front of my tongue: warm here, warm here, warm here, every blade passing it on to the next, and all of it, the whole bank, pointed at me. The fur-tree behind me is saying something slower and darker, at the back, bitter, like strong tea: rain coming, rain coming, a day off yet. The moss is mostly complaining. And the pond, under it all, tastes green and very old and very patient, and I realise it's the taste of the voice's old home, and that the voice is listening to me taste it.

Well?

I can't answer. I'm grinning so hard it hurts.

The bill for this one is subtler. Food tastes of noise now. The white roots taste of everything they were ever next to: wet clay, a worm going past, a rival root complaining about room. The glass-bell fruit tastes of the bee that pollinated it and the tree's opinion of the bee. I lie in the hollow that night trying to remember the taste of porridge, plain Firstfield porridge with nothing in it but oats and salt and Ma's back turned while you sneak honey, and I can't get it. I can remember that it was plain. I can't remember what plain tasted like.

You were very simple, the voice said, my first morning. I think about that, with the grass whispering warm here at me all night like a child who won't go to sleep. I used to be one note. I'm not sure I wanted to stop.

* * *

On the fourth morning I plant Saul's seeds.

The paper twist is damp and soft as cloth from three days in my fist and my pocket, and when I shake it the seeds rattle, faint and dry inside. I pick a spot by the pond where the sun comes in early and stays late, between the last of the reeds and the first of the grass, where the earth is black and crumbly and smells, to my new tongue, of a hundred small things quietly eating each other.

First knuckle deep and not a hair more, Bram. I push each seed in with one finger, the way he taught me, and pinch the soil shut over it.

These are old, says the voice, tasting through my fingertips. Like you.

"They're Saul's."

They're very quiet.

"They just sit there," I say. "That's the point of them."

I pat the earth flat. There's nobody to say anything to but a pond and a voice and a lot of nervous grass, so I say what Gran said to me, in her chair by the dead stove, holding out the twist.

"In case wherever you're going," I tell the Spill, "hasn't got proper ones."

The grass leans in to listen. It's only my warmth it wants. I don't mind.

* * *

On the sixth evening I follow the stream down out of the ravine, to see where it goes.

It goes into a valley. The ravine opens out and the stream runs dark and quick along the bottom of a broad shallow bowl, and on the far side the ground climbs to a low rise, and beyond it, far off, there's a line. A line so straight nobody in the Spill drew it. On this side, the tangled dark green of everything out here. On that side, gold, flat and shining, going on to the edge of seeing: grain, more grain than the whole of Firstfield could eat in eight generations, standing in the late light as if it gave off its own.

And the grain is breathing out. As the sun goes down, the heat of the day lifts off the fields, and it carries seed with it: fine, pale, floating seed, each grain on its own little tuft, rising in slow golden clouds like the steam off Ma's washing, drifting out over the cut and over the valley, glittering where the sun catches it.

And down out of the ridges behind me, to meet it, come the sky-grazers.

Up close they're bigger than I thought and gentler than I thought, both. Long, pale bodies, the length of a man lying down, with a soft swelling ridge along the back where the float is, and short wings that barely move, held out like a dancer's arms. Soft blunt faces, like a calf's, with big surprised dark eyes. They drift down into the seed clouds with their mouths open, and they graze, sifting the air, turning slowly, a whole flock of them, maybe sixty, maybe more, hanging in the gold over the valley like a field of washing hung out on nothing.

The air under them smells. To my new tongue it's warm and sweet and faintly sour, like something busy. Like a thing I know and can't place.

Halfway down the near slope, in the grass, there's a grey post. Waist-high, with a cap on top that's cracked across and furred with green. As I come past it, something inside it whirs, and a little vent on one side opens and sniffs. With a sound like Saul getting the last of his tea.

It's tasting you, says the voice.

"What is it?"

Not alive. A pause, with an edge on it. Very rude.

I think of three little vials of my blood, labelled in Dr. Ommery's neat handwriting, going up the long stair. The post whirs again, and the vent closes, satisfied.

"Good evening," I tell it, with great dignity, and walk on down to the water.

I'm on the stones by the stream, watching the flock, when one of them falls.

I see it first because it's wrong. It's at the bottom of the flock, lower than the others, and it's working too hard. Its mouth is open and it's sifting and sifting, but its wings are going, which the others' don't, and it's sinking anyway, slowly, the way a leaf sinks in still water. It touches the far bank, tries to lift, beats its wings and gets nowhere, slithers down into the shallows twenty paces from me, and sits there with its legs folded wrong and the water running round it, piping, thin and high, like a kettle left on.

The flock drifts on over its head. Nobody comes back.

I go.

My feet take me, slipping on the stones and then not slipping, because the new plating grips wet rock like a cat grips a rug. The grazer sees me coming and pipes louder and thrashes, and I stop, and do the only thing I can think of, which is crouch down a long way off and breathe. The Window breath, the one we do for the visitors. After a while it stops thrashing and just watches me with its big, surprised, exhausted eyes.

I come closer a step at a time. I put my hand on its side.

It's warm, and soft, softer than anything, a fine pale down like the inside of a dandelion clock. The swelling along its back isn't swollen. It's slack, like an empty wineskin, folded in on itself in creases.

I put my ear to its belly.

Saul's old horse, back home, gurgles all day like a drain. You can hear its supper going round from the other end of the field. This belly is quiet. There's a heart in there, going too fast, but under the heart there's nothing. No gurgle. No busy.

And it doesn't smell. The flock overhead smells warm and sweet and sour, and this one smells of nothing. Of wet down and stream water and nothing else.

I sit back on my heels in the shallows, and think.

It eats seed. It floats. There has to be something in between those, because nothing in Firstfield is free either. Ma's honey costs the bees their whole summer. So what does a sky-grazer pay for floating? It must be the seed. The seed goes in, and somehow it comes out as float.

So the seed's going in. I can see it: its mouth is gold with the stuff. And the float's not coming out.

"Something's stopped," I say. "In the middle. Between the eating and the floating."

Yes. The voice has gone quiet and interested, the way it was with me at the pond. It's tasting through my palm. There's a room in there. A pouch. Full of little lives, very small, millions. They eat the seed and they breathe out gas, and the gas is lighter than air, and that's the float. A pause. They're tired.

"Tired how?"

Copied. They've been copied too many times. Every generation copies the last, and every copy is a little wrong, and the wrong adds up. Copy of a copy of a copy. And the seed's been copied wrong too, the gold stuff from over there, so now they've got a food they don't quite recognise and a body they don't quite remember how to work. They're not dead. They've just gone slow. Like a song somebody's hummed so many times they've forgotten the tune.

I look at the gold beyond the cut. Three of the grain lines are already off-reference. A soft, pleased voice through a vent, and a smell of coffee. I didn't know what it meant. I still don't, really. But I know what it tastes like, now. I scoop a little of the floating seed off the surface of the stream where it's settling, and touch it to my tongue, and it tastes like a word spelled nearly right.

"Can you fix it?"

I can only write in you, says the voice. And even if I could get into it, you'd have to name a sky-grazer's insides to me. You can't even name where your own hungry is.

The grazer pipes, weakly. Its head is starting to droop into the water.

The smell of nothing. I know that smell. That's what's been bothering me. I've smelled that nothing before.

* * *

It was the hard winter, the one when something went wrong in the ribs of the dome and frost grew on the inside of the glass in ferns and feathers, and the whole village slept in its coats.

I was small enough then to fit under the stairs standing up. I was small enough that feeding the bakehouse crock was still a new job, a proud one, and one morning I lifted the cloth and nothing came up to meet me. No sour. No cellar. The starter was grey and flat in the bottom of the crock, with a skin on it like an old puddle, and it smelled of nothing at all.

We made bread with it anyway, for three days. It came out of the oven like roof slates. Jory's da broke a tooth on a loaf. By the third day the whole of Firstfield was walking round grey and cross and chewing, and Ma cried a bit, in the bee shed, where she thought I couldn't hear.

On the third night Gran got out of her chair. She took the candle and went to the dark corner under the stairs and knelt down, which took her a long time, and pulled out the mother crock.

It was brown clay, older than the house, with a cloth tied over the top with string. Gran untied the string with her bent fingers and lifted the cloth, and the smell came out and filled the whole kitchen, sour and strong and yeasty and alive, like a cellar that's happy. Like something that had been waiting there in the dark all this time, keeping the tune.

She took one spoonful. Just one. She put it in a cup.

"Is that all?" I said. I'd thought it would take the whole crock. I'd thought it would take something enormous.

"That's all it ever takes," said Gran. "It's not the flour that's sick, Bram. It's the little ones in the crock. They got cold, and they got tired, and they forgot how. These ones remember." She tapped the clay. "From my gran. From her gran before her. They've never been anywhere. That's why they remember."

She held the spoon out to me, with a scrape still on it. "Taste."

I licked it. It bit me. Sour at the sides of my tongue and something sharp right at the back, like a tiny slap.

"There," said Gran. "Every morning, when you feed the bakehouse crock, you taste it. If it doesn't bite you back, it's sulking, and you come and tell me."

The next morning I woke up and the whole lane smelled of bread. Real bread. People came out of their houses in their nightclothes and their coats, under the frosted glass, just to stand in the street and breathe it.

* * *

Every morning of my life since, I've lifted that cloth and fed that crock and licked that spoon, and every morning it bit me back.

I've been walking round all my life with Gran's gran's little ones in my mouth.

Oh, says the voice, slowly, tasting me now instead of the grazer. Oh, yes. You're full of them. Old ones. The same family as the ones in there, from before all the copying. And then, in a voice I haven't heard from it before, almost respectful: They remember the tune.

"I'm the crock," I say. Out loud, in the shallows, to a dying sky-grazer and a stream. "I'm the mother crock."

I don't know how to give a grazer a spoonful of me. And then I do, because I've seen it in the Heritage Primer, on the page about birds: a mother on a nest, and three gaping beaks, and her bending to feed them out of her own mouth.

I scoop up a double handful of the floating seed from the slack water by the bank, where it's settled in a golden scum, and I put it in my mouth, and I chew.

It's bland and woolly and faintly sour, and it tastes, to my new tongue, of a song with the wrong words. I chew until my jaw aches, until it's a warm paste, until it's as much spit as seed, and then I take the grazer's soft blunt face in one hand and lean in, and it opens its mouth, surprised, and I feed it. Mouth to mouth. Like a bird.

It's the most disgusting thing I've ever done. Jory would pay money to see it.

The grazer works its lips. Swallows. Looks at me with enormous dark eyes, as if to say, What was that?

Nothing happens.

I do it again. And again. My jaw is on fire. The sun's going down behind the ridges and the whole valley's turning purple, and the flock is just pale specks against it now, and I'm kneeling in a cold stream with my hand flat on a quiet belly, chewing, waiting, the way Gran waited with the cup on the stove all night.

Nothing.

And then, under my hand, so faint I think I'm imagining it, a gurgle.

Then another. Then a warmth, spreading out under the down like a stove being lit. Then a creak, from the slack pouch along its back, the noise a wineskin makes when you start to fill it.

The grazer lifts its head. It looks at me. It opens its mouth very wide.

And it burps.

It is the biggest burp I have ever heard. It's like the mill sluice opening in spring. It echoes off the far side of the valley and comes back. And it rolls over me, warm, in a great soft cloud, and it smells of bread. Fresh bread. The bakehouse in Firstfield at six on a winter morning, with the frost on the glass and the loaves on the board and the whole lane coming out in their nightclothes.

For one breath I'm home.

I laugh. I'm crying, but mostly I'm laughing, kneeling in the stream with bread-breath on my face, and the grazer's back is rising like dough in a warm kitchen, filling, smoothing out its creases, and its legs unfold, and its wings come out like a dancer's arms, and its feet leave the stones.

It hangs there in front of me, a hand's breadth off the water, wobbling. It bumps my face with its soft nose. Then it rises, slowly, and then less slowly, up into the purple, piping, one long joyful note, and goes to find the others.

I stand up in the stream and do a dance. I'm not proud of it. Nobody sees except the post.

That, says the voice, was disgusting.

A pause.

Do it again.

* * *

I do it again.

The seventh evening, two of them come down. The eighth evening, five. Not falling, exactly: drifting down out of the bottom of the flock on purpose, as the dusk comes on and the seed rises, landing heavily in the shallows or on the bank, folding their long legs, and turning their soft blunt faces towards me with their mouths open, like Tilly when there's honey cake. They can smell the bread on the ones I've done. Or they can smell it on me. I can't tell any more where one smell stops.

I chew. I feed. I wait with my hand on a belly till it starts to sing. My jaw aches all day, and the hole in my middle gets bigger, because everything I chew goes to them and not to me, and I eat roots between grazers like Saul eats bread between rows.

My feet have kept changing, too. I asked for grippy, the voice says, and grippy is a big word. By the eighth day the plating has crept up round my heels and over the knuckles of my toes, ridged like the bark of the leather trees, and I can stand on a thorn and only know about it from the sound. When I walk the stream bank I look back at my own prints and don't quite know them.

Each time, the burp. Each time, the bread. I never get used to it. Every single time, for one breath, I'm home.

The ones I've done float higher than the rest. I can see it, at dusk, when the flock comes down to graze: a cloud within the cloud, riding a little above the others, steadier, turning easier. I can find them in the flock with my eyes shut. They smell, faintly, of bread. The slow ones, the ones I haven't got to yet, hang at the bottom, working too hard, and they smell of nothing.

Every dusk, as I go down to the water, the grey post on the slope whirs and sniffs. I start waving at it. It never waves back.

They're not mine. I want to be clear about that, because I can feel myself wanting them to be. They don't follow me about. They don't come when I call. They come down at dusk because that's when the seed rises and that's when I'm there, and they go up at dawn because that's where the seed is. On the eighth night, a few of them don't go up. They come and settle around the fur-tree where I sleep, in the dark, a ring of long pale shapes folded down in the grass, their floats creaking softly as the night cools, smelling of bread and sour and warm down. One of them, half asleep, stands on my hand. It doesn't notice.

The grass doesn't point at them. I notice that, lying there, with my new tongue. Warm as they are, the grass leans past them as if they weren't there. Ask it about a sky-grazer and it just says weather, sky, nothing. Ask it about me and it says warm here, warm here till I want to stamp on it.

My shirt catches on a thorn that night, by the water, the grey knit one they gave me upstairs, and when I pull free a corner of it stays on the thorn. There's green thread on it, along the hem, very small: PROPERTY OF THE MORIEL HERITAGE SERVICE. I look at it for a while. I leave it where it is.

Upstairs, they took three vials of me and labelled them and didn't look at me once. Down here, a sky-grazer burps bread in my face and then looks at me for a long time with its surprised dark eyes, as if to say, Well. That happened. Then it eats a beetle off my shoulder.

On the eighth night, lying in the ring of sleeping grazers, the grass changes its tune.

It's still saying warm here. But there's something underneath it, from far off, up on the ridge, passed along blade to blade, so faint I have to hold my breath to taste it. Warm. Moving. Warm. Moving. And the fur-tree behind me goes very still and stops purring, and the moss stops sighing, all of it, all at once, the whole Spill holding its breath with me.

The clicking doesn't come. Not that night. But I lie awake till dawn and listen to the grass telling the whole wild exactly where I am, and I think: that's how it found me at the pond. It never needed to see me at all. It just needed to ask.

* * *

On the ninth evening, I tie my ninth knot and go down to the flock.

They're grazing low tonight, along the edge of the trees on the slope above the stream, on the far side from the post, where the seed comes drifting up the valley thick as snow. The air is gold and then rose and then going violet, and the ridges behind me are turning purple, and I'm up in the trees among the grazers with my jaw aching and my mouth full of seed, chewing for a slow one with its head in my lap.

That's when I see them. Down across the stream, on the post's side, at the water's edge.

A woman. Crouched on her heels at the edge of the stream, looking at the mud. Very still. The first person I've seen in nine days, and something in my chest goes up like a grazer with a full float, and I nearly shout.

I don't. She could be from upstairs. She could be the man in the coat's. She could be anything.

Beside her, by the water, there's a pile of dark stones. A tall one, almost as tall as a door. I don't remember it being there yesterday. I'd have noticed.

I'm still frowning at the stones when the grass starts shouting.

Warm here. WARM HERE. Up the slope behind me. A hard, fast ripple, every blade bowing in turn, a long green path of arrows leading up out of the trees and across the slope, straight to the boy under the fur-tree with a grazer's head in his lap. And something walking up the path. Following the arrows. Warm, moving. Warm, moving.

Clicking.

I'm up. The slow grazer scrambles away piping. I go along the slope, away from the clicking, and the grass bows after me as I run, here, here, here, and the moss sighs under me, and it's my first morning all over again except that now I can taste it coming: hot iron and sour cheese cloth, thick on the back of my new tongue.

The slope ends. Of course it does. It ends at a shelf of rock above the stream, a lip of stone with a drop below it, twice the height of the meeting house, onto boulders. There are no reeds down there. There's no pond. There's no mud. I'm hot from running, and every blade of grass from here to the ridge is pointing at me.

It comes out of the trees.

Long and low and slate-backed, the plates rubbing, the wedge of its head swinging up, the bulb on its stalk turning to me and stopping. It isn't hurrying. It's had nine days to think about me.

Its head splits open sideways like a book.

All around me, the flock is still grazing. Low, along the lip of the shelf, at head height, they drift in the seed with their mouths open, not knowing anything yet. Two of them are right beside me. Big ones. Strong. I know them by the smell: bread, the strongest bread on the slope, two of the very first ones I did, riding high and steady and full. One has a nick out of the edge of its wing. I fed that one three times.

And for no reason at all I'm standing at the bottom of the bell tower in Firstfield, the summer before, and Jory beside me with his eyebrows up so far they've nearly left his face.

Bet you won't.

I did, that time. All the way to the top, to see if the sky got closer. It didn't, and Elder Marrow had to come up with a rope, and Jory told everyone it was his idea, which it was.

Bet you won't.

The thing gathers itself, all its plates sliding together at once, a long low rattle.

"Watch me," I say.

I grab them.

One hand in the soft down at the base of each long neck, where it's thickest, fingers dug in to the knuckle, and I jump off the shelf of rock with nothing under me but a drop onto boulders.

The two grazers scream. A high, outraged, piping scream, right in my ears, and the sound goes through the flock like wind through grain, and the whole slow pale drift of them breaks.

They go up. All of them at once, a great pale burst of bodies and beating wings, floats swelling with a noise like a hundred bellows, piping, piping, and my two go up with them, and I go up with my two. My arms nearly come out of their sockets. My feet kick at nothing. Below me there's a snap like a trap shutting, and something brushes my heel, and I look down and the thing is right there under me, reared up on the lip of the shelf, its head open, its bulb swinging up after me, and I kick it.

I kick it with my new plated heel, as hard as I've ever kicked anything, right in the soft blue bulb.

It makes a noise I'll hear for the rest of my life. A sort of shocked, wet click. It drops off the lip of the shelf and down out of sight among the boulders, and I'm gone.

I'm flying.

I'm flying. Swinging between two outraged sky-grazers like the clapper in the bell, over the shelf, over the boulders, out over the stream, with the wind in my mouth and the valley turning under me, purple and gold and spinning. My heart's going like Tilly's feet on the stairs. I'm screaming. I'm laughing. I can't tell which. Both.

This, says the voice in my head, very calm and very interested, was not what I meant by do it again.

And then, below me, the flock dumps gas to turn.

I feel my two lurch and catch. They're full. They hold. But at the bottom of the flock are the slow ones, the ones I never got to, heavy with seed and smelling of nothing, and when they let go their gas to turn, there's nothing behind it.

They drop.

Three of them. I watch them go, folding up like dropped washing, straight down, out of the violet sky, towards the stream. Towards the woman crouched in the mud with something in her hand, who is looking up, and not moving, and towards the pile of dark stones beside her.

"LOOK OUT," I scream, which is useless, from up here, with the wind and the piping and the whole flock going mad.

And the stones stand up.

I see it. I see it. The tall pile of dark stones by the water comes apart and moves, faster than anything that big should move, stones grinding and tipping and re-stacking with a noise like a landslide changing its mind, and it leans over the woman and spreads, and it's a roof. A lean-to of dark rock, over a woman in the mud, built in the time it takes to blink.

The first grazer hits it. I feel the thud in my teeth from up here.

My two carry me on, up, round, away. The flock has found its height. They're going north, towards the ridges, and my two go with them, but lower than the rest, and heavier, labouring, because they're carrying a boy. Behind me, the valley falls away. The last thing I see, as the trees close in under my feet, is the roof of stones, still standing over her.

What, says the voice, was that.

It doesn't sound bored. For the first time since the pond, it doesn't sound bored at all.

* * *

They carry me over the ridge.

The ridge goes by underneath, close enough to touch, and then it drops away, and on the other side there's another valley, and another ridge behind it, and another behind that, all purple and blue and going on and on in the last of the light, further than I've ever seen anything go. More Spill. More of everything. Somewhere out there a river catches the sky. Somewhere further out, something enormous and slow is moving on a hillside, and I've got no name for it at all.

The air's cooling. My two are sinking. They come down slowly, piping to each other in low worried notes, over a slope of deep moss on the far side of the ridge, and I let go with numb fingers from about the height of a hay cart and land on my back in the moss with a long sigh, hhhhhhhh, all the way down.

I lie there. The two grazers drift down beside me, smelling of bread, and fold their legs, and look at me with their big surprised eyes. The one with the nicked wing burps, softly. I laugh till I can't breathe.

Then it's quiet. The fur-trees down the slope start to purr. Overhead the clouds pull aside, and there they are again, all of them, the stars like dust, more than all the people and all the seeds.

And up from the other side of the ridge, carried on the cooling air down the long valley, so faint I think at first it's the pond-ringing thing, comes a sound.

A woman laughing.

Not a polite laugh. The other kind, the kind that comes out of you when you've been very frightened and you're not any more and you're sitting in mud. It goes on for a long time. It's the best sound I've heard in nine days.

I don't know who she is. I don't know what kind of thing in all of Moriel builds a roof out of itself without being asked.

But I keep coming back to one thing, lying in the moss with two grazers breathing bread at me, and the grass all round me leaning in, whispering warm here to anything that's listening.

Just before they stood up, the stones turned. The way the flowers turned to watch me on my first night. The way Gran turns in her chair without opening her eyes, because she always knows where you are.

They didn't turn to the woman. They turned to the sky. They turned to me.
