What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 14 of 24

Chapter Thirteen: Hold Last Heading

← Chapter Twelve: Who Wrote YouContentsChapter Fourteen: No Reader on Record →

UNITS KEEP STATION ON THE UNIT AHEAD. IN EVENT OF SIGNAL LOSS, UNITS HOLD LAST HEADING AT TRANSIT SPEED UNTIL SIGNAL IS RESTORED. DO NOT STAND IN FRONT OF THE LEAD UNIT.

— enamel operator's plate, Varro-Tandem GLEANER harvester, Pale sector 9; scratched underneath, very small: where else would you stand

The punt's bottom hisses on silt and stops, and Teasel leans on his pole and says, "That's as far as wet goes."

I climb out. The mud's warm. My soles grip it. Up ahead, Hask walks out of the last of the channel with water pouring off his stones, and the crab that's ridden his shoulder ledge all morning looks at the dry country in front of him, thinks better of it, and climbs down and sidles back towards the sea without a word. Biscuit, on my head, watches it go with deep suspicion.

Teasel looks at me a long moment. Then he takes a moonpear out of his shirt and holds it out. It glows a little in the shade of his body.

"Mind the dry," he says. "It'll have your chest. Breathe through your nose." Then, to Sedge, much rougher: "And you come back."

"I always come back."

"You come back this time," says Teasel, and pushes off before she can answer, and doesn't look round once, all the way back into the trees.

Sedge watches him go with her arms folded. She smells of a wire pulled tight.

"He doesn't give people pears," she says, as if it's my fault.

◆ ◆ ◆

We go up over the last salt-grass and a belt of grey thorn that rattles like dry beans in a tin, and over the low hills behind it. And at the top of the last one, the world goes gold.

Everyone stops. Even Hask.

It goes from my feet to the end of everything. Grain, waist-high on me, all one height, all one colour, all leaning the same way under the same wind, so the whole plain moves at once like the sea, only dry. The wind runs across it in long waves and I can watch each one travel. One starts at my feet and rolls away, and I count, and I'm at forty before it's too small to see.

And the sound. At home, on Thursdays, the dome does the sprinklers, and for an hour the whole village hisses like a pan of milk just before it boils. This is that, except it doesn't stop at the end of the hour or the edge of the village. A million million stalks rubbing, hssssssss, all the way to where the sky comes down.

The air tastes of chaff, dry and papery and a bit sweet, like the inside of a flour sack. My chest feels it at once, a tight tickle under the ribs. I breathe through my nose like Teasel said. Biscuit takes one look at the sun coming up white over all that gold and pours itself down the neck of my shirt.

"Who planted it?" says Sedge.

"A company," says Odile.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

At Highwater nothing is the same as the thing next to it. I watch Sedge try to fit the Pale into her head, and fail.

"It's all one," she says at last, the way you'd say it's all dead.

She steps forward and lays her palm flat on the tops of the grain, the way I touched the warm grass my first night out of the dome. Then she snatches it back. There's a thin red line across her hand.

"It cut me."

"Edges like paper," says Odile, already wrapping her own hands in strips of sailcloth. "Keep your sleeves down."

Hask comes up and stands with the grain hissing against his carving.

"Examiner," he says. "This field is very large."

"It is, Hask."

"It is not enumerated," he says, sounding almost pleased, and walks on into it.

◆ ◆ ◆

In all that flat, the things that stick up, you notice. The spines first: great curved pipes, grey and streaked with rust, coming up out of the grain on legs and arching over and going down again, one after another, in rows to the edge of the world, with little brass noses hanging off them, dripping. Under each spine there's a ditch you can't see, because the grain grows right to the lip. You only know by a line of greener stalks and the smell of wet. From far off they look like the ribs of something enormous that died lying down, and the grain grew up over it.

Then the stations, grey and square, each with a mast and a blue light blinking slow as a sleepy eye.

And the patches. You smell them first: sweetness gone over, like the bakehouse bins on the third day of summer. Then the gold in front of you turns the colour of an old nail. The stalks in the rust stand up too straight, because the heads are light. I pull one and roll it in my fingers. Husks. Not a grain in it.

I stand in the rust with the empty head in my palm and hear the soft pleased voice of the man in the colour-changing coat, through the Upper House door. Three of the grain lines are already off-reference.

"Bram," says Odile, from ahead. She doesn't say come on. She says my name and waits.

She's got two days. She told Lenna so at dawn, and Lenna said then go, and hugged her, quick and hard, which surprised them both.

I drop the husk.

◆ ◆ ◆

By mid-morning the footfall stops being far away.

I've heard it since the Undercroft, a door shutting in a big house, fainter all down the coast until the sea drowned it. Out here, every step is bigger. Fifty-one. Thump. Fifty-one. THUMP. I feel it in my knees.

Hask has stopped twice, his whole stack turned east like a dog's nose.

"It has walked within my hearing for many days," he says. "Stone hears stone."

"It's not stone."

"No. But it is heavy, and patient, and it does not hurry. I believe it has been listening to me also."

We come over a long low rise between two spines, and there it is.

◆ ◆ ◆

I've never seen anything so big that moved.

You could put the meeting house inside it, and the bakehouse after. It's a machine; it's got bolts as big as my fist, and panels, and pipes. But it's standing in the grain on six legs like a beetle the size of a hill, and it's grazing.

Low at the front there's a mouth wider than our lane, with teeth like a comb. The teeth go through the grain, slow, and the grain goes in, and somewhere inside there's a deep grinding chew, grrrrrumm, and out of the back comes a plume of chaff that drifts away gold like smoke off a bonfire. Behind it a straight path of stubble runs off to the horizon.

It's been wild a long time. Moss all down its legs, black-green, thick as fleece. A whole tree growing out of what must have been the bin on top, crooked, grey-leaved, full of small brown birds coming and going, and pale flowers trailing down its flanks, and white streaks where the birds have been for years. On its side, under the moss, letters taller than me: a G, an L, and the rest gone to rust. It smells of hot oil and old straw and bird and the sun on metal, and it ticks deep inside like the dome's ribs cooling at night.

It lifts a leg. So slow. Moss dripping. Puts it down.

THUMP.

Biscuit, down my shirt, gives a squeak of pure joy and comes out.

"Biscuit, no—"

But Biscuit's smelled it. Oil. Biscuit smells of lamp oil and river, and has been looking all its life for something that smells this much like Biscuit. It flows down my leg and away through the grain, and I go after it, and Sedge after me, and then all of us are standing in the stubble in the great cool shadow, with the chew going grrrrrumm overhead, and Biscuit is halfway up the front leg, rubbing its whole face on a dripping joint with its eyes shut.

Sedge has stopped dead. "Is it an animal?"

"It's a machine."

"The grey boats were machines," says Sedge slowly. "They didn't have a tree."

The chewing stops.

Its head is a broad flat front over the mouth with a row of round glass eyes along it like a crown, most of them cracked, two still lit a dim yellow. They swing round, the way an old dog turns when you come into the kitchen. And they settle on Hask.

Hask has walked right up to it. His bottom stone is level with the first joint of its leg. A pile of carved stones in the shadow of a hill on legs, and they look at each other, and neither says anything for a long time. I put my palm on the ground. Its hum is in the dirt, deep and uneven, like an old man breathing in his sleep. And Hask's hum under it. Two slow sounds in the earth, side by side.

"Hask," says Odile carefully. "Is it of the larger sort?"

"It has not been measured."

"I think we can assume—"

"Appendix C does not permit assumption." His top stone grinds and slides out from his stack like an arm, cut to the notary's ell. "First it is necessary to establish whether it is a beast."

"It's a machine, Hask."

"It grazes. It wanders. It has young living on it." The birds. "It has not been told what to do for a very long time, and it continues anyway. Appendix C does not require that a beast be alive. It requires that it be measured."

He lays the ell against its leg and walks it up. One. Two. He reaches the top of his reach and stops. And the machine, which has stood there patiently the whole time, lowers itself. Joint by joint, with a long groan of old metal and a shower of moss and two indignant birds, it kneels its huge front down into the stubble the way a cow kneels, until its cracked crown of eyes is level with Hask's top stone.

Sedge's fingers dig into my arm.

Hask measures it nose to tail, all the way down its flank in the shade, counting through the ground, and the machine turns its two lit eyes to follow him the whole way. Biscuit falls asleep in the moss.

At last Hask comes back round to the front.

"Forty-one ells, and part of a forty-second. It is of the larger sort."

"Thank you, Hask," says Odile, very gravely.

"I am not sure what I shall do with it. It does not appear to be preying."

The machine breathes out, a long sigh of hot air smelling of oil and old grain, and a faint pip comes from inside it, like a question.

"I believe," says Hask, "it was also wondering what I was."

◆ ◆ ◆

An hour later we find its cousins.

I hear them first: under the hiss of the grain, a ticking in the ground, quick and light, like rain on the bakehouse roof. And under that, every few seconds, a flat dull click I feel in my teeth, coming from a station mast.

Click. And every harvester in the field turns.

There are forty of them, or near enough, each the size of a hay cart, grey and six-legged and clean, with a comb-mouth and one wide glass eye at the front like a lamp, and VT stamped on their sides. They go across the field nose to tail, and when the click comes they turn, but not all at once like grazers. Like a rope being pulled: the front one turns, and the one behind it turns at the same place, and the one behind that, all down the line.

The lead one hits a rock and stops dead, its eye flashing orange. And the whole line bunches up behind it, nose into tail, clack clack clack, like ducklings when the mother stops in the lane. When the lead backs up and goes round the rock, they all go round it, in exactly the same place, though none of them can see it.

Sedge laughs. "They're stupid."

"They're polite," says Hask.

One harvester has dropped out at the near edge, standing still with its mouth jammed full of stalk and wire, ticking unhappily, its eye a steady orange. And Biscuit, who has a taste for oil now and isn't going back, is already up its leg. It drapes itself in the warm shade under the eye's hood, throat glowing amber, across the top of the glass. It covers the left half of the eye completely.

The harvester stops ticking. Then, very slowly, it turns. Right, away from the side that's gone dark. All the way round in a circle, chewing on its wire, with Biscuit riding its face like a hat, and back to where it started, and on round again, looking for the half of the world that's gone missing.

Sedge sits down in the stubble because she can't stand up for laughing. Hask makes a short grinding rumble in the ground that I'm almost sure is a laugh.

I fetch Biscuit off. The harvester stops turning the moment its eye is clear and stands there ticking, as if it would prefer not to discuss it.

We go wide, along a strip beside a spine. Sedge is in front, still giggling, and she puts her foot down on what looks like more grain and isn't.

She drops. I grab her collar. Odile grabs my belt. Sedge hangs with her legs kicking over nothing, and below her, through the stalks, I see the ditch: a straight cut as deep as Saul's barn is tall, lined with old grey stone, brown water at the bottom, thick and still. We haul her out. She's white under her freckles, her smell a struck match.

"Watch for the green stalks," says Odile, breathing hard. "The green ones mean water."

Sedge looks at the line of bright stalks running off under the spine, straight as a ruler, all the way to the sky.

"I hate this place," she says. And then, because she's Sedge: "Did you see my legs go, though?"

◆ ◆ ◆

In the afternoon we come to a field that's dying.

Not a patch. All of it, spine to spine and past both, the colour of an old nail, standing straight up and empty in the heat. The smell comes off it like heat off the oven, sweet and rotten and sour. Flies. Nothing else.

I don't decide to go in. I just do. I kneel in the middle and put my hand on the ground and listen.

A field of grain talks. Not much. Water. Sun. Here's where the root's going. All one voice. Out in the gold this morning it was like the whole village singing a hymn: one tune, a million mouths.

Here, there's a gap.

The hymn's still going, thin and dry, but a voice is missing out of the middle. Not a clever one. A low one. The note that holds the others up, the way Elder Marrow's voice holds up the midwinter hymns so the rest of us can wander about on top of it. You don't hear that note till it's gone, and then you can't hear anything but.

Look, says the voice behind my eyes. You know how now. It's only grain. It won't cost much.

I take an empty head between my fingers and name it, seed, and go in, just to the door, the way you look through the bakehouse window without going in to buy.

It's the same city I saw in Davin. Roads, doors, the roaring oven-streets, the four letters on the thread: Apple, Crock, Gran, Tilly. But it's wrong in a way I don't understand at first. In Davin the old book was clean and the new writing was scrawled on top. Here it's the other way round. The top pages are fine: be grain, be gold, grow this tall. It's the bottom of the book that's going.

The first pages, the ones that tell the little busy machines how to read three letters at a time, are worn, the way the meeting house steps are worn in the middle where eight generations have walked. Copy of a copy of a copy. The little machines stumble on them. They build a chain and it folds wrong, and the shape doesn't do anything, and the grain stands in the sun with an empty head and doesn't know why.

It's not the grain that's drifted. It's the floor it's standing on.

Every line on this world is built on that floor, says the voice quietly. Grain, grazers, bees, reef, trees. The same first pages under all of them. Everything that copies itself wears.

"Mine aren't worn." I've read Davin's, and Davin's were mine.

No. Yours are the cleanest copy I've ever tasted. That's what I meant, at the pond. That's what a beginning is. A pause. That's why the men upstairs want you.

I come out with a cold sweat down my back in all that heat. Odile's crouched at the edge of the rust with her slate on her knee. She doesn't ask. She waits, the way she waited at Highwater with her tea until I came down.

"It's not the top," I say. My voice is rough with dust. "It's the bottom. The bit underneath everything. It's wearing out. And I've got a clean one, and that's all I am to them. A clean bottom page."

Odile looks at me for a long time. Then she writes it down, slowly, so I can watch the letters go on. And to my surprise, it helps.

◆ ◆ ◆

Late afternoon, in the long shade of a spine, the drone comes.

A thin whine high up, like a gnat in a bedroom. Odile's head comes up and she says "Down," very quietly, and we're flat in the grain with the stalks hissing over us, and Hask simply being a pile of stones, which he's good at.

It comes over low: grey, flat, the size of a tea tray, blue light at the front, VT on its belly. It swings round in a long lazy curve, and comes back.

"It'll log whatever it finds," breathes Odile, "and upstairs will decide what it was. Stay still."

The drone swings away east. Towards the big machine.

The old harvester has followed us, I realise, all afternoon, fifty-one counts at a time. It's standing in the grain a few hundred paces off with its tree on its back, chewing. The drone goes straight at its head and hangs there, ten feet from its cracked eyes, blue light blinking, looking.

The old machine stops chewing.

It lifts its head up and up on those mossy legs, higher than I've seen it, until its eyes are above the drone, and opens something on its front I hadn't seen, a round black mouth like a trumpet's, and bellows.

It's a horn, low and cracked and enormous, the sound you'd get if the meeting house could moo. My gills slam shut. Biscuit goes rigid in my shirt. The drone flips in the air like a leaf and tumbles away, its blue light spinning.

And across the field, forty grey harvesters stop dead.

One heartbeat. Two. The ticking in the ground has stopped. The station goes click, and nobody turns.

The old horn sounds again. And every harvester turns to it at once, the way the grazers turned when one of them piped. Same tongue. They're cousins. The old one is saying the oldest word they were all made knowing, and the word means run.

They run.

◆ ◆ ◆

Not away from the horn. Not anywhere sensible. Out of their field in a long grey line, nose to tail, faster than I knew they could go, six legs each pumping like the mill's pistons, the ground a drum under them, chaff coming off them in a gold storm. The comb-mouths are open and the teeth are going, a row of flashing blades at the height of my chest.

Straight at us.

Not at us. They don't know we're here. Whichever way the lead one was pointing when the horn blew, that's the way, and the lead one was pointing down the strip between the spines, and we're in the strip. Ditches either side under the grain. Open Pale ahead, flat as a table, and forty sets of blades coming down it faster than we can run.

Hask turns.

"Examiner." His voice comes up through the drumming ground, perfectly calm. "Crushing by conveyance, wheeled, tracked or legged. Item two thousand four hundred and fifteen. Get under me."

"There are four of us, Hask—"

"Then I shall be inadequate," says Hask, "for the first time." And he begins, slowly, to lower himself into a roof.

He'll hold Odile. He might hold Sedge. Forty of them will go over him, legs and blades, and I don't know if even Hask holds after forty.

I look at the lead harvester. It's ten lengths in front, its wide eye glaring orange, and every single one of the others is following it. Not the horn. Not the mast. The one in front. Round a rock because the one in front went round a rock.

And this morning, at the edge of a field, one went round and round in a circle because an otter was lying on half its face.

"Sedge. Get under Hask."

"What are you—"

"Bet you won't," I say, which isn't fair, and I'm already running.

◆ ◆ ◆

Not away. Up.

The nearest spine comes out of the grain on its rusty legs right beside me, and I go up the leg the way Sedge taught me to go up a tree, mostly by laughing at me. My plated soles grip the rust. My hands find the bolts. Then I'm on top of the pipe, round and warm and ringing under my feet, and I run along it.

It arches down ahead, a great grey rib, and the harvesters come up underneath on my left, the lead one's back hammering along below the spine, gaining, the noise like the whole of Firstfield falling downstairs. My chest's on fire. The dust's in it, the dry ache, a cough coming up like a bucket from a well. Breathe properly, says Ma.

Bram, says the voice, very fast. Your legs. Shall I—

"No time to name it!"

Then run faster.

The lead one's under me. Two lengths. One.

I think of Jory's face, at the bottom of the bell tower.

I jump.

◆ ◆ ◆

I land on its back on my knees and both hands, and it's like landing on the mill wheel when it's running. Everything's going up and down at once. The metal's hot. My soles grab, slide, grab. I nearly go off the side into the blades of the one behind, and I get a fist on the edge of the eye-hood and hang on.

The eye's right there below me, orange, wide as a cartwheel. It can see the strip ahead, and Hask lowering himself into the grain like a man kneeling in church, and Odile and Sedge going under him.

I get my shirt off over my head with one hand, the too-big sailcloth with the red patch on the elbow, and lean out over the hood, hanging on by my knees and my soles and one fist, and throw it over the left half of the eye.

Half the world goes dark for it.

It turns.

Right, away from the dark. Not much. A lean, like a horse when you pull one rein. The strip goes past at a slant now, the ground tilting, and behind me I hear the second harvester reach the place where the lead one turned, and turn there too. Clack. And the third. Clack. Like a rope being pulled.

On our right, the grain goes bright green. A long line of it, straight as a ruler.

"Sorry," I tell the harvester. I don't know why. It's very hot under my hands and it's only doing what it was made knowing. "Sorry. It's not deep. Well, it is. Sorry."

The green line rushes at us. I let go.

I hit the grain on my shoulder and roll, and the stalks cut my bare back like a hundred paper edges, and I keep rolling because I can't stop, and fetch up on my face at the very lip of the ditch with my nose over nothing.

And the lead harvester goes in.

It doesn't slow down. It holds its heading right off the edge of the world, all six legs still going, and drops nose-first into the ditch with a noise like the oven door slamming times a hundred, and a great brown sheet of water comes up out of the cut and comes down on me.

And the second goes in on top of it. And the third. Each one reaching the place where the one in front went, and going there too, nose to tail, faithful, polite, all the way down. They pile up in the ditch like dropped loaves, legs going, mouths churning mud, grey bodies sliding over each other, and the clang and splash and grind of comb-teeth on old stone goes on and on.

Then it stops.

Forty harvesters, in a ditch the depth of Saul's barn, in a long grey heap, legs waving slowly in the air and every eye flashing orange at once, like a field of the worst fireflies in the world. On top of the heap, the lead one's eye is still half covered by a sailcloth shirt with a red patch on the elbow.

Somewhere out in the Pale, the station mast goes click, hopefully.

Nobody turns.

◆ ◆ ◆

I lie on the lip of the ditch, wet and coughing and bleeding from about a hundred small places, and I laugh. I can't stop. I laugh until the cough takes over and cough until the laugh comes back.

Sedge drops on her knees beside me, covered in chaff, smelling of a struck match and a green stick cracking at the same time, which I didn't know a person could do.

"You idiot," she says. "You absolute — you threw your shirt at it—"

"Bet you wouldn't."

She hits me on the arm, hard. Then she looks at the ditch full of harvesters waving their legs, and her mouth goes, and she's lying in the stubble with her hands over her face, howling.

Hask grinds up and regards the heap for a long time.

"Forty harvesters," he says. "I measured none of them. They are no longer moving with intent."

Odile comes last, and stands over me with her hands on her hips, her collar torn and chaff stuck to the musk on her jaw. She smells like a lamp turned all the way up: fierce, bright. And under it, the embarrassed thing, like Ma when you catch her singing, only bigger.

"Is there a form for that?" I say, from the ground.

"No," says Odile.

She takes out her flask and unscrews it and holds it out to me. I've never seen her offer it to anyone.

"Item fourteen," says Hask at once.

"He's earned item fourteen. Drink it slowly."

It's like drinking a bonfire. Tar and smoke and burnt raisins, all the way down to my feet. I cough so hard Sedge has to thump me, and Odile takes it back and drinks, and screws the cap on, and says nothing at all.

Biscuit puts its head out of Odile's coat, smells the oil in the ditch, and makes a small noise of longing.

"No," say all three of us at once.

Out across the Pale, the old machine lowers its head and goes back to grazing, as if it had never said anything at all.

◆ ◆ ◆

We camp in the shade of the spine, because nobody can walk any more. Odile climbs down the heap of grey backs and fetches my shirt off the harvester's eye, wet and chaffy and smelling of oil, and Biscuit sleeps in it.

The sun goes down red through the dust, and the spines stand up black against it, rib after rib. I can taste the dew coming. Before she slept, Sedge tipped a little of her water skin into the crack between Hask's seventh and eighth stones, where his leaves have gone limp and grey at the edges in the dry, the way you'd water a plant on a windowsill. Hask said nothing. Hask let her.

I tie my knot. The twenty-first. I've eaten Teasel's moonpear and three of Odile's crackers and a fistful of raw grain from a healthy field, and I'm still hollow.

You're thinking very loudly, says the voice.

"You said there were others like you. Asleep. And one of them's here. That's why we came this way."

I said.

"What are you? Not hungry, mostly. Not small, curious. The real answer."

It's quiet so long that Odile's slate stops scratching. Then, behind my eyes, it does a thing I've never felt it do. It goes careful, the way Gran goes careful before she says something she's decided to say.

When your village was new, it says, the ones who brought everything here were afraid of exactly this. The grain. The rust. They knew it would drift. So they made a place to keep the first copy of everything they brought. The grain the way it was. Your crock the way it was. The bees and the clover, the trees, the reef-builders, the grazers. And the floor under all of them. Clean.

They made seven of us to keep it. Seven ponds, all across this world. We hold it, and we read it out to anyone who comes to read with us, so that whenever a line wears soft, someone can come to the pond and set it right.

We were the Library. I'm a Librarian.

Out in the dark, something enormous takes a step. Fifty-one.

"What happened?"

Nothing. That's what happened. It sounds tired. The ones who made us came to read, at first. Then their children came, less. Then their children's children, now and then, to look. Then nobody. You can't read on your own; we were made for two. So I slept, and the pond grew reeds, and things came and drank and fell in, and I tasted every one of them, and they were all noise. Copies of copies. Two hundred years, near enough.

And then a boy fell in at dawn with his leg open and his blood in my water, and he tasted like my shelves.

My throat's shut, like my gills.

I should tell you that I was hungry, it says. So hungry, Bram. I'd have taken anything that tasted right. That's true.

"And the rest?"

I asked if you wanted fixing, and you didn't say yes. You said, would I still be me. A pause. Nobody ever asked me that. The ones who came long ago asked what I could do. You asked what you'd be. Then I asked who that was, and you couldn't tell me, and you said okay anyway.

So it's both. I was hungry, and I liked you. I don't know how to make it only one.

I lie on my back in the stubble with the stars coming out over the Pale like dust somebody's breathing on.

"Were you lonely?"

It doesn't answer that. Which is an answer.

"Show me," I say.

It'll cost. More than Davin. And you'd have to name me.

I think of the meeting house's one shelf of books, and the Charter in the cedar chest, going on for ever in the lamplight.

"You're a library," I say. "Show me the library. All the way in."

◆ ◆ ◆

It isn't like going into Davin. That was sinking, honey with a lamp in it, and then a city, roaring.

This is going down a stair.

Cool. Dim. Quiet as the meeting house at midwinter with the lamps out, when you can hear the snow coming in through the top vents and landing on the boards. It smells of the pond: stone and faint mint and the green-gold sharpness of my own wrist. Down and down. Then it opens out.

A hall.

It goes off further than I can see, the way the Pale does, and it's full of shelves. Not wood. Living ones, pale and folded like the gills under a mushroom cap, row after row, curving up into the dark. On every shelf the thread, wound on spools and packed in bundles, so much of it the hall smells of it the way the mill smells of flour.

Nothing's being built. Nothing's hauling. It's being kept.

Walk, says the voice. It's all round me here, in the walls. Read the shelf-marks.

The first shelves taste gold, like the field this morning with the hymn going all round me. I take down a spool. It's grain. The Pale's grain, but not the Pale's grain: whole, every page sharp, the top of the book saying be grain, be gold, and the first pages underneath crisp as the day they were written, every three letters clear.

I hold it up beside the dying field I can still taste on my fingers, and I can see, line by line, exactly where the field has gone soft. Here. And here. Like holding the Primer next to Tilly's slate when she's copied it wrong. You don't have to be clever. You just look from one to the other.

Look twice, says Saul, somewhere.

I go on. Honey and warm wax: bees, and the clover they were made for, wound side by side the way they were meant to go. Chalk and salt: the stitchers. Hay and wet wool: grazers.

And one that's sour, and cool, and breathing.

I know it the way you know the smell of your own house in the dark. A cellar that's happy. The bakehouse at five in the morning with the cloth off the crock. Gran's mother crock under the stairs.

The first one. The one every crock on Moriel was copied from, that the grazers' fermenters came down from before they went slow in the Spill. Kept clean on a pale shelf under a pond for two hundred years by something that has never tasted bread.

I put my hand on it. It's warm.

And there, says the voice. In the middle.

The shelves go round in rings, like the rings in a cut tree, and at the centre, where you'd put the lamp in a round room, there's something that isn't on a shelf at all.

The floor.

The first pages. The machinery that reads all the rest. Grain and bees and reef and crock and grazer, all built on it, the way every house in Firstfield is built on the same hill. Here it is on its own, untouched. Not worn in the middle like the meeting house steps. Whole. Humming very faintly, like the dome at night.

I don't have to read it. It's mine. It's Davin's under the wild. It's what they took out of Lenna and Joss and Hollis Fenn and One to Four and Seven, a little at a time, until there was no use left in them.

And it was here. All along.

I stand in the middle of the hall with my hand still smelling of the crock, and I understand something so simple I nearly sit down on the floor.

There's no place in here for anybody to lie.

There's a place for a reader. I can feel it, a warm worn hollow near the floor like the dip in Gran's chair, the right shape for someone to sit and look from one book to another. Made for two. But no bed. No white room. No board. Nothing in here takes from anybody. Whoever built this built it so that nobody would ever have to be the clean copy. So a boy from a village could be a boy from a village, and keep the old ways because he liked them, and go home for supper.

If anyone had come and read with it, the bell would never have rung for Lenna Brook. There'd be no plaques. There'd be no four drilled holes, with the dust still fresh, for mine.

Yes, says the voice, all round me, very quietly. That's what we were for.

◆ ◆ ◆

I come out like the pond spitting me up.

Full dark, my teeth knocking, my wrist lit so bright the stalks throw shadows. Odile's coat is over me. Sedge is holding my hand in both of hers and rubbing it as if she can start a fire in it.

"You were gone an hour," she says. "You stopped breathing properly—"

"Eat," says Odile, and puts a cracker in my mouth, and another.

And under the cold and the hunger there's the other thing, the thing that comes back with you. With Davin it was a wanting to be high, and a flinch at grey.

This is stillness.

Water with nothing moving in it. Reeds growing up and dying back and growing up. Dusk after dusk, two hundred years of them, with nobody coming down the bank. Past sad. The way a held breath stops being a breath and becomes just how you are.

It's mine now. I don't think it's going to go.

Sorry, says the voice. I didn't know you'd get that bit.

"I wanted to know."

So I tell Odile: the hall, the grain the way it was, the crock, the clean floor. No bed. For once she doesn't write it down.

"Seven," she says at last.

"Seven."

"And one sleeps in the Pale."

South, says the voice. Not far. There's an old station. It tastes like me with my eyes shut.

"South," I say. "Not far."

Odile looks south into the dark, past the black ribs. Then, for some reason, at her own hands, lit blue-green at the nails, as if she's never seen them before.

"Then we'll walk at night," she says. "It's cooler."

◆ ◆ ◆

It takes until the middle of the night. Hask leads. Odile walks with her hand under my elbow, which I'd mind if I could stand without it. The old machine comes after us a little way, fifty-one, fifty-one, then stops on the rise behind, its two dim eyes watching us go.

The grain thins and goes half wild. Then grey grass, and the ground slopes down in a long slow curve to where a river used to be, before it moved and left a bend of itself behind.

The station on the rise above it isn't like the others. It's round, not square, low, of a stone that looks grown more than cut, the colour of old bone in the starlight. No blue light. Bare mast. Door chained.

Below it, the fence: old wire rusted to lace between posts that lean every way, like Saul's after a storm. On the gate an enamel plate, most of its letters gone. I can make out —RCHIV— and NO ENTRY. Odile stops in front of it and reads it for longer than there is to read, and takes out her slate, and stands there in the dark not writing anything.

Inside the fence, the pond. Crescent-shaped, the old river bend, scummed green-grey, with a cracked dry rim where it's been shrinking for years. Dead leaves. The smell of a ditch in late summer.

"It's dead," says Sedge softly.

All the way round its edge, there are reeds.

I hold my hand out over them, the way I did on the first morning with the clicking coming down the ridge. And the reeds lean away from my warmth. All of them. The grass at my feet leans in, the way grass does, and the reeds lean out.

"It's not dead," I say.

◆ ◆ ◆

The year the blight really came, I was just big enough to hold the candle.

Saul's east rows. The dome's sprinklers had stuck on for a week and everything was steaming, and then the brown came. Not a caterpillar hole. Not nearly blight. Real blight, spots that spread and joined up overnight like ink on wet paper, and smelled like a cellar that wasn't happy at all.

Elder Marrow said, "Pull it, Saul. Burn it before it takes the row." Ma said it. Even Gran.

Saul didn't pull it.

It was his oldest plant, at the very end of the east rows by the wall, and every evening for two weeks he went out to it with a little knife and a bucket and a jar of vinegar and me. I held the candle. He cut off every brown leaf, one at a time, and wiped the knife in the vinegar between every cut so the brown wouldn't ride the blade to the next leaf. The vinegar stung my eyes. Wax ran down onto my fingers, and I didn't say anything, because Saul wasn't saying anything either.

It lost every leaf but the top four.

It made one tomato. Late, small, split down one side, the ugliest tomato I ever saw. Saul cut it in half and gave me half. It was warm from the sun and so sharp it made my teeth ache, with a grain of salt on it from his fingers.

"Everyone looked once," said Saul, with his mouth full.

Then he scraped the seeds out of his half onto a scrap of paper, careful as anything, folded it into a twist, and put it in his waistcoat pocket.

"Not dead," he said. "Only nearly."

◆ ◆ ◆

I climb the fence. It's mostly holes.

The mud at the edge is cracked into plates, then soft, then water, warm on top and cold underneath, round my knees. I push the scum aside with my hands and it parts like skin on old custard, and underneath, the water's clear.

I kneel and drink, because I already know.

Stone. Faint mint. So cold it aches in my teeth.

"Bram," says Odile at the fence. Not come back. Just my name.

"It's all right."

I put my wrist in the water, the green-gold lines under the skin like leaf veins.

Hello, says the voice. Not to me. Out, into the water. And the water, very faintly, rings: a note like a wet finger round the rim of a glass, the note I used to hear on my own pond in the Spill at night and never knew what it was.

Nothing.

Hello. It's me. I've got one, look. He's got the old floor, and he says okay, and he asks good questions. Wake up.

The water rings again. In my jaw. In my teeth. In the soles of my feet.

And something rings back.

Deep in the middle of the crescent, a light. Faint. Green-gold, like the first moment of dawn through the reeds. It grows. It comes up through the clear water towards me, slow, unfolding, soft as a hand made of moss. It doesn't hurry. It has never had to hurry for anything.

Behind me, on the bank, Sedge takes a breath and doesn't let it out.

It touches my wrist.

And I feel two things talking. Not in words. Like two people on either side of the bedroom wall, late, when you're meant to be asleep: just the up and down of voices. One is mine. The other is slower, and older, and so sleepy it keeps stopping in the middle and starting again, the way Gran does dozing in her chair and still trying to finish a proverb.

It tastes me, the way mine did on the first morning. The old floor. The clean pages. The crock.

A warmth goes through the water like the sun coming up.

And then it lets go.

Gently. It doesn't go away; it only stops holding on, as if it's touched something it wanted very badly and found somebody's name already on it.

It can't, says the voice quietly. You're taken. You're mine and I'm yours, and a person can't hold two of us. It was made knowing that. A pause. It needs its own. Somebody to read with. Somebody who'll say yes.

"Who?"

I don't know. Somebody willing. Somebody who asks what they'd be.

The light hangs there under the water in front of my knees. And it doesn't sink. That's the thing. It doesn't go back to sleep. It stays, faint and green-gold, just under the surface, like a lamp turned low and left burning in a window.

Waiting. But not the way it was waiting before. The other way. The way you wait when you've heard feet on the path.

The reeds lean away from me all round the pond, and the grass leans in, and the stars sit on the scum like dust.

And in my head, so faint I nearly miss it, a voice that isn't mine. Slow. Sleepy. Old as the floor.

Who's that?

I open my mouth to answer. Bram, son of Wenna, of the house of Hale. I know how now.

But it isn't asking me.

The light under the water has turned. Just a little. The way the first flower turned on my first night out, before the one beside it turned too.

Towards the bank, where the others are standing.
