Chapter Fourteen: No Reader on Record

REGISTER WORKS. Notice of decommissioning under Revision 33. Archival wetland AW-3, Pale sector 11. No reader on record in sixty years. Status: LAPSED. Asset retained in situ: do not drain (cost not justified). No entry.

— tin tag wired to the chain on a station door, Pale sector 11; the wire has rusted through on one side, so that the tag hangs by a corner and turns in the wind

I didn't hear it ask. I want that clear, because I put nearly everything else about that night and the day after on paper, and that part I have only on Bram's word.

What I saw from the bank was this. The light came up through the clear water to his wrist like a fish rising to a hand, touched, and let go. Then, instead of sinking, it stayed: a lamp turned low, a hand's depth under the surface, the faint green-gold of the lines in his skin. Bram knelt in the pond to his chest with his head on one side, the way you listen to a voice in another room. Then he turned and looked at us.

"It wants to know who's there."

Behind us the Pale hissed in the dark, rising and falling with the wind like breath.

Hask answered first. Someone had asked a question, and it is his nature to supply the record.

"I am Hask," he said, into the ground, so that the water round Bram shivered in rings. "Proxy of the Orrun, under mandate, three hundred and eleven years. Bearer: the Examiner."

The light came along under the surface towards the bank, slow as a cloud's shadow over a field, and lay against his bottom stone a while. Then it drew back.

"It says you're very interesting," Bram reported, "and there's nothing in you to read."

"That is correct," said Hask, not at all offended. "I am carved."

Then it came to me.

I stood with my boots in the cracked mud, the launcher I'd taken off Pell-22 slung on my back, because it was evidence and because nobody had asked for it back. The light came in under the water at my feet and rested there. I felt nothing. That is the honest account. Only the mint coming up off the water, cold in the nose, and a feeling I have had at a few doors in my life, of being looked over kindly by someone who has already decided.

It moved on.

I had told Teasel I was the plain kind, and he'd said there was no such thing. Standing there with my nails glowing and my jaw smelling of goat, I had to allow he had a point. But whatever the pond was looking for, it was not in my coat. I didn't mind. I minded a little.

It stopped at Sedge.

She had been standing a pace behind me with her arms folded, and when the light came and lay in the water at her bare toes she did not step back. It grew. A lamp turned up a quarter-turn, until the reeds at her feet were lit from beneath, every blade a line of green-gold fire.

"It's asking you," said Bram, very quietly.

"I know it's asking me. I'm not stupid." Her voice wasn't quite steady. She crouched, her long fingers hanging between her knees, and looked into the water a long time. Then she said to it, plainly, the way Highwater says it to anyone who comes up the ladder: "Who wrote you?"

Bram listened. I watched his face change.

"It says, Maud," he said. "It says she had ink on her hands, and she sang flat. And all the rest of them. On the hill." A pause. "It says nobody's asked it that before."

"Well, they should have." Sedge stood up. "Not in the dark. Mae says you never go in water you can't see the bottom of in the dark. Tell it in the morning."

"It heard you."

"Then it can wait," said Sedge, and sat down with her back against the fence post nearest the water. "It's good at that, apparently."

◆ ◆ ◆

Bram slept the moment he came out, folded up in my coat on the bank with Biscuit in the crook of his knees, his wrist still lit. Hask lowered himself into the mud beside him like a wall going up round a garden.

I took the watch. Sedge didn't sleep either.

The Pale at night is not dark. The stars come down to the ground on every side, and the grain takes their light and gives it back silver, so the whole plain lies faintly lit like a sea under a moon that isn't up. I went up to the station and found the door chained, a tin tag turning on a wire, and under my boots, set into the doorstep, a disc of brass the size of my palm, green with age, worn smooth in the middle by feet.

I put my nails to it to read it. Engraved, in tiny careful capitals:

STATION OF THE FIFTH POND. FROM THE LANDING STONE, 131 DEGREES 40 MINUTES, 9,216 CHAINS. M.F.

I sat down on the step and laughed, very quietly. You can go a whole season on a new world without meeting anyone who speaks your language, and then you find a benchmark in a step: someone two hundred years dead, telling you exactly where you are and how she knew.

When I went back down, Sedge was where I'd left her, chin on her knees.

"Examiner. If I say yes to it. What am I on your slate?"

"A person," I said. "Same as yesterday. Sedge, of Highwater. Maker and line: us."

"And on theirs?"

I could have said I didn't know. In that light, with her looking at me like that, I didn't think I'd get away with it.

"On theirs you'd be what he is," I said, with my chin at the sleeping boy. "Something with the old book in it. Or the key to the old book. Something they'd want to own."

"Materials," said Sedge.

I didn't answer, which was an answer.

She turned her wrist over in the starlight: the long fingers, the rope calluses on the tips.

"They wanted Wren," she said, "for a minute and a half." Then she put her chin back on her knees and watched the pond, and didn't say anything else till dawn.

◆ ◆ ◆

Dawn in the Pale comes up along the whole rim of the world at once, and the first thing it shows you is the wet.

The grain was soaked to the knee with dew. The stalks had stopped hissing and were only whispering, heavy-headed, each bowed under a row of beads. Over the oxbow lay a skin of mist no thicker than a sheet, white as milk, the reeds standing up through it and the station above it the colour of bone, and the whole crescent smelled of stone and mint and wet straw.

Sedge took off her boots and her outer shirt and gave me her knife.

"Hold that. If I don't come up, don't come in after me. You swim like a wet dog."

"Wet dogs hold."

"Not in mud." She looked at Bram, sitting up grey-faced in my coat with Biscuit squinting out of the collar. "You. Tell me what it says. All of it. Not just the nice bits."

"I don't get to choose the bits," said Bram, and coughed. "It says them."

She went in. The mud took her to the ankle, then the knee, then she was through the mist to her waist in clear water, the white closing behind her like a curtain, and the light came up to meet her, unfolding, soft as a hand made of moss. It touched her wrist. She flinched once and didn't pull away.

"It's freezing."

"It says sorry," said Bram. "It's been asleep."

"What'll I be?" said Sedge, to the water. "After. Tell me what I'll be."

Bram listened a long time. "It says: more. You'd be more, and it would be less alone." He swallowed. "It says it's not sure those are different things."

"That's not an answer."

"It says it's the only one it's got."

Sedge laughed. It came out of her before she meant it, a laugh that started before the joke had properly ended, and the light round her wrist brightened with it as if it had been waiting for that particular sound.

"Fine," she said. "Yes."

She took a breath that went all the way down, and went under.

◆ ◆ ◆

I counted. It is a Survey habit, and I'd have done it anyway.

At two hundred the light under the mist had grown to a smudge the size of a cartwheel. At three hundred I had my boots off.

"Don't," said Bram. "She's all right. It's reading. It reads slower than mine. It's old."

At three hundred and fifty I was in to my waist, and it was colder than he'd promised, the cold you feel in the roots of your teeth. Under my feet the bottom went soft and then went to stone, old fitted stone. Somebody had laid this pond a floor.

Behind me, Hask walked into the water, stone over stone, the mist parting round him, until it stood at his fourth.

"She is not the bearer," he said. "I have no mandate." He stopped beside me. "I shall stand here. It is not an act."

Four hundred. The light was spreading.

It went out from her in every direction at once, the way frost goes over a window, along the floor and up the far bank and round the long curve of the crescent, until the whole oxbow lay lit from underneath like a lamp with a great curved shade. The scum broke into plates and the plates sank, turning over as they went down through the clear water like leaves in autumn. The reeds round the rim, which had leaned away from my warmth all night, stood straight up, every one, with a long rustle that went round the bank like a congregation getting to its feet.

Then the note: a wet finger round the rim of a glass, but deeper, and under it a second note, Bram's, answering. I felt it in my shins. The water round Hask's stones trembled.

Up on the rise the old harvester lifted its head out of the grass, its two dim eyes brightening a shade, and made out of the round black horn on its front a very small sound: the pip it had made at Hask the day before. All the brown birds went up out of its tree at once.

Four hundred and ninety. My count had gone ragged. Five hundred.

Five hundred and twelve, and Sedge came up out of the middle of the light like something being born, and dragged in a breath that sounded as if it hurt.

◆ ◆ ◆

Hask got to her first: she came up an arm's length from his fourth stone and grabbed it and hung there, lips blue, teeth going like a mill wheel. Then he moved, very carefully, a stone's width at a time, towards the bank, so she could walk her hands down him like a ladder.

On the inside of her right wrist, faint as the veins in a leaf held up to the sun, the lines were drawn.

On the bank Bram put my coat round her, which left him shaking too, and the two of them sat in the mud with their shoulders together and their lit wrists in their laps and said nothing.

Then Sedge said, through her teeth, "It's been on its own so long."

Her face crumpled. She put her forehead on her knees and wept, not like a girl who is sad but like somebody who has been handed something very heavy without warning and is finding out what it weighs.

"I know," said Bram.

"You didn't say—"

"I didn't know how." He put an arm round her, awkwardly, across my coat. "It doesn't go. But it gets to be yours. Then it's not so much."

I looked away up the bank, to give them what privacy a pond has.

That was how I saw the drone.

◆ ◆ ◆

It came in low out of the sunrise, a tea tray with a blue light and VT on its belly. It didn't sweep. It came straight to the pond, as if something had told it where, and hung over the lit water at the height of a house, its blue light blinking at the two children on the bank.

It blinked for a long time. Then a hatch opened in its belly, and something grey and folded began to drop out on a line.

The launcher was off my back before I'd thought about it. A drone over water, against the sun, sliding in the wind: you lead it by half its own length and you squeeze, you don't pull. The launcher coughed. The net opened like a dandelion clock and took the drone round its rotors with its own net still half out of the hatch, and the bundle fell into the reeds with a sound like someone dropping a bag of spoons.

The launcher chimed.

RECOVERED MATERIALS: 1 (one) DRONE, survey, Varro-Tandem Bioreference. Ownership: Varro-Tandem Bioreference. Entered to company stock 6:14 am.

"It's recovered itself," I said.

Sedge looked at the drone buzzing feebly in two nets, and at me, and then she was laughing, helplessly, her nose running.

"Do it again."

Biscuit had already gone into the reeds. A small amber glow moved along the drone's flank. There was a sound of enthusiastic licking.

"Biscuit, no," said Bram, without hope. "That's oil."

Hask came up out of the pond with the water pouring off his stones and said, in the voice he keeps for matters of state, "Examiner. I must report a fourth leaf."

And there it was, at the joint between his seventh and eighth stones, beside the three that had wilted at their edges in the dry: a new one, pale, beaded with pond water, opening as we watched like a hand deciding to let go of something.

"It occurred in the water. I believe at about the time of the note." A grinding pause. "I shall have to amend the amendment to the amendment. My customs record is becoming longer than my mandate."

Sedge wiped her nose on my coat sleeve. "I watered it. Last night."

"I am aware." The top stone turned, very slightly, towards her. "I have been considering whether a gift must be declared."

"Must it?"

"I have not decided," said Hask, "and I have decided that I am in no hurry."

◆ ◆ ◆

There's no form for what Sedge had done. So I took a blank from the pad in my inside pocket, the paper ones that don't need charging, and wrote it the way Fallow taught me to write anything that might one day be argued over: plainly, with the time.

Pale sector 11, the Fifth Pond (Register: AW-3). Day 22, a little after dawn. SEDGE, of Highwater (PERSON), entered the pond of her own accord, having asked what she would be and been answered, and said yes. Witnessed: O. Pastern, Examiner; Bram Hale; Hask, Proxy.

I read it out to her. She held out her hand for the pen and signed. Sedge. Big letters, leaning backwards, as if they'd been pushed.

"What's that for?"

"So that if anyone ever says you were taken," I said, "there's a piece of paper that says you weren't."

She looked at the reeds, where the drone had stopped buzzing. "It saw me. Before you shot it."

"It blinked for a long time," I said. "Long enough."

I'd been doing sums since the blue light came over the bank, the way you can't help counting the steps on a stair. Yesterday Sedge had been a girl with a drawer of her own and a line that said us. Now she was the other half of a key to the oldest, cleanest copy of every line on Moriel, and somewhere in a grey office a drone's last picture was arriving on a desk.

1 (one) JUVENILE, unclassified. A minute and a half.

She wasn't going to be unclassified any more. I didn't know yet whether that would help her.

I folded the paper into the pocket over my heart, which is not a thing I say about paper often, and went up the rise to see about the door.

◆ ◆ ◆

The chain was newer than the door by a long way: grey iron as thick as my thumb, run through a staple set in a patch of cheap mortar, a padlock the size of a fist, and the tin tag. The door itself had no lock at all. It was a slab of the same grown stone as the walls, with reeds carved into it so shallowly you saw them only when the low sun came across, and a latch: a plain bar of brass worn bright in the middle, the kind you lift with one finger.

I showed the chain my warrant, which is what the law requires. General access, every door on these premises. The chain was not impressed.

"Examiner," said Hask. "May I?"

"Is it a harm?"

"I have looked. The door is not the bearer's, and the chain is not the founders'." He set his top stone against the staple. "And no one has been entombed in there. I should like to keep it so."

He leaned: three hundred and forty kilograms less four leaves, applied slowly. The mortar gave first, with a sound like a biscuit; then the staple came out all at once and the chain slithered down and lay in a heap across the brass disc on the step, as if ashamed.

I lifted the latch with one finger.

◆ ◆ ◆

Inside it was cool, the first cool since the orchards. And dust, like grey felt on everything, so fine that my first breath tasted of chalk and, under the chalk, something faintly sweet, like the inside of a very old book. A round room, a round window in the roof like an eye, grimed so that the morning came through it brown. And growing out of the bone-pale walls at shoulder height, all the way round: shelves, not cut or built but grown, curving, folded at their edges like the gills under a mushroom.

Bram stopped in the doorway.

"That's what it looks like," he said. "Inside. Last night." He put out a hand to the nearest shelf and didn't quite touch it.

In the middle, a desk of the same stone, a stool worn into a hollow, and on the desk, under a glass bell gone milky with age, a book.

I lifted the bell. Two hundred years of still air came out and smelled of ink.

Boards covered in plain cloth the colour of wheat. On the cover, pressed into the cloth: THE LIBRARY OF MORIEL. A Survey of its Seven Ponds, with their Bearings from the Landing Stone.

The hand was a surveyor's copperplate, upright, every figure set exactly in its column, and every so often, where the writer had been pleased with something, a long tail on a letter that she hadn't been able to help. There were maps in brown ink washed with colour that had held: seven ponds across the face of the world, each on its own page, each drawn with its reeds and, beside it, a little inked sun with the reeds leaning away from it. On every page a bearing and a distance from the Landing Stone in degrees and chains, and the same small M.F.

On the title page, in a corner, the brown print of an inky thumb.

On the next, a short preface. I will put it down exactly, because by the end of that day I had read it more times than the Manual:

These ponds keep the first copy of everything we brought, so that when it wears, as all copied things wear, it may be set right from the first copy and not from any living one. Let the village remember the old ways by choosing them. Let no child of ours ever be kept for a copy.

Maud Ferrier, surveyor, for the Library. Witnessed by all at the Landing.

I sat down on the stool, in the hollow somebody had worn in it, because my legs had decided for me.

◆ ◆ ◆

Each pond's page had a column ruled down its edge: Readers. On the Fifth Pond's, the first names were in the founders' hands, two and three to a year. Then the hands changed and the names thinned: one a year, one every few years, a child's spelling, a grandchild's. A gap of eleven years, and one more name in pencil, pressed hard. Then ruled lines, empty, to the foot of the page.

Every pond the same, thinning to the same blank. Nobody had stopped coming on purpose. They had come less, and less, and then they hadn't. On the last line of the First Pond's column, beside a name, somebody had written very small: came to look.

The Register said no reader on record in sixty years, and it was perfectly correct. I had the founders on my knee and the Register in my slate, and I knew it would cost me the morning.

"I need to sit," I said. "Properly."

Sedge, still in my coat, the dew drying in her hair, sat down across the doorway without being asked. "Nobody comes in. I know."

◆ ◆ ◆

Oak, iron-bound, beeswax and cold stone. My hand on it. In.

The hall of places is the widest in the Register, because the world is big. I went to a side bay near the floor, the kind of shelf you have to bend to read.

Seven drawers. AW-1 to AW-7. Archival wetland. Status: LAPSED. Reader on record: none.

Lapsed is the quietest discrepancy in the Manual. In the Reading it looks like this: beside each drawer, where a corridor should open to whatever the drawer is for, there is a doorway, and the doorway is bricked up. Old soft red brick, the mortar gone sandy so that it ran from under my thumb. Nobody had walled anything in with malice. You brick up a door when nobody uses the room behind it and the draught gets in.

Then I did a thing I had never done. I built a second hall out of the book on my knee.

Maud Ferrier had done half the work. Her hall was the bone-pale stone of the room I sat in, round-shelved and cool, smelling of ink and mint, its drawers lettered by hand: the First Pond. The Second. Seven, with her bearings on the fronts and her readers' columns down their sides, full at the top, thinning, empty.

Two halls side by side, with a wall between.

A join is the simplest thing the Register does: wherever a drawer here holds the same key as a drawer there, open a corridor. It does the rest itself, all at once.

The trouble is the key.

I tried the numbers first, because they were there. AW-3 to the Third Pond: a corridor opened, crooked, and at its far end Maud's drawer described a pond on a hillside in the cloud, while the drawer I'd started from sat in the Pale with its feet in an oxbow. The Register had numbered its wetlands in the order it gave up on them; Maud had numbered hers in the order she dug them. I had joined two lists on a column that only looked alike, which is the oldest mistake there is, and the corridor shut with a sound like a drawer on a finger.

Then the places. Both halls knew where every pond was; that is what a survey is for. But the Register held grid references on the Tally's reckoning, metres from a datum at the Preserve, and Maud held degrees and chains from a stone at a landing site I couldn't have found on any map I owned. I laid one over the other and it was like two pages of the same story laid skew: every line there, none on top of its twin.

I stood between the halls with my thumbs on two drawers that were obviously the same pond and would not agree, and for a while I didn't know what time it was.

◆ ◆ ◆

My second Survey season, the world where Big Imre threw his coffee pot, we spent three days on the wrong side of a valley.

The settlers' charter map put their river four hundred metres west of where ours did, and the settlers were ready to fight us over it. Three days of measuring in the rain, and every time it came out the same.

On the fourth morning Big Imre came out of his tent with his coffee, looked at both maps, and put his thick finger on a hill.

"What's that stone?"

"The charter benchmark."

"And ours is on the ridge. Two stones. Two maps. Each one's right from its own stone." "Find two places both maps agree on, Pastern. Turn one map till they sit on each other, and slide it till they touch. The rest of the world comes with them."

We found a ford and a burial cairn. By noon the river was where everyone said it was, and nobody had to fight.

◆ ◆ ◆

Two places both maps agreed on.

The first I had sat beside in the dark with my thumb on it. STATION OF THE FIFTH POND. 131 DEGREES 40 MINUTES, 9,216 CHAINS. And my slate, as I sat there, had known exactly where that step was on the Tally's reckoning, as it always does.

The second I had filed myself.

Twelve days ago in the Spill, under an open case, I had entered into evidence seven tomato seedlings and a notched stick at the edge of a green-gold pond below a ravine, where the stream widens, with a grid reference to the metre, because the Manual requires it and because Fallow would have come back from the dead to ask. And there in Maud's hall was the First Pond: by the stream below the ravine, where it widens; Spill country.

Two stones.

I put my left hand on the drawer of the step and my right on the drawer of the seedlings, and I turned Maud's hall.

It turned like a heavy door on a good hinge. Four degrees and a third: the difference between north on a world you've just landed on and the north the Tally settled on a century later. Then I slid it, the width of the plain and a little more, until the step sat on the step and the pond on the pond.

The wall between the halls thinned like mist going off a field.

And the corridors opened. Not one at a time. All seven at once, the way the Register does a thing when you finally ask it properly: seven lit passages from seven bricked doors straight across to seven hand-lettered drawers, and in every doorway the soft old brick fell inwards with a sigh of sandy mortar and a smell, absurdly, of mint.

AW-3 to the Fifth Pond. AW-6 to the First. AW-1 to the one in the cloud. Seven for seven, each within a stride of where it ought to be, as if the founders had been waiting two hundred years for someone to stop measuring from the wrong stone.

I walked one. Of course I walked the First. It ran straight and certain, and at its end was Maud's drawer, its readers thinning to came to look, and beside it a corridor I had built myself twelve days ago, to a small drawer in the hall of evidence: seven red-stemmed seedlings, a notched stick, a reed stem with eight knots in it.

Last night, lying in the stubble under a spine with my coat over him, a boy had walked down a stair into a room like this one. He had come in through the blood and I through the paper, and here we both were, a night apart, in the same hall.

I stood in the corridor until my hands stopped shaking.

On each of the seven, on a paper form, because in the Reading paper is the only thing that weighs what it should: Discrepancy, class Lapsed. Archival wetland AW-, and the number. Identified with the Library of Moriel by the founders' survey (M. Ferrier), joined on position, two benchmarks. Proposed: REINSTATED. Examiner O. Pastern.

On AW-3, under Reader on record, I wrote a name, and ran a corridor from it across the great hall to a drawer that said SEDGE. Place: Highwater. Maker / line: us.

Then I went back to the oak door the long way, through every hall, so as not to leave by a window. It was so cold my palm stuck.

◆ ◆ ◆

I came out with the drawer slammed shut behind my eyes, the hollow under the ribs, the cold. It was noon. The brown light from the round window had crossed the floor and climbed the far wall and lay on the gill-shelves like honey.

Sedge took one look at me and pushed a fistful of raw grain into my hand, rubbed out of the husk between her palms, and two of my own crackers she'd found in my coat. I ate them all. The grain tasted of dust and sunlight and, very faintly, of bread.

On my slate, waiting:

Discrepancies AW-1 to AW-7 received. Class: LAPSED. Proposed status: REINSTATED. Reinstatement of a lapsed place requires confirmation by the Chief Examiner. STATUS: PENDING.

And under AW-3: Reader on record: SEDGE (PERSON). Entered Day 22.

I opened the book to the Fifth Pond, to the first empty line at the foot of the readers' column, and held out the pen.

Sedge looked at the column a long time: all those names, thinning, and the one in pencil pressed hard. Then she wrote her own under it, big, leaning backwards.

◆ ◆ ◆

Halloran called while I was still shaking, bacon frying in my ear.

"Pastern." Tock. "You're not at the Preserve."

"No, sir."

"Today was the day I was counting. I said I'd count every hour of it." Tock. "I've got a Chancery seal the size of a dinner plate asking why one of my Examiners has spent ten days on a closed case and run up a forged Index, a living cave, a populated coast and a schema proposal. And this morning I have seven more filings in my tray." A pause, frying. "You want me to confirm the reinstatement of a library."

"The founders' own survey, sir. Signed. I'm sitting next to it. The preface says, quote, let no child of ours ever be kept for a copy."

The frying went on a while without any shells in it.

"Varro-Tandem filed at ten to seven this morning," said Halloran, in a different voice. "Pale sector eleven, archival wetland AW-3 and contents. Contents, I'll read it: one (1) reader, juvenile, line unknown. Recovered materials, on the strength of a drone image. I take it you know the contents."

"She has a line, sir. It says us. And she's been reader on record since about eleven, with a corridor to a person's drawer."

"Four hours after their claim. So it's a contest: a claim on a thing against a person in a drawer. And the moment I confirm your seven, the Chancery sees seven ponds worth more than the Index, every one with a corridor running to some child." Tock. "You've put a price on them, Pastern."

"There was a price on them already, sir. Nobody had written it down."

Silence. Long enough for a man to eat a sunflower seed and decide something.

"Your ten days were up at dawn. I'm supposed to close this case at midnight and recall you." A rustle of paper, loud against the relay. A drawer opening and shutting. "I appear to have lost a form."

I waited.

"The recall form. I had it here. Thirty-one years in this chair and I've never lost a form in my life." Tock. "It'll take me a week to find it."

"Yes, sir."

"Don't thank me. If you thank me I'll find it this afternoon." The frying. "And Pastern. The ponds. Is it sound?"

I looked across the room at the thumbprint on the title page, and at a girl in the doorway with her chin on her knees and a lit wrist, looking out at the water.

"It's the soundest thing I've ever found, sir."

"Then bring it to me," said Halloran, and the line closed like a log settling in a grate.

◆ ◆ ◆

At the back of the book, after the seventh pond, was one more page that wasn't a survey at all.

A different hand: round and slow, the letters joined as if holding hands to cross a road. To know the night. Set down by Nan Tolley, who sowed the first of it here, learned off her da, who had it off his.

Take one beard off the ear and lay it in your palm and breathe on it. If it walks, the air will hold a cloud. At sundown look along the standing grain: if all the beards are turning the one way, like a clock wound backwards, the cloud is coming up off the low ground tonight, and by midnight it will sit on the hills and you will not see your own feet. Bring the sheep in. If the dew is heavy at sunrise, you may leave the gate open; the day will be fair. The grain is only telling you what it is about to drink.

Below, in Maud's copperplate, smaller: Tried. True. M.F.

I took the book out into the hot afternoon. The grain round the station had gone half wild over a century, patchy and uneven, and it stood to my waist hissing like the rest of the Pale. I picked an ear and pulled off one of the long stiff bristles, the beard, as long as my finger, with a kink near its foot like a bent knee, and laid it in my palm and breathed on it.

It walked.

It twitched and turned, the kink straightening and coiling at once, and it moved across my palm in little jerks on its own, like the hand of a clock that has decided to go somewhere, and fell off the edge into the grain.

"Do it again," said Sedge, behind me.

I did. She did. Bram came out squinting, Biscuit sulking in his shirt against the sun, and did it, and when it walked he laughed out loud for the first time since the ditch.

"It's drinking," he said. "Your breath. One side of it drinks and the other side doesn't, so it bends." He put his palm on a stalk. "The whole field's doing it. Slower. They're all a bit thirsty."

At sundown I stood at the edge of the wild grain with the book under my arm and looked along it, as Nan Tolley said, the light coming low and red across the Pale. Every beard on every ear was turning: slowly, the one way, a whole field of tiny hands going backwards round a clock. The hiss of the stalks had gone soft, the dry edge off it. The back of my throat tasted of wet straw.

South and east, past the end of the Pale, the ridges stood up purple. And on top of them, far off, a cluster of lights, tall and white and very fine, like needles stuck upright in a pincushion: the towers of the Upper House in the High Gardens, the last sun on their glass.

By midnight, if Nan Tolley was right, a cloud would come up off the low ground and sit on those hills, and nobody up there would see their own feet.

I put one ear of the wild grain in my coat pocket, in with the fungus dust and a moonpear stone and the paper with Sedge's yes on it. My coat had been getting heavier for twenty-two days.

◆ ◆ ◆

Pell-4 came down out of the evening in the one-seat skiff, the grey beetle-legged one with the blue nose light, and landed on the rise with a precision that suggested he'd been practising. He looked at the drone in two nets in the reeds, with an otter asleep on it.

He was as immaculate as ever, the small enamel 4 at his lapel, except that his left hand was still a little puffy from the bees, and he looked tired. I hadn't known they could.

"Examiner. Good evening. I've been sent with a message."

"Then deliver it."

"Presently." He set his ledger case on the step and squared it with the edge. "First I wish to defect."

Hask turned his top stone.

"I wish to be entered in the Register as a person." He said it quickly, the way you say a thing rehearsed on a long flight. "I understand from the company's complaints file that you did it this week for three hundred and three people, at about one a minute, on a slate with no backlight. I should like to be three hundred and four." He took out a folded printout. "I have been in company stock since Day 17, 9:14 am. One, Associate, grade 4. Twelve's launcher. Release requires disposal authorisation, and nobody is willing to sign my disposal, which on reflection I prefer, but it means that for five days I have been, in law, a crate." He held it out. "I should like to stop being a crate."

Sedge had come up behind me and was staring. "He wants a drawer?"

"I want a drawer," said Pell-4, with dignity. "I'm not particular."

I opened a new row in the hall of persons, because the Manual has always allowed it and nobody ever asks.

"Name."

"Pell. Just Pell."

"Place."

"Head office. No. Put here."

I put Pale sector 11. Then I read him the last slot, as I had to three hundred and three people the day before.

"Maker, slash, line. At Highwater they ask it another way. Who wrote you?"

"Oh, that's simple. The original licence was written from the original Associate, whose genome and memory were—" He stopped.

"Yes?"

"From the original," he said again, more slowly. "Whom I am. Obviously. Except that Seven would say he is, and Twelve would say he is, and Nineteen..." His stylus had come out of its own accord and hovered over his ledger. "If I write myself, I concede I was written from somebody, who must therefore be another copy, with a number, which I would have to enter, which would be Seven's, or Twelve's. And if I write one of theirs..." He looked at the stylus. "I can't write Seven's. I'd sooner be a crate."

"Put us," Sedge offered.

"Absolutely not."

"It works."

"You," said Pell-4, "don't have eleven of yourself filing against you over the coffee rota." He put the stylus away with great care, and I watched the company come back into him like water into a footprint. "I withdraw the application, Examiner. I'm so sorry to have taken your time. I'll remain in stock."

"The row's open," I said. "It'll stay open. It doesn't expire."

"That's very irritating of it," said Pell-4, and I thought for a moment he might say something else. Instead he looked past me at Sedge's right wrist, lit in the dusk, and the stylus came out again by itself and wrote one word on his ledger before he could stop it. I read it upside down. Materials.

He looked at it. He didn't cross it out. But he closed the ledger over it as if it had been rude.

"Nothing personal," he said, and it didn't sound like the motto. It sounded like an apology from a man who had never been taught one. "Everything documented. The message."

He set a small grey disc on the step beside the brass benchmark, touched it, and stepped back as if from a stove.

◆ ◆ ◆

The voice that came out of it was soft and pleased, the voice of a man who has never in his life had to raise it. I had never heard it before. I knew it at once, because behind me Bram went entirely still, and the six slits on his neck slammed shut.

"Examiner Pastern. Lisle Varro. Good evening.

"I've been reading you for ten days. You've cost my company a forged Index, a mineral claim, a coast, forty harvesters and, I'm told, the lead copy's ledger, to an otter. I'm not complaining. I've waited twenty years for someone to cost me something I could learn from.

"This morning one of my drones saw a pond light up in Pale sector eleven, and a child come out of it. This afternoon your Chief Examiner's tray filled with seven wetlands, and I have read the preface you quoted, and I should very much like to read the rest.

"Come to dinner. The Upper House, tonight, in the garden towers. A skiff will be at your station at moonrise. Bring the young lady from the pond, if she'll come; I'd like to meet the reader on record. The boy and the Proxy will be quite safe below the cloud. I give you my word on that, which you have no reason to value, and which I keep.

"I'm told I have a terrible sum. I've never pretended it was pleasant. But it balances, Examiner, and nobody has ever shown me another that does.

"If you have a better sum, I will fund it."

The disc clicked, and was quiet. On the far ridges the needle-fine lights of the towers had come on, one after another, very high and very clean.

"He means that," said Pell-4, picking up the disc. "That's the trouble with him." He went back to his skiff and sat in it with the canopy open. "I'm to wait and observe. I'm sorry. I'm told it's not personal."

◆ ◆ ◆

"No," said Bram.

He'd gone white under the dust, looking at the towers.

"He's the one. Through the door. In the coat. Draw from him. He said it like ordering lunch." He turned to Sedge. "You're not going up there. He asked for you. That's why—"

"I know why he asked for me," said Sedge.

She stood at the edge of the rise with her arms folded, the lit wrist tucked under her elbow.

"He asked for the pond. I'm the pond now. I said yes to it in front of you all, and she wrote it down." She looked at me. "If you go up there with a book and no pond, he'll say it's a nice book."

She was right. That was the worst of it: I'd been working it out since the disc clicked, the way you work a sum you don't like the answer to, and she'd got there first and said it plainer.

"You'd be a witness," I said. "Not evidence. You stand beside me and say what you said at dawn, and you don't say anything else."

"I can do that."

"You've never done it once in your life."

"I can start," said Sedge.

Bram wanted to argue; I watched him want it. Then he looked at her wrist and stopped, because he knew better than anyone alive what she'd said yes to, and that it had been hers to say.

"Hask and I wait below the cloud," he said at last. His voice was rough.

"Hask and you wait below the cloud. Hask, if it comes up thick tonight—"

"It will," said Hask. He had turned his whole stack to the low ground. "I can hear the wet in the earth, rising. The grain has been saying so since sundown, I think. I did not know grain could say anything." A grinding pause. "If it comes up thick, Examiner, I shall go up into it as far as stone can go, and wait at the top of that. And then I shall consider whether I may go further."

"That isn't in the Schedule, Hask."

"No," he said. "I have noticed a great many things are not."

◆ ◆ ◆

The moon came up out of the Pale like a lamp out of a well, and with it, out of the south, a long grey skiff with a blue nose light, bigger than any I'd seen and quieter, gliding in low over the grain.

I washed my face in the pond; the light under the water came a little towards my hands, curious, and went away again, polite. I checked my flask (full, scalding), my slate (cracked, dark), my warrant, my pad of paper forms, the survey wrapped in a square of sailcloth, and in the pocket over my heart, the paper with Sedge on it, leaning backwards.

Every beard on the wild grain was turned now, wound back as far as it would go. Down at the river's old bend beyond the pond, the first white was lifting off the earth, thin as breath on a glass.

Sedge came and stood beside me, her knife at her belt where I couldn't persuade her to leave it, her chin up.

"What do I call him?"

"Whatever he asks you to," I said. "Then watch what he does with his hands."

Behind us Bram stood with Hask, Biscuit on his head, his own wrist lit in the dark like a lamp left in a window. He didn't say goodbye. He said, "Look twice," to both of us, as if it were something he'd been given and had decided to lend.

The skiff settled on the rise, and its door opened on a little lit room of grey leather, smelling of cedar.

I had a better sum. It was under my arm, in a dead woman's beautiful hand, with her thumbprint on the front. And it was standing beside me in a borrowed coat, still dripping a little, with a light in its wrist and a knife at its belt.

We went up.
