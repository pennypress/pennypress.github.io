What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 12 of 24

Chapter Eleven: Apple, Crock, Gran, Tilly

← Chapter Ten: Population: NoneContentsChapter Twelve: Who Wrote You →

Every word in this book, and every book in the meeting house, is made of the same twenty-six letters. Learn them well, and nothing written can be kept from you.

— The Firstfield Heritage Primer, page one, "Our Letters"; copied out on slates by every child in their first week of school

"A what?" I say.

"A discrepancy," says the stone-man.

His voice doesn't come out of anywhere. There's no mouth. It comes up through the mud and the big tree's roots and the boards under me, and I hear it in my jaw before I hear it in my ears, low and slow, like the mill wheel turning over once in its sleep.

The children on his bottom stone have gone quiet. The crab on his shoulder lifts one claw, as if it's seconding him.

I've seen him twice now. Once from the sky, swinging between two grazers, when a pile of dark stones turned round to look at me and then stood up and became a roof. And once an hour ago, from a bridge, which is when I dropped the moonpears.

Close to, he's bigger. Eight stones, or nine, each as big as our kitchen table and carved all over in tiny close lines, like the Charter if the Charter went on for ever. There's lichen on one. There are three pale leaves coming out of a crack between two others, like a buttonhole, which is the most surprising thing about him, and he's got a lot of competition.

And I can't smell him.

I've smelled everything since the Hiveplains. I smell the woman beside me, her tea and the dried honey on her hands, and under them, wary and tired and steady, a smell like a lamp turned down low. I smell the children, and the crab, a bit. But the stone-man smells of wet rock, and salt, and the green of his leaves, and nothing else. No mood. Nothing to tell me if he's cross or kind.

He's the first person in a week I've had to just listen to.

Oh, says the voice behind my eyes, very quietly, as if it doesn't want him to hear. He isn't written. Not in anything I can read. He's carved.

"What's a discrepancy?" I say.

"A thing that is there," says the stone-man, "and is not what the record says." His top stone tilts a little, towards the woman. "The Examiner finds them. I keep the Examiner."

"From what?"

"From four thousand one hundred and twelve harms," he says. "The others are her own affair."

The woman drinks her tea.

◆ ◆ ◆

I should say how I got here.

After the reef, after the old woman on the landing said Ada Hale's chin, I opened my mouth to ask how she knew, and she put one dry finger on my lips. "Not now. You're grey. Eat something and sleep, and then we'll see whose you are." I lay down in a net to close my eyes for a minute and opened them on the next morning, with the whistles screaming grey boat, grey boat and Sedge shaking me by the foot.

So I spent yesterday up the trees, and watched a woman in a punt hang a man in grey from a fig tree by his own net, and the stones walk out of the sea. When she said my whole name, I ran. Then I watched her drink tea and do sums for three hours while six children climbed her stone-man, and I felt stupid. So I came down.

◆ ◆ ◆

"You were the roof," I say to the stone-man. "At the stream. In the Spill."

"I was." The top stone turns back to me. "Three grazers fell. Item two thousand and six."

"What's item two thousand and six?"

"Falling livestock."

The woman makes a small noise into her flask that might be a cough.

"I'm sorry," I say. "That was me. The flock went up because I grabbed two of them, and the weak ones couldn't keep up, and—" I stop. "Did they hurt you?"

"My third stone is cracked." He says it the way Saul says it rained. "It hears better now."

"You turned round," I say. "Before you stood up. You turned round to look at me."

A long grinding pause. A ripple goes out round his base.

"You were falling upwards," says the stone-man. "It is not enumerated. I wished to see it."

Nobody's ever said anything like that to me. I think he means it the way Tilly means it when she wants to see a thing again.

The girl with plum on her chin tugs at his carving. "Ask him. Ask him what the Shining is."

The stone-man goes still. Not stone-still; he's always that. Stiller.

"I was asked," he says to me, "and I could not answer. I was told I am from beyond the Shining. By a child, in a kitchen, under the dome. She had a spoon halfway to her mouth. I have been waiting to answer correctly."

A kitchen. Under the dome. A spoon halfway to her mouth, because she'd forgotten it was there.

"Was she eight?" I say. My voice has gone small.

"She was of that size," says the stone-man.

"That's Tilly," I say. "That's my sister."

I look at the woman. She's put her flask down.

"We came to your house," she says. "On my first morning. Your sister told me you weren't bad. You just wanted to know." A pause. "Your grandmother didn't look up. She was carving a little wooden bird."

Gran carves when she's waiting for something. Something goes up my throat and sticks there, and I have to look at the mud for a while.

When I can, I look up at the stone-man.

"It's the skywalks," I tell him. "The glass walks over the village, where the visitors go. In the morning they light up all along like a necklace. We call them the Shining. So beyond the Shining is everything that isn't Firstfield. It's here."

The stone-man doesn't move for so long that the crab gets bored and climbs down.

"Then I am from beyond the Shining," he says at last. "I was correct."

He turns his top stone down to the little girl, slowly, with enormous care, the way you'd turn a full jug.

"It is a walk made of glass," he tells her, "where the people go who are looking."

She thinks about this. Then she says, "I knew that," and climbs up onto his shoulder ledge where the crab was.

He's happy, says the voice, puzzled. I can't smell it. I can just tell.

So can I. Something in the boards, in my jaw: a tiny change in his hum, like a held note going up a hair.

"Hask," says the woman. "His name's Hask."

◆ ◆ ◆

Then she tells me what she is.

She doesn't make a speech of it. She turns her slate round on her knee and talks while the tide comes in under the platform, slow and brown, lifting the weed on the roots.

Her name is Odile Pastern. She's an Examiner of the Inspectorate of Discrepancies: when the great book of everything on Moriel says one thing and the world says another, she goes and looks. Before that she dug ditches on new worlds and got bitten by things.

"I'm not with the men in grey," she says. "I'm not with the Preserve. I can't make you go anywhere. I can't take you back by myself; I'd need a ruling, and I haven't made one. And I can't stop them coming for you by standing in front of you, though I'll do that as well."

"Then what can you do?"

"I can write things down," she says. "And when I write them down properly, they're true. That's all an Examiner is."

It's the silliest thing I've ever heard, and she doesn't say it like it's silly.

I breathe in. My nose does it without asking now. And under the tea and the honey and the salt I catch something that makes my gills flutter.

Hay. Wet wool. Honey gone a bit to vinegar. And under it, very faint, warm bread.

"You smell of my grazers," I say.

She touches her jaw, on the left side. There's nothing to see there. "One of them sat with me, by your pond. It wiped its face on me. I can't get it off."

"Are they all right?"

It's Hask who answers, in the voice that comes up through the boards.

"The Preserve sent eleven people with fire to burn your valley," he says. "The Examiner made your flock evidence. Evidence may not be destroyed, altered or cleared. The people with fire went home."

I look at her.

She's looking out over the water as though none of this has anything to do with her. But it comes off her, faint and warm, under the lamp-turned-down: something embarrassed. Like Ma when you catch her singing.

"You wrote them down," I say.

"Sixty-three grazers," she says. "Under one number. It was quicker."

Biscuit, who has been circling her flask for some time, puts its whole face in the steam, sneezes, and goes on smelling it with an expression of great happiness.

"Thank you," I say.

"Don't." She sounds cross. She doesn't smell cross. "You haven't heard the rest."

◆ ◆ ◆

She turns the slate round again.

There's a picture on it of a boy with a village haircut and a clean neck, looking at whoever made the picture like they were a dog he wasn't sure of. The white room under the Upper House, the spring I was called. I remember the man behind the box saying chin up, and me thinking I didn't know it was down.

Under the picture there are words.

REFERENCE 12 · HALE, BRAM · MORIEL HERITAGE PRESERVE STATUS: RETIRED.

And nothing after. A line, and then nothing, like the bottom of a page somebody tore out.

"That went in more than a week ago," says Odile. "Eleven hours after I opened a case on you. Someone upstairs entered it, and the Register agreed with them."

I know that word. I've known it all my life. It's on the eleven brass plaques above the elders' bench, polished like coins, after the second date on every one. Called. Retired. The work is holy and it takes the rest of your life, and when it's done, you're retired.

"But it means honoured," I say. "It means you did the work, and you rest."

"In the Register," says Odile carefully, "it means there's no next line. Nobody's responsible for you. Nobody has to know where you are."

"It means," says a voice behind us, "they put you out where things eat you."

◆ ◆ ◆

The old woman is standing in the doorway.

I didn't hear her come. I didn't smell her either, and that frightens me more than anything since the storm, until I see why. She's leaning against the woven wall, and the wall has had her. Her arm and shoulder and the side of her face have gone the grey-brown of the wicker, slow and patchy, like ink soaking up through blotting paper. Even her smell's gone quiet, the way a moth goes quiet on bark.

She steps off the wall, and the colour drains out of her a little at a time, until she's brown as a nut again. She lowers herself onto the boards beside me, one hand on her stick and one on my shoulder, with a creak in both knees I can hear in my jaw.

She smells of woodsmoke, and salt, and, very faint under everything, of something sharp and green that I last smelled on my own wrist.

Oh, says the voice, very softly. There's somebody in her.

"You've met my lodger," the old woman says, though she can't have heard it. Maybe she just saw my face. "Don't mind it. It's nosy, but it means well."

"Who are you?"

She looks at me sideways with her good eye. "You know how to ask that better than that, Bram Hale. Ada would skin you."

My face goes hot.

"Bram," I say. "Son of Wenna, of the house of Hale. Grandson of Ada Hale." I swallow. "Whose are you?"

She smiles. It goes all the way up her face and makes the clouded eye crinkle along with the good one.

"Lenna," she says. "Daughter of Sal, of the house of Brook. Reference Number Five of Firstfield." She tips her head at the slate on Odile's knee as if it's a dog that once bit her. "Retired."

◆ ◆ ◆

Number Five.

I know where it is on the wall. Fifth from the left. Two dates. No name; none of them have names. I used to wonder why, during the long prayers.

"You're dead," I say. It comes out stupid.

"I am seventy-seven," says Lenna Brook, "and I have never once been dead. I've been retired for sixty-two years. It's very restful. I recommend it." She pats my knee. "You've got her chin. And her way of looking at a thing as if it owes her money. Is she well?"

"Her chest's bad."

"It always was. She used to wheeze up the meeting house steps and blame the steps." Her good eye goes far away, past the drowned trees. "We were girls together. You couldn't put a knife between us. We stole bread off the bakehouse sill and blamed the starlings. There weren't any starlings."

"There still aren't."

"Good," she says. "Some things ought to keep."

"Do you know Gran's sayings?"

"Do I know—" Lenna laughs, the creaky gate kicked open. "Ada got every one of them off my mam. Go on. Try me."

"Look twice."

"Saul's," she says, without blinking. "His da said Look twice and spit once. Saul dropped the spit when he started courting."

I laugh. I can't help it. Saul, with his stick and his tomatoes, spitting.

"The root doesn't see the fruit," I say slowly, "and the fruit forgets the root, and the tree goes on anyway."

Lenna nods along, as if it's a song, and when I stop, she goes on.

"And the seed remembers both."

I stare at her.

"Ada always left that line off," she says. "Said it was too pleased with itself." She shrugs. "It is, a bit. My mam liked it."

My hand has gone to my pocket without asking. Saul's twist is in there, soft as cloth, weighing nothing. I can feel the last few seeds through the paper, small and hard and quiet.

The tide comes in under the platform, and lifts the weed on the roots, and lets it go.

◆ ◆ ◆

She takes me up to see the others.

Odile comes too, because Lenna says, "You'd better. You'll want to write them down, and they'll want to watch you do it." Hask stays at the foot of the big tree, explaining the Shining to a new lot of children who have come specially. As we climb I hear him through the trunk. He's added a bit. It shines in the morning.

The graves are on a tree on its own at the edge of the village, reached by a bridge that's one rope to walk on and one to hold. Lenna crosses with her stick tucked under her arm like an umbrella. Odile crosses like someone who's done worse. I do the middle bit with my eyes shut.

On the other side, a platform open to the sky, the breeze smelling of salt and, up here, something cleaner, like rain that hasn't landed yet. Five long boxes of earth, each the length of a person, planted thick with something low and silver-leaved that smells of thyme when the wind crushes it. At the head of each, a board with letters burned into it.

A woman is kneeling by the nearest, pulling weeds. Her hands are wrong: brown and ridged and hard all over, like the bark of the big tree, and they move slowly, the way old roots would move if roots moved. She closes them round a weed one finger at a time, and only then looks up.

"Hollis," says Lenna. "Ada's grandson. The Twelfth."

The woman wipes her bark hands on her apron, which does nothing at all, and holds one out. It's like shaking hands with a gate.

"Hollis," she says. "Daughter of Ruth, of the house of Fenn. Number Six."

"There's no house of Fenn," I say, before I can stop myself. "In Firstfield. There's not."

"There was," says Hollis Fenn, without any fuss, and goes back to her weeds.

I read the boards. One, Two, Three, Four, Seven. And under each number, burned in a careful slanting hand, a whole name: a first name, and whose, and what house.

I read all five out loud. I've delivered bread to every door in Firstfield, and I've never heard a single one of these names.

"How do you know who they were?" says Odile quietly. She's got her slate out, but she hasn't written anything.

"Firstfield's small," says Lenna. "I counted. And then I went and found them. One to Four I found as bones, where they leave you. I knew them by what they had. A ring. A boot. A tin whistle somebody's da had made." She leans on her stick. "Seven, I found alive. He lasted nine days. I'd been looking for him a year."

"Where they leave you," I say.

She tells me. Not long; she's not a woman who tells things long. When the board upstairs has drawn all it wants from you, you go to sleep in the white room, and you wake in the back of a grey skiff with no windows, and then on your back in the grass, far out past the ridges, where the heat-hunters go and nobody else does. No shoes. Nothing. The skiff lifts off, and the grass leans in towards you, whispering warm here, and you've no idea yet what that means.

"Retired," says Lenna. "Honoured. At rest."

The wind crushes the silver plants, and the platform smells of thyme.

I think of the brass plaques, polished like coins, and the four little holes drilled in the wall for mine. I think of the grass in the Spill, leaning in.

"I ran before they'd finished with me," says Lenna. "Out of the white room with a lodger I never asked for and a coat off a hook. After that, whenever the bell rang a Calling, I'd count off the years they usually keep a Reference, and go and wait where they leave you. You can hear the bell from the ridges, if the wind's right."

"How many did you get?" I say.

"Four," says Lenna Brook. "And one I can't name."

◆ ◆ ◆

The others come one at a time.

Mae Cotter comes up the trunk without a ladder, wet from the channels, silver scales from her wrists to her elbows flashing like a fish turning. She taught Sedge to dive. She says Mae, daughter of Jen, house of Cotter, Number Ten with water running off her nose, and when I say there's a Jen Cotter who does the mill's accounts with Ma, her face goes very still and then very bright.

"Does she still sing when she adds up?"

"All the time," I say. "Ma says it's why the books never balance."

Mae laughs and has to sit down.

Pim Ashby doesn't come up. When Lenna lifts the blanket over his door he flinches back from the afternoon as if it's shouting, his eyes huge and gold, the black in the middle gone small as a seed.

"Look twice," Pim says to me from the shadows, when Lenna tells him who I am. And then, as if he's checking: "And once in the dark."

"Saul just says look twice."

"Saul's young," says Pim. He's at least fifty.

And Joss Tanner is in a hammock on the platform below, wrapped in two blankets in all this warmth, with a cough that comes up from the bottom of him like a bucket from a well. He's the youngest. Ten years ago he was called. He hasn't changed much outside, Lenna says. They took the most from him.

He looks at me and his face does a slow, astonished thing.

"You're the Hale boy," he says. "You fell in the flour at the bakehouse. Your gran fished you out by the ankles and you came up white all over like a ghost, and sneezed."

I don't remember that at all. I believe it entirely.

"Does my mam still do the eggs?" says Joss.

"Thursdays," I say. "On the bakehouse step. She keeps the brown ones for Gran."

Joss closes his eyes. Then he coughs, a long one, and I cough too, my dry gill-ache, the paper crackle, and we sit there coughing at each other like two old men outside the meeting house, and Sedge, who has turned up from nowhere as usual, says, "Oh, wonderful, there's two of you," and Joss laughs so hard he has to be sat up.

Odile writes their names down. All of them. Slowly, the way Master Aldous writes the date on the board, so you can see each letter go on. Each of them watches her do it.

◆ ◆ ◆

Back on the graves platform, with the sun going down orange behind the orchard and the tide nearly in, Lenna asks me.

"When you went. Did Ada say anything?"

And I'm back in the kitchen.

Three in the morning. My coat on, bare feet on the cold stairs because shoes make noise. The dome's night-blue at the window, soft as the inside of a mussel shell. The stove dead, smelling of old ash and cold iron. The mother crock in its dark corner under the stairs, sour and cool, breathing to itself.

And Gran in her chair, in her shawl, awake.

She wasn't surprised to see me. I thought at the time she'd heard me on the stairs. But she was sitting very straight, the way she sits for a Calling, and her hands were already in her shawl, already holding the twist. Already twisted. Saul's seeds, counted out and folded, waiting. She'd have had to go down the east rows for them in daylight, after the Calling, with everyone still cheering.

In case wherever you're going hasn't got proper ones.

The root doesn't see the fruit, and the fruit forgets the root, and the tree goes on anyway.

And then she closed her eyes and said the last thing in a different voice, younger somehow, the way you say something you learned off by heart a long time ago.

"She said, Go and see, then," I tell Lenna. "Somebody in this family ought to."

Lenna Brook doesn't say anything at all.

She sits down. Not on purpose; her knees just go, and she's sitting on the edge of a grave-box with her stick across her lap, and her skin, all down the side touching the board, goes slowly the colour of the burned wood. Hollis stops weeding.

"I said that," says Lenna at last. "On the stair, the morning I went up. Ada was crying, and I didn't want her to see me cry back, so I said, Go and see, then. Somebody ought to. As if it was an adventure." She laughs, but not like a gate. "I was fifteen. I was being brave."

The sea comes in under the trees, glowing a little now where it moves.

"Sixty-two years," says Lenna, "and she's had it in her mouth the whole time, the old crow."

◆ ◆ ◆

"There's the one I can't name," says Lenna, when she's stood up and her skin's come back. "You'd better see him before dark. He doesn't come in."

We go out to the edge of the orchard, where the trees thin and the wind comes straight in off the reef. Odile comes. Sedge comes, which nobody asks her to and nobody tells her not to, with Biscuit on her shoulder, because Biscuit has decided Sedge is the one with the plums. Hask wades the channel below us with his seventh stone above the water.

Lenna talks as we go, quick and low. They found him eleven years ago on the mudflats, eating crabs raw. One of theirs, her lodger said, from the way he was made under the changes. But the wild had been at him for years. Things out there swap words like children swap marbles, and he'd been left in the middle of them with nothing.

"He talks," says Sedge. "A bit."

"He talks," says Lenna. "He doesn't answer. Ask him his name, he says up. Ask him whose he is, he looks at you as if you've asked a tree." She stops at the foot of the tallest tree at the edge. "Pim's Eight, so he's Nine, unless I've counted wrong. I've told him every family name I've got. None of them took."

The tree's roots stand out in the water like the legs of something about to walk off. Up at the very top, where the branches are thin enough to sway, there's a dark bundle wedged in a fork.

"He likes it high," says Lenna.

I climb, better than on the first day. Sedge goes up round me and says so. Lenna takes a rope ladder at the speed of a woman who's climbed this tree for eleven years, and Odile comes behind her. Hask stays at the bottom in the rising tide, like a doorstop for the whole tree.

At the top, the wind smells of the open sea, cold and huge, and the crown sways in a slow circle, and I can see the Knuckle and the long new gold of the Mouth, and beyond it the sea going out to the edge of everything.

He's asleep along a branch as thick as my waist, one arm hanging, his cheek on the bark. Long and lean, not much of him that isn't muscle and bone. His skin is mottled green-brown and grey in patches the size of leaves, and as the branches move and the light comes and goes, the patches shift, very slightly, to match. His nails are thick and dark, like Biscuit's claws. He smells of salt and crab and green bark, and of a hundred things I can't put names to, layered up like the smells in an old coat.

There's somebody under there, says the voice. I can almost taste it from here.

"Can you read him?" I say, very quietly.

If you touch him. If she lets you. If he lets you. A pause. It'll cost. More than the reef. Reading's dearer than speaking.

Lenna's on the branch below, hanging off it by one arm the way Sedge hangs off things, watching me with her good eye very narrow.

"My lodger says yours could do it," she says. "It's been saying so since you came. It says yours is a better reader." A dry little smile. "It says it a bit sulkily."

"Can I?"

Lenna looks at the sleeping man a long time. "I've told him every name I've got," she says. "Go on. Ask him. If he says yes, it's yes."

So I lean out along the branch, and the sleeping man opens his eyes.

They're grey. Ordinary grey, like rain on the dome. The only ordinary thing about him.

He lifts his head the way a dog does and breathes in at my wrist, where the green-gold lines are. Once. Twice.

Then he puts out his long hand, slowly, and lays it in mine, palm up, like a child giving you a stone it found. And closes his eyes again.

That's yes, says the voice.

"That's yes," says Lenna.

His hand is cool and hard-skinned, and under the hard skin there's a pulse, slow and steady as the tide.

Name it, says the voice. Name what you want to see, so I can hold it.

"Him," I say. "All the way in."

◆ ◆ ◆

It's like going down into the pond.

The light changes first. The sunset goes, and the wind, and the swaying, and the smell of the sea, and I'm sinking through something warm and lit and thick, like honey with a lamp in it. Then there's a wall in front of me, soft and wet and moving all over like the skin on hot milk, and I go through it, the way you go through a soap bubble if you're careful.

And I'm in a city.

It's huge, and lit, and busy. Long thin roads cross it every way, stretched tight as rope, and things walk along them hauling loads bigger than themselves, step, step, like porters on the long stair. Doors in the walls open for one kind of thing and not another, snap, snap, like the bakehouse shutters on a windy morning. Everything is talking: little puffs of taste, more of this here, less of that there, pass it on, like the whistles at Highwater.

One cell, says the voice, calm, the way Ma sounds in a bee cloud. Only one. He's got more of these than there are stars you saw. They're all like this.

There's a roar.

Off to one side, a whole street of long bean-shaped things with folds inside like the gills of a mushroom, and they're roaring. Hot as the bakehouse oven when the door's open at five in the morning and you have to stand back. Everything that walks past them comes away carrying a little of the heat.

They burn the food, says the voice. That's where his warm comes from. Yours too. They were something else once, a very long time ago, a small thing living on its own. Now they live in here and pay the rent in heat.

Inside each roaring thing there's a tiny loop of something, round as a ring.

Their own writing. They brought it with them. It comes down from mothers. Only mothers.

Somewhere in Firstfield, a long time ago, a woman I don't know gave a baby this. It's still here. It's still roaring.

◆ ◆ ◆

In the middle of the city there's a wall inside the wall, and through a door in it, the library.

I don't know what else to call it. It's quiet in here after the oven street, the way the meeting house is quiet after the square. And it's full of thread. Miles of it, wound on spools like Ma winds wool on her bobbins, the spools packed into bundles and the bundles packed into shapes, all of it so fine that if I unwound it I could go from Firstfield to the sea and back.

And it's written on. All along the thread, close as the carving on Hask, there are letters.

Read it, says the voice.

"I can't."

You can. There are only four. It sounds like it's smiling. Name them, and you can hold them.

So I look at the four of them for a long time, the way I looked at the Primer the first week of school, with my slate on my knee and Master Aldous going round behind us.

There's a round one, plump and sweet, that makes me think of the first page. A is for Apple. So that's Apple.

There's one that tastes sour and cool, like something alive breathing in the dark under the stairs. Crock.

There's one that's steady and a bit stern and always holds on to the Crock, wherever the Crock is, across the thread. Gran. Gran's always by the crock.

And there's a quick small one that never lets go of Apple. Not ever. Every Apple on one side, a Tilly on the other, holding hands.

Apple, Crock, Gran, Tilly.

I laugh, there in the library in the middle of a man I don't know, and the sound goes all down the thread.

Good, says the voice. Now read.

It's slow at first, like the Primer was. The letters go in threes, and every three is a word. Small busy machines copy a stretch and carry it out into the city, where other machines read it three at a time and build what it says: long chains, clicking together bead by bead. Then the chains fold. All by themselves, over and under, tucking and pleating, the way Gran folds a sheet off the line, corner to corner, shake it, corner to corner again, until it's a small tight square with a shape all its own. And the shape does something. Carries. Opens a door. Holds a wall up.

The thread doesn't build the man. It builds the things that build the man.

I read faster. Apple, Gran, Crock. Tilly, Tilly, Apple. Words, and sentences, and long passages that build the roads and the doors and the oven-things, the skin, the hair, the grey of the eyes.

And then I see the other writing.

◆ ◆ ◆

It's all over.

Across the old letters, in among them, crammed into the margins, there's new writing. The same four letters, but in another hand, a hurried hand, a hand from somewhere else. Here a whole sentence has been copied in from something that lives on bark and changes colour, and that's the leaf-shade on his skin. Here's a passage from something with claws. Here, a stretch about drinking the sea and not dying. Here's one that's been copied over and over, a dozen times, like Tilly writing her name all down the edge of her slate.

Layer on layer. Some of it neat. Some of it smudged and crooked, a copy of a copy, the way the reef's word was crooked at the Mouth.

The wild did that, says the voice. Years of it. Things out there swap words. They got into him and left their writing.

I'm angry. I think of him on his back in the grass with no shoes, and every little thing in the Spill writing on him, because nobody was there to stop it.

But the more I read, the stranger it is.

Because the new writing isn't instead. It's over.

Like the big slate on the schoolroom wall, written on and wiped and written on again for a hundred years, so that when the light's low you can still see the ghost of old lessons under today's.

I tilt my head, the way you do at the slate.

And under all of it, line after line, there's the old text. The first hand. Steady and plain and careful, going on and on.

And I know it.

I know it the way you know your own handwriting. The way you know your own lane in the dark. Nearly all of it, Apple-Gran-Crock-Tilly, word after word, page after page, is the same as mine. Not like mine. The same. As if somebody took the book in me and the book in him off the same shelf.

Firstfield, says the voice, very quiet. This is what you taste like. Under all that, he tastes like you.

The new writing changes his skin, his nails, his eyes, what he can drink. It's very loud.

But it's on top. The book underneath is still the book.

◆ ◆ ◆

I want to fix it.

There's a passage deep in where the new writing's gone in crooked right across something that matters, something about the way the city in his head talks to itself, Tilly's name scrawled over the middle of the sentence, and I think: that's why he says up. That's why he can't answer.

"Can you rub that bit out?"

The voice is quiet a long moment.

I can only write in you, it says. You know that. And then, gentler than I've ever heard it: And he didn't give me that. He gave me his hand. He said I could look.

The city goes on round me, hauling, roaring, folding. It's been going on for more than forty years without me.

There, says the voice suddenly. Bram. Smell that.

◆ ◆ ◆

It's a passage I haven't read yet. First hand, hardly touched; the new writing has gone round it the way water goes round a stone. And when I read it, Apple, Crock, Crock, Gran, the city builds what it says, and what it says is a smell.

Not a smell for anything. A smell for him. The one under the salt and crab and bark, that says to anyone with the nose to read it: this is who I am, and this is whose.

Kin smell like kin, says the voice. It's written. It runs in families.

I read it.

And I'm on the bell tower.

Not really. It's just that the smell is so exactly the smell that the rest comes with it. The top of the bell tower, the summer Jory said bet you won't, when I went up to see if the sky got closer and then couldn't get down. Pigeon dust. Hot slate. The great bell beside me, cold even in summer, smelling of old iron. A rope coming up over the parapet, rough and tarry, and a big hand on it, and a big slow face with a beard like a hedge, puffing, and arms coming round me, and I'm carried down the inside of the tower one rung at a time with my face in a wool coat.

And the coat smells of this.

Tar, from the rope. Pipe smoke, gone stale. And under those, warm and close and a little sour, like green apples in a bowl: the smell of the man himself.

Elder Marrow.

Who reads the Calling letters in his big slow voice. Who climbs the bell tower once every thaw, on the same day, alone, and rings the bell once, just once, and comes down again, and nobody ever asks him why.

Everyone knows why.

◆ ◆ ◆

I come out like being pulled up out of the pond by my collar.

It's dark. The sun's gone, the wind's colder, and the sea below the tree is glowing green everywhere it moves. I've been gone a long time.

And I'm cold. Cold from the inside, as if every oven-thing in me has gone out at once. I'm shaking so hard my teeth sound like Saul's stick on the tomato frames. My gills are clamped shut. My wrist is burning green-gold, so bright the branch round me is lit, and Sedge's face, very close, and Odile's, with her slate held to her chest.

And I'm so hungry I could eat the tree.

"He's back," says Sedge. She's got both arms round me; I don't know when that happened. She smells of fright, the struck match, and she's very warm. "Lenna, he's freezing—"

"I know what he is." Lenna's already climbing up with something in her hand. "Eat."

A moonpear. I eat it in four bites, skin and all, and then another, and a lump of sticky brown stuff that tastes of figs and salt, shaking, with Sedge holding me on the branch. It's not nearly enough.

Sorry, says the voice. It sounds hollow, the way I feel. That was dear.

And under the cold and the hunger there's something else. Something that isn't mine.

A wanting to be higher. An ache in the hands for bark. A flinch, deep down, at the colour grey. A loneliness so big and old it doesn't even hurt any more; it's just weather. None of it comes with a picture. I don't know what happened to him out there, or for how long. The thread doesn't keep that.

It's only that I've been inside him, in the chemistry of him as he is right now, tonight, in his blood and his skin. And it rings in me afterwards, like a bell you've stood too close to.

He's awake. He's sitting a little way along the branch with his knees drawn up and his long hands round them, his skin gone dark grey and blue with the night, watching me with his rain-grey eyes as if I'm a bird that's done something unexpected.

I lean towards him and breathe in.

Salt. Crab. Bark. A hundred smells from a hundred things. I know them now; I've read them. I can take them off him one by one, like coats off the hooks in the meeting house porch.

And under them, warm and close and a little sour, like green apples in a bowl.

"Davin," I say.

He doesn't move.

"Your name's Davin," I say. My voice is shaking as much as the rest of me. "Davin Marrow. Son of Tobias Marrow, who's an elder. Of the house of Marrow, of Firstfield." I have to stop and get my breath. "Your da rings the bell for you. Every thaw. Once. He climbs all the way up to do it, and he's got a bad hip, and he does it anyway. Everyone knows. Nobody says."

The man on the branch looks at me for a long time.

Then he breathes in at his own wrist, slowly, the way he did at mine. As if he's smelling it for the first time.

On the branch below, Lenna Brook makes a noise I don't think she meant to make.

◆ ◆ ◆

Odile is writing.

She's writing by the light of her own fingernails, which glow blue-green in the dark the way the sea does. I can see her lips move.

"Reference Nine," she says under her breath. "I had you as a number." And something comes off her into the cold wind that I've never smelled on her before. Fierce. Bright. Like a match that's been struck and held, and not shaken out.

"Lenna. His father's first name. Is it right?"

"Tobias Marrow," says Lenna slowly. "Toby. Three years under me at school. He threw a frog at Ada once." She wipes her good eye with the back of her hand, roughly, as if it's done something to annoy her. "She threw it back."

From the bottom of the tree, far below in the glowing water, a voice comes up the trunk and into my jaw.

"Examiner. Is it a discrepancy?"

"Yes, Hask," says Odile. "It's a good one."

"I am glad," says Hask. A long, thoughtful pause. "The boy is shaking. Chilling of the body is enumerated."

"For the bearer, Hask. Not for him."

"I am aware," says Hask. "I wished it noted."

And Biscuit, who has slept through the whole thing in Sedge's collar, chooses this moment to wake up, smell the fig paste on my chin, and lick it off. Thoroughly. Including the inside of my nose.

Sedge laughs so hard she has to hold on to the branch with her feet.

◆ ◆ ◆

I can't climb down yet; my legs have gone to string. So we stay in the crown of the tall tree at the edge of everything. Sedge comes back up with blankets and a pot of something hot that tastes of fish and fennel, and I eat the whole pot.

The shaking goes down to a shiver. The shiver goes down to a hum.

Sedge sits next to me under one of the blankets. Not touching. Very nearly. If I breathe in I can smell her, wet wood and plums and the green-stick smell, and I don't, on purpose, because it seems rude. And then I do anyway.

"What was it like?" she says. "Inside."

"Like the bakehouse at five in the morning," I say. I think. "Like reading."

"I can't read," says Sedge.

"I'll teach you. There's only four letters."

"There's more than four, I've seen writing—"

"Not that kind."

She looks at me sideways under the blanket, and for once she doesn't say anything clever. Her mouth goes up at one corner and stays there.

On the branch below, Lenna and Odile are talking low, the way grown-ups talk once the children are meant to be asleep. I catch third drawer, and the Register can't hold him, and Odile saying I've got three days, and Lenna saying Then you'd better not waste any of them sleeping, girl, and Odile, surprised, laughing.

I tie my knot. The twentieth. My fingers still taste of salt, and something under the salt, like green apples.

Bram, says the voice.

"Mm."

Thank you for that. A pause. I haven't read anybody. Not like that. Not for a long time.

I want to ask what it means, a long time. Who it read before. But I'm warm now, and full, and the tree's swaying, and the hum in my jaw is going down like the dome's note at night.

I'm nearly asleep when I hear him.

"Davin," says Davin Marrow, to nobody. Rough. Slow. Trying it the way you try a new tooth with your tongue.

And then, after a long time, as if he's had to go a long way down to fetch it:

"Marrow's."

Lenna goes absolutely still. Odile's slate stops.

Nobody says anything. Nobody needs to.

I open my eyes to see his face.

And past his face, past the branch and the reef and the long gold line of the Mouth, out on the dark sea where there shouldn't be anything at all, there are lights.

Small ones. Low on the water. White, with a blue at the front, like a nose. One. Then another behind it. Then another.

I stop counting at six.

They're coming in on the tide, in a line, as neat as writing.
