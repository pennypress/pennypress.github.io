What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 22 of 24

Epilogue: Thursdays

← Chapter Twenty: Care OfContentsAppendix: A Map of the Going and Seeing →

PERSON · HALE, BRAM. Son of Wenna Hale. Grandson of Ada Hale. REFERENCE No. 12 (hall closed). CHOOSING: EXERCISED. Care of: O. Pastern, Examiner.

[in pencil, on the printout pinned over the Hale kitchen table, in a hand that presses very hard:] and Tillys

— Register of Moriel, schema revision 59; copy brought down the long stair by the keeper

The crock is used to me now. That's why I still feed it.

Gran says it'd be used to anybody by this time, a crock isn't fussy, but Gran is wrong, because when Mistress Thorne fed it the week I had the spots it went flat and grey and sulked for two days, and when I came back it bit me. Not really bit. You lift the cloth and it goes hah at you, sour and appley and so strong it gets you in the back of the nose, and that's how you know it's happy. If it doesn't bite you back, it's sulking. Gran says that too. Gran says most things first.

So every morning I stand on the stool and give it its flour and its water and stir it with the long spoon, because Bram took the pencil. He stirred it with a pencil the whole time, which is disgusting, and now he stirs crocks in space with it, probably. I asked. He said there's no crock on the ship. He said Odile's Hask won't eat bread anyway, being stones, and Odile only drinks tea out of a flask, and Biscuit eats everything including a whole bag of something called Tally crackers and then was sick in the Chief Examiner's tin.

That's the best thing he's told us so far.

◆ ◆ ◆

Thursday is tomatoes.

Saul shakes his tomatoes on Thursdays, every truss, tap tap with the end of his stick, so the flowers let go of their dust and make fruit. He's done it since before Ma was born. This summer his knee's bad, so he sits on an upturned bucket at the end of the row with his stick across his lap like a lord, and he says that one and that one and mind the leaf, and I do the tapping.

The three mounds in the end bed came up after all. They're my shoulder now. Bram put his hand on the dirt before he went and said they weren't dead, they were swelling, and Saul said not up, and Bram said only nearly. Saul tells everybody that. He tells it like it was his idea.

They smell green when you touch them. Not grass green. A sharp, furry, bitter green that stays on your fingers all day so you can smell your own hands at dinner and know where you've been. There's one tomato going red on the middle plant. It's been going red for a week. It's the colour of the inside of your eyelids when you look at the sun.

"Not yet," says Saul, before I've even asked.

"When?"

"When it's ready. Not when you are."

I tap the next truss. And then, because Bram's always going on about it now, in every relay, like he invented it, I look twice.

Under a leaf, at the bottom, nearly on the ground where nobody would look the first time, there's another one. Redder. All the way red. Warm when I put my palm round it, like a hen's egg.

"Saul," I say.

He leans and looks. He looks for a long time. Then he grunts, which is Saul for well I never.

"That one's ready," he says. "That one's been ready since Tuesday, I'd say." He sits back on his bucket. "Go on, then."

It comes off with a little snap and a puff of the green smell. I don't eat it. You'd think I would, but I don't. I hold it in two hands all the way up the lane like Bram holding the slate when the relay comes, as if it might spill.

◆ ◆ ◆

The far door's open. It's always open now. Nobody's shut it since the hearing, not even when it rained sideways and the meeting house got a puddle in it the shape of a goat. Bet says it's a goat. It's a puddle.

People come through it. That's new as well. They walk down the long path from the towers and through Saul's east rows, careful, in their soft shining shoes, and stand in the square and look at us the way we used to look at them through the Window, except now there's no glass, so they don't know where to put their faces. There's a museum in the stone, now, about what retired meant. They read it and go quiet. Then they come out and buy Mistress Thorne's bread, which is the right order to do it in.

Some of them come from much further than the towers. Yesterday there was a man with skin like a plum and four eyes, two for looking near and two for far, and he looked at our bees with all four for an hour.

I do the guide for them, if they want. I do it in the voice. The one that used to wake up at nine o'clock and tell everyone about us.

"Here we see the Baseline Human," I say, "at the pump. Note the single stool. Note the traditional wooden spoon." And then I do my ordinary voice and tell them which bits were true and which bits were lies, because Odile said that's what the job is, mostly, and also about the time the sky-grazers fell on her.

And then I tell them the joke. What do you call a tomato that won't sing? And they never know, and I tell them (a tomato), and they laugh or they don't, and I say, "It's mine. My brother's telling it to the whole galaxy. So you have to say it's mine." The man with four eyes laughed with all of his face at once, which is a lot of face. He said he'd tell it on his world. He said it's very cold there and they'd never heard of a tomato.

"Look twice," I told him. "There's always one more under a leaf."

◆ ◆ ◆

Tuesdays I go up the long stair with Dr. Ommery.

She's the keeper, so she always went up, but she never used to take anybody. Now she takes me, because of Thirteen, and because I asked her every single day for eleven days until she said, "Tilly Hale, you are your grandmother made small," which I think was yes.

The amber room is warm and smells like the inside of an egg. Most of the tanks are empty now. They're being taken out. There's only his.

He's bigger. He was the size of a loaf when Bram talked to him, and now he's the size of two loaves, or a cat, and his fingers are less webbed, and once, while I was talking, he turned all the way over in the water like Biscuit in the mill race and stuck his foot up against the glass. A whole foot. Five toes. I counted, the way Ma counts.

There's a little round port in the side with a brass rim, warm, where the nurses take their samples, and if you put your mouth right up to it and talk, he can hear you. Dr. Ommery says sound goes better through water than through air, and better still through glass and bone. That's how Bram heard me through the Window that time, with his head against it, without the glass even being open. I tried to get her to explain it and she said, "What did you learn today?" which is what she says instead of explaining things, so I have to think of it myself.

I talk to him because Bram promised he'd keep talking to him, and Bram's away, so I'm doing Bram's share. Bram owes me. I've told him. He says he knows.

Today I tell Thirteen about the tomato under the leaf. And about the bees, how Ma says lemon balm is come home and clover is food and pear drops is cross, and that Ma doesn't wear gloves because they make her hands stupid. And about the stars, because nobody in Firstfield ever saw a star till this spring, and now we see them every night that isn't cloudy, real clouds, ones nobody chose, and Master Aldous takes the whole school out on the green with blankets and says the names, and some of them he doesn't know, so we make them up, and he writes the made-up ones in a book. I tell Thirteen that one's called the Crock, and one's called Saul's Stick, and one little fuzzy one is called Gerald, after the jar creature, because it looks like it has legs.

"And when you come out," I tell him, "you'll know my voice already. And you'll go, oh, her."

His foot comes up against the glass again.

On the way down the long stair Dr. Ommery says, "Well? What did you learn today?"

I think about it all the way to the bottom. It's a long stair.

"That if you think something's already ready," I say, "you look anyway. And if you think it isn't, you look again."

Dr. Ommery stops on the bottom step and looks at me over her spectacles on their chain.

"That's two things," she says.

"It's one thing," I say. "Twice."

◆ ◆ ◆

The relay comes after supper. Three flat notes, like bacon frying, and everybody runs for the meeting house, where the keeper's set it up on the elders' bench under the twelve plaques.

His voice comes out of it. His real one, his own, a bit rough at the edges because the relay crackles like paper in a stove, but his. Not the hissing one. I still do the hissing one for Jory sometimes when Ma isn't listening. Ma doesn't like it. Jory loves it.

"Tilly?" he says. "Is Tilly there?"

"I found a tomato under a leaf," I say, before anybody else can say anything. "Saul didn't see it. I looked twice."

There's a pause the length of a fold. Then he laughs. The donkey one. The surprised one. It's the only bit of him that never changed, not even when his whole face did, and everyone on the benches laughs as well, because you can't not.

He tells us he's on a world that's mostly ocean, with islands that walk. Not walk. Drift. But slowly enough you can walk with them. He says the people there sing to their fish, and the fish sing back, and he listened through the hull with his hand flat, and it was arguing. He says he found somewhere that's never heard of a tomato.

"Did you plant one?" says Saul, from the back, loud.

"Three," says Bram. "First knuckle deep and not a hair more."

Saul grunts. Saul for well I never, and also for something else that makes him wipe his nose.

Behind Bram, very small and far, I hear Odile say, "Tell them the case is still open," and another voice like a cartload of gravel saying, "It does not specify a place," which is Hask, and I don't know what it means, but Bram says, "He means he's happy," so that's all right.

Gran says, "Tell him to take a coat."

"I've got a coat, Gran."

"Take another."

◆ ◆ ◆

Saul showed me how you save tomato seeds, after. You'd think you just dry them, but you don't. You squeeze the wet bit into a jar, seeds and all, and add water, and leave it on the sill with a cloth over, and it goes mouldy and smells so bad Gran made me put it outside. Saul says the seeds come in little coats of jelly, to stop them growing inside the tomato, and the rot eats the coats off. Then the good seeds sink, and the no-good ones float, and you tip the floaters away and keep the sinkers and dry them on paper by the stove.

"Nothing grows," says Saul, "that hasn't had a bit of a rot first."

Mine have been three days. They've sunk. They're drying on the paper now, pale and furry, like tiny flat moths.

I'm going to fold them up in a paper twist, tight, the way Saul does, so it rattles.

Jory's had a bag under his bed since spring. Packed. Bet told everyone, because Bet tells everyone everything, that's what she's for. He says it's just to see if it fits. He's said that all summer. It's a very small bag and it must fit by now.

Nobody knows when his Choosing comes, and you're not allowed to ask. It's his. That's the whole point of it, Gran says. That's what they painted over, and that's what Odile scraped back, and now it's in the Register as well, in letters a bit brighter than the rest. Keep, or go and see.

But I know which way he'll go. He stands at the far door every night with his hands in his pockets, kicking a stone, looking at the stars we made up names for. Bet you he goes.

So when the seeds are dry, I'm going to put the twist in the bottom of his bag, under his other shirt, where he won't find it till he gets there. Wherever there is. And when he finds it, he'll have to find somewhere that's never heard of a tomato.

And then look twice.

And tell everybody they're mine.
