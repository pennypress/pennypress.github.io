Chapter Five: Keep Moving

CLAIM UC-114 (Ridge Undercroft, north face). Workings: suspended. Wall consolidant: BINDER line B-4, self-setting, sugar-fed; left in place. Biological status: none (subsurface; inert). Renewal: automatic. [scratched into a bolt-plate, in a different hand:] B4 HOLDS. KEEP MOVING.

— Register of Mineral Claims, Margin district, entry unchanged since filing; and an anonymous miner

I wake up because a sky-grazer is breathing bread into my ear.

It's the one with the nicked wing. It has folded itself down in the moss so close that its soft blunt face is lying across my shoulder, and every breath it lets out is warm and yeasty and faintly sour, like the bakehouse with the door just opened. The other one is a little way off, legs tucked, float creaking as the morning warms it. The moss under me is wet with dew and still holds the shape of my landing, a long boy-shaped dent with a sigh at the bottom of it.

I lie there and don't move, because it's the best I've felt in ten days, and because everything hurts. My arms feel as if someone has tried to take them home. There's grazer down in my teeth.

The sky over the ridge goes from grey to the colour of weak tea, and then the sun comes up behind me and lays itself down the slope, and the floating seed starts to lift out of the valleys below like breath off a horse. Gold, rising. All over the far hills, little glittering clouds of it, going up.

The nicked-wing grazer lifts its head. It smells the seed before I see it. Its float gives a long low creak, filling.

That's when the grass starts.

I taste it before I understand it, the way you taste milk on the turn before you've thought the word sour. Down at the bottom of the slope, a ripple runs through the grass, sweet and quick on the front of my tongue: warm here. That's me. That's always me. But behind it, coming down from the ridge we flew over, passed blade to blade the whole long way, is the other thing. Fainter. Darker. Bitter at the back.

Warm. Moving.

I sit up. The grazers pipe, low and worried.

"It's alive," I say. I kicked it in the bulb and watched it fall off a cliff, and it's coming over the ridge, slowly, because it never hurries.

Something is, says the voice. I'd go somewhere.

"Where?"

It doesn't answer, because it doesn't know, and then the morning wind turns, and I find out where.

Cold air. Pouring up the slope out of nowhere, against the sun, smelling of a cellar. Not Gran's crock-under-the-stairs cellar: a deeper one, stone and wet and mushroom, the smell of the bottom step in the mill where nobody goes. I follow it with my face, the way a dog does, and twenty paces up the slope from where I landed there's a ring of fern I hadn't seen in the dark, and inside the ring of fern, the moss just stops.

It's a hole. A round black throat in the hillside, wider than our kitchen, with roots hanging down into it like unravelled rope. The cold is coming out of it in long slow breaths. In. Out.

I think it through, crouching at the edge with my teeth starting to go. The thing hates cold water: I watched it pull its foot out of the pond as if the pond had insulted its mother. It finds me through the grass. And down there, wherever down there goes, there's no sun, so there's no grass. Nothing to point.

The grazers are rising behind me. I hear their floats fill, a slow double creak like two people settling into chairs, and I turn round.

They're a hand off the moss already, hanging, legs unfolding. The seed is going up and they have to go with it.

"Go on," I tell them. "Go on, then."

The other one goes first, straight up into the gold, piping. The nicked-wing one hangs a moment longer. It drifts close and bumps my forehead with its soft nose, once, hard enough to rock me back on my heels. It smells of bread and of me. Then it rises after the other, turning slowly, up and up, until it's a pale speck in a glittering cloud, and then it's one pale speck among a lot of pale specks, way off over the valleys, and I can't tell any more which one is mine.

They aren't mine. I know that. I watch until my eyes water anyway.

Behind me, along the ridge, the grass is still passing it on. Warm. Moving.

I fill both trouser pockets with the fat white roots that grow round the fern, the ones that taste of wet bread and pennies. I take the reed stem out of my shirt and tie the tenth knot. Then I sit down on the lip of the hole, put my plated feet on the first of the hanging roots, and go down into the cold.

The light goes out like a door closing.

For a while there's only the roots in my fists, wet and stringy, and the cold climbing up my legs like water filling a bath. The circle of sky above me shrinks to a plate, then a coin, then a button. My daylight eyes are no use down here. They keep trying, which is worse; they keep making shapes out of nothing, faces, doorways, Ma.

So I use the rest of me.

The rock is cold and slick under my left palm, then, a little further down, it isn't. It's soft. Furred, like the inside of a mitten, and faintly warm, and when I leave my hand on it, something comes up through it into my wrist. A hum. Lower than the fur-trees, lower than the dome on its quietest night. I don't hear it so much as feel it arrive in my back teeth, the way you feel the mill wheel through the floor of the mill.

Oh, says the voice. It's woken right up. Oh, this is big.

The air tastes of stone. Not the dusty dead stone of the drone hatch; wet stone, mineral, like licking a key. And underneath it, with my new tongue, something else, a slow taste, sweet going one way and bitter coming back, so faint and so slow it's like listening to two people talking in the next house through a wall.

My feet find a floor, gritty and cold enough to bite even through the plating. I let go of the roots and stand, blind, and take one step.

And the dark lights up.

Not all at once. In front of me, on the wall where my warmth is, a shelf of something pale starts to glow. Blue-green, soft, like the last of a summer evening caught in a jar. Then another shelf, higher up, catches it. Then another. The light runs away from me up the walls and over my head, shelf after shelf, gill after gill, like somebody walking ahead of me lighting lamps, and I tip my head back and keep tipping it, and it doesn't stop.

It's a room. It's the biggest room I've ever been in. Bigger than the inside of the dome. The walls go up and lean in and meet far above me in arches, and the arches are made of fungus: great ribbed shelves of it, pleated like the underside of a mushroom cap, each pleat a little brighter at the edge than the middle. Between them the rock is threaded with pale lines, thousands of them, branching and joining and branching again, and I realise I'm looking at roots. Not the fungus's. The Spill's. The roots of every fur-tree and fern and glass-bell bush on the hill above me, coming down through the ceiling and hanging there, wound through with the pale threads. A whole forest, seen from underneath.

And everywhere, everywhere, drips. Every one rings. Some high, some low, tink, tonk, tink, never twice the same, like a choir that's been told to tune up and has decided to stay tuning forever.

Somewhere much further in, under all of it, there's a long dull roar. Water. A lot of water, going somewhere fast.

My breath is smoking in the blue light. I didn't notice I'd stopped walking.

It's talking, says the voice. It sounds hungry and pleased, both. All of it. Every one of those threads. It's been saying the same sentence since before your gran was born, and it's only halfway through.

"What's it saying?"

I put my palm on the furred wall and the hum comes up into my teeth again, and the glow brightens round my fingers like a cat leaning into a hand. Up close it smells sweet. Not flower sweet; bruised sweet, like the apples in the bottom of Saul's barrow at the end of the week.

Sugar's down by the water, the voice says. Pass it on. Something heavy fell over that way. Pass it on. Warm thing at the door. Pass it on. A pause. That's you. You're news.

"Hello," I say to a mountain, and laugh, and the laugh goes up into the arches and comes back down a long time later, very small, as if the mountain is trying it out.

I walk for what feels like a whole day. The glow lights ahead of me and fades behind me, so I'm always walking in a moving room of blue-green light, and when I stop and sit, it lowers itself, slowly, the way Gran lowers the lamp.

There are things that were made here, once. A rail on the floor, rusted to lace, running off into the dark. Iron rods driven into the walls, with plates on them furred over like buttons on a coat. On one plate, somebody has scratched letters with a nail. I rub the fur off with my sleeve.

"Holds what?"

No idea. It's very old. The voice sounds bored again; it doesn't care much for things that aren't alive. The rock doesn't remember who wrote it.

I keep moving. It seems polite.

I sleep in a dry niche above a pool, with my back against the furred wall so the hum can go into my spine, and when I wake I don't know if it's morning. I tie a knot anyway.

There's a light in the pool.

Not the blue-green of the shelves. Warmer. A soft amber pulse, under the black water, slow, like someone breathing on a coal. It goes bright, dim, bright. It moves.

I lie very still on my ledge.

The light comes up through the water and becomes a throat. A round, glowing pouch under a sleek dark chin, and above it a face, flat and whiskered, with two eyes like drops of oil, and then the rest of it slides out onto the stone, the length of my forearm. It's brown-black and so wet it looks poured. It has webbed paws, and I count them twice, because the first time I get six and think I've made a mistake. I haven't. Six legs, two at the front and four behind, and it walks like water running downhill, every leg doing something slightly different.

Its whiskers are in the pool, trailing. It keeps them there while it looks at me, as if it's tasting the water and the boy at the same time.

Hungry, says the voice. Curious. Wet. A pause. It thinks you smell like a disappointing fruit.

"How can it think that? It hasn't smelled me."

It's smelling you now. It's very disappointed.

The throat pulses brighter. Its nose goes up and works, and then it isn't looking at me any more. It's looking at my pocket. At the last fat white root in my pocket, which I was saving, which is the last thing I own that tastes even a bit like bread.

"No," I say.

It's already moving.

It's up the rock and onto my legs and into my pocket faster than anything has a right to be, a cold wet weight that smells of river and lamp oil, and out again with the root in its teeth, and off down the slope in a long brown pour of legs. I grab. I get a fistful of air and a whisker-flick across the back of my hand that tingles like nettle.

"Oi!"

I go after it. Of course I do.

It goes over a rock pool and I go into it, and it goes under a shelf and I go into the shelf, and the glow lights up as I come, so the whole chase happens in bursts of blue-green. A lit cave with a brown streak in it. Dark. A lit cave with a brown streak and a wet boy in it, swearing. Dark.

It stops on a flat stone in the middle of a stream, where it thinks I can't get to it, and sits up on its four back legs, holding the root in its two front ones like Saul holds a sandwich, and starts to eat.

I wade straight in, waist deep, so cold it stops my breath, and I grab the other end of the root.

It holds on. I hold on. It growls, a little rattling purr in its glowing throat. I growl back, which I haven't done since Tilly was small enough to wrestle. We stare at each other over the root, nose to nose, in the water, in the dark. Its breath smells of my breakfast.

It says, says the voice, that this is its root. It also says that all roots are its roots.

"Tell it that's not how roots work."

I can't tell it anything. I can only taste what it means. It means teeth, mostly.

The root breaks.

We both fall over backwards. When I come up, spluttering, it's on its stone with half a root and I've got the other half, and its throat pulses slowly, amber, amber, as if it's thinking hard.

It eats its half. Then it looks at mine.

I'm cold to the bone and starving, and the root in my hand is a soggy pale stub. I break it again and hold out one piece on my flat palm, the way you give a carrot to Saul's horse.

The creature looks at my palm for a long moment. Then it leans out off its stone, delicately, all its whiskers forward, and takes the piece so gently I barely feel it. It eats that too. Then it slides into the water, swims round me once, and climbs up my shirt onto my shoulder, where it sits, dripping down my neck, pulsing.

"You're a thief," I tell it.

Its throat glows. Its whiskers are in my ear.

The last thing I had that tasted even a bit like home was a root, and it stole that, and now it's on my shoulder like the root was a fair price. In Firstfield the bakehouse heel is the best bit and the first bit to go, and you never, ever see who took it.

"Biscuit," I say. "You're Biscuit."

It doesn't care what you call it, says the voice. A pause. It likes your ear.

Biscuit knows the way to everything that can be eaten, and that turns out to be most of the way to everything.

It leads, in long wet pours, and waits for me with obvious contempt. It eats a beetle off a wall, and a second off my sleeve. It finds a pool with tiny clear shrimp in it and fishes them out with its whiskers and won't share, and when I try a shrimp anyway they taste, to my noisy tongue, of cold and stone and a very small amount of panic.

That day, or whatever it is, we come to a skylight.

A column of real light, standing in the middle of a cave like a white tree. It comes down a shaft from far overhead, where a fur-tree must have fallen and torn its roots out of the hill, and lands on a mound of earth and leaf-litter, and the mound is green. Ferns. Moss. Tiny pale seedlings, all of them pointing up the shaft at the sky.

I put my hand into the light, and it's warm. Actually warm, like the windowsill at home. I hadn't known how cold I was until then.

Biscuit won't come into it. It sits at the edge of the column with its throat dark, squinting, offended by the whole idea.

I take out Saul's twist of paper. It's damp through and soft as a rag, and when I shake it the seeds still rattle, but fewer now. I kneel on the mound, where the earth is crumbly and warm and smells of leaf-rot and sun, and push five of them in, first knuckle deep and not a hair more, and pinch the soil shut.

These again, says the voice. Still very quiet.

"They're meant to be."

Biscuit comes into the light then, after all, squinting, and digs up the nearest seed with one paw and eats it.

"Biscuit."

It chews, looks thoughtful, and spits it out onto my knee. Not food, apparently. I push the seed back in with my finger, and pat the earth flat, and say it, the thing I've said once already, to a pond.

"In case wherever you're going hasn't got proper ones."

Biscuit sneezes. Up in the shaft, a long way off, a bird I've never heard before says something in four notes, and stops, and says it again.

The next day the caves start going quiet.

I notice it with my hand first. I've got into the habit of trailing my palm along the walls, so the hum comes into my teeth. And then the wall is cold. Not furred. Wet and slick, like the outside of a jug of well water. No hum. The glow doesn't come.

The quiet isn't empty, quite. It's full of drips, and the drips ring differently here. Flatter. Like knocking on a table instead of a bell. And it smells.

It smells of nothing.

I know that nothing. It was in the bottom of the bakehouse crock in the hard winter, and it was on the fallen grazer in the shallows, wet down and stream water and nothing else. The smell of something that has stopped.

Biscuit's throat has gone dim. It presses itself against my ankle, all six legs, damp and shivering.

We go on, because the river's roar is that way. The arches overhead stay dark as we pass under them, dead shelves, grey at the edges like burnt paper. And growing over them, over the walls and the dead gills and the rusted rods, there's something else.

It's grey. Smooth. Rubbery, like the skin on boiled milk, if boiled milk were the size of a barn. It lies in folds and drapes over everything, filling the cracks, sealing the pale threads under it the way wax seals a jar. It doesn't glow. When I bend close to it, it doesn't smell of anything at all, not even stone, which shouldn't be possible.

It isn't talking, says the voice, slowly. It's not saying anything. It's just eating.

"Eating what?"

It tastes through my fingertip, from a careful distance. The talk. It's grown over the threads and it's drinking whatever comes down them. Sugar mostly. A pause, and something in the pause I haven't heard from it before, a sort of distaste. Somebody wrote it. On purpose. It's like your feet: somebody gave it a shape. Only it's been copied and copied and the shape's gone wrong. It was meant to hold something. Now it holds everything.

I'm still frowning at that, standing with my weight on my right foot, when something takes hold of my left heel.

I look down. The grey is round my foot. I don't know when it got there; I didn't feel it come. It's a smooth grey lip, closed round my heel to the ankle bone like a mouth round a spoon, and it isn't soft any more. It's hard. It's going hard as I watch, the way Ma's plaster goes hard on the wall in the time it takes to say a prayer.

I pull. It holds.

I pull harder, and there's a sound like a cork leaving a bottle and my foot comes out of it, plated sole and all, and I go over backwards onto the wet rock.

Where my foot was, there's a grey lump, already as hard as a step. In the top of it is a hole the exact shape of my heel. I can see every ridge of the plating pressed into it, sharp as a seal in wax. If it had been my old pink heel, with skin you can pinch, I'd still be standing in it.

"Keep moving," I say, to nobody. "Right. Got it."

That's when the mountain coughs.

First, my tongue. A wave of bitter comes through the air, sharp at the back, so strong I gag: down the threads from somewhere off to the left, the furthest end of the living wall, passed hand to hand, heavy, falling, heavy, falling, the way the grass passed on the hunter. I've got just long enough to think that's a warning before the floor jumps.

The noise is like the end of the world, and then it's like a sack of potatoes, and then it's dust. Off in the dark to my left, a piece of the ceiling the size of our house has come down. Grit rains on my head. Biscuit is up my back and on top of my skull before the dust reaches us, all six feet in my hair, its throat blazing so bright the whole cave goes amber.

When the rumbling stops, I'm crouched with my arms over my head, coughing. The air tastes of rock flour. Behind us I can taste the network passing the warning on into the living caves. Heavy, falling. Pass it on.

That bit fell because it was dead. I'm sure of it, the way you're sure which tooth hurts. The grey drank the talk out of it, and when the talk stopped, the threads stopped holding. And the dead bits don't hum, and I can't hear which ones are about to fall until they're falling, because I'm only hearing with a hand.

I need to hear it. All of it. The whole mountain, at once.

There was a watch in Firstfield. One. It was Master Aldous's, and it was older than the schoolhouse, silver gone soft and brown with handling, and he wound it every morning at the start of lessons with a tiny key on a string while we said our names.

One winter afternoon, when the dome was doing its grey and the lamps were lit and nobody could sit still, he took it out of his waistcoat and held it up by its ring.

"Who can hear it tick?"

We all leaned in. I could, just. A thin little scratch of a sound, like a beetle walking on paper.

"Not much of a voice," said Master Aldous. "Bram. Come here." He smelled of chalk and lamp oil and the pear drops he kept in his drawer for when someone had been brave. He held the watch out by the ring. "Put that between your teeth. Gently. It's worth more than the meeting house. Now put your fingers in your ears."

I did. The schoolroom went quiet and woolly, the way it goes under the bath.

And then the watch was enormous.

It wasn't in my mouth. It was in my head. Tock. Tock. Tock. Like a cart going over cobbles behind my eyes, like someone knocking from the inside of my skull to be let out. I jerked so hard I nearly bit it, and the whole school laughed, and Master Aldous laughed too, and caught the watch as it fell.

"Where did you hear it?" he said.

"In my teeth."

"Further in than that." He tapped my jaw, by the hinge. Then the bone behind my ear, the hard lump Dr. Ommery had made us all find on ourselves. "Jaw. Skull. And three little bones in there, smaller than seeds, that pass the sound along hand to hand. The hammer, the anvil, the stirrup." He made us say them. All of us, all together, the way we'd said the parts of Gerald in the jar: hammer, anvil, stirrup. Jaw. Skull. "Ears are only the porch, Bram. The house is further in."

Jory wanted to know if it would tick louder if he swallowed it. Master Aldous said we'd all find out together if he tried.

Hammer. Anvil. Stirrup.

I say them aloud in the dead cave, with the dust still settling and Biscuit on my head, and the words go out flat and come back flat. Jaw. Skull. The lump behind my ear.

"Can you," I say, "make all of that hear? Through the rock. Through the threads. Like the watch."

Yes, says the voice, at once, and very gently. Now that you've told me where the house is. Shall I?

It always asks. Sometimes I wish it wouldn't.

"What does it cost?"

Food. Heat. Sleep. A pause. And you'll hear things. All the time. I don't think you'll be able to stop.

The bitter comes down the threads again, faint, from somewhere far off. Another bit going.

"Yes," I say.

It starts in my jaw. A deep ache, like having a tooth pulled backwards, and then it spreads round behind my ears, and it itches, terribly, on the inside of my head where no fingernail can get. I hit the side of my head with the heel of my hand, which does nothing, which Biscuit objects to. My eyes stream. I'm so hungry suddenly that I eat a beetle off the wall without thinking about it and then think about it.

Then it stops, and I put both hands flat on the rock, and I lean down, and I put my teeth against the stone.

And the mountain opens.

I hear Biscuit's heart. It's sitting on my head and I hear its heart through my own skull, quick and light as rain on thatch. I hear my own heart come back to me off the walls. I hear all the drips, the whole choir of them, so I can tell where every single one falls, like seeing in the dark with the back of my head. I hear the river: not a roar now but a hundred voices of water, one deep fast one and a lot of small ones running into it, and I know it's off to my right and down, and I know how far. I hear the living network, the whole web of it, humming, branching, every thread a slightly different note, sweet and busy and slow, further than I can follow, like standing in the middle of all of Firstfield on market day with your eyes shut and hearing every single person in it.

And I hear the quiet.

It's a hole in the sound. Ahead of me, and spreading, a great ragged ring of nothing where the hum just stops, the way a field of crickets stops when you walk into it. I can hear its edges. I can hear the edges moving, slow as a tide coming in, the hum going out thread by thread. And every so often, inside the quiet, there's a tick, a faint dry tick, like a cooling stove. Rock deciding.

I take my teeth off the stone. My whole face is buzzing.

Well? says the voice.

"It's huge," I say, which isn't anything like enough.

On top of my head, Biscuit's heart is still going, a tiny quick drum I can hear without even trying now, right through my scalp. I'll never not hear it. I find, wiping my nose on my sleeve, that I don't mind.

◆ ◆ ◆

The quiet has a middle. That's the next thing. Rings do.

I go in towards it with my teeth to the rock every fifty paces, listening, the way Saul listens to a tomato frame before he leans on it. Where the stone ticks, I go round. Where it's silent and the grey hangs in thick folds from the roof, I go round wider. Twice I feel the bitter come down the threads, and I'm already somewhere else when the roof comes down behind me.

Biscuit leads half the time. Its whiskers keep going out into the air, twitching, its throat lit, and I realise after a while it's smelling something it wants, the same way the quiet goes.

Then I smell it too. Not bruised-apple sweet, like the glow. Sticky sweet. Glass-bell fruit and fur-tree sap and something like honey left in the sun.

The middle of the quiet is a cave shaped like a bowl, and in the bottom of the bowl is the grey. All of it. More grey than I've seen anywhere, heaped and folded and fat, glossy, the size of a haystack, sitting in its own lap. And above it, out of a crack in the wall at about the height of my head, water is weeping.

A thin steady trickle, glinting, running down a groove worn in the rock and straight down into the grey, into a sort of grey pool where the folds have shaped themselves into a basin to catch it. The air over it is so sweet I can taste it with my eyes.

Biscuit makes a noise like a kettle and goes for it.

"No!"

I grab it round the middle, all six legs going, and it twists round and bites me in the thumb, not hard, and keeps reaching for the trickle with every whisker it owns.

It says you are the worst thing that has ever happened, says the voice. It says it hates you forever. It says that's sugar.

I hold the furious otter against my chest and look at the water and think.

The water comes down off the hill above, through all those roots. Once, the threads would've passed it on, sugar's down by the water, pass it on, a little to every cave for miles. Now it all runs into one mouth.

Nothing in Firstfield is free. Ma's honey costs the bees their summer. The grazers pay for floating with seed, and when their seed stopped working, they fell. So what's this paying? It doesn't glow. It doesn't talk. It just holds, and holding the roof of a mountain is dear. All of it is paid for with that trickle.

"If it doesn't get the sugar," I say slowly, "it can't hold."

No.

"So it lets go."

Yes. The voice has gone very interested. And then the talk comes back. And also, it adds, possibly the ceiling.

I look up. The roof over the bowl is grey from edge to edge, and silent, and ticking.

"Can you fix it? The grey. Starve it, or—"

I can only write in you, the voice says. You know that. And then, almost kindly: You don't need me for this one. You need a wall.

I need a wall. I need the trickle to go anywhere but into that basin. And the groove it runs in has a lip on the low side, and below the lip, I can hear it with my teeth to the floor, there's a crack in the rock running off down towards the river, a dark slit where the water would go, all on its own, if something just turned it.

I put Biscuit down. It goes straight for the trickle. I let it; at least it's busy.

I gather flat stones from the old roof falls. I lay them across the groove one at a time, slantwise, the way Saul lays a sod to turn rain off a bed, so the water hits them and spills sideways over the lip and down into the crack. The trickle bends. Most of it goes over the lip and down the slit, glinting, but a little still seeps between the stones into the basin, and a haystack can live forever on a little. And every time I let go of a stone, the water nudges it.

I need mortar. I need something that sets.

I look at my left heel, and the little pale ridges on it, and then at the grey, and then at the hole in the step back there, the perfect shape of my foot.

Anything that stays still.

"Oh, you won't like this," I tell the voice.

I already don't.

I step down into the groove, onto the stones, with the sweet water running cold over my plated feet. I crouch and put my hands on the top stone and press it hard into the gap, and I put my weight on the others with my feet, and I hold them there. And I stop moving.

I hear it with my jaw when it starts: a soft, thick creeping, under the drips, like dough rising. The grey comes up out of the basin towards the stones, because the stones are still, because I'm still, because that's what it was made for. It closes over the lowest stone. Over the gaps between them. Round the edges of my feet. I feel it reach the plating and grip, cold and then tight, and every bone in my legs wants to run.

Gran's count. Five hundred, before you peek at bread.

One. Two. Three.

At a hundred and forty, it's over both my feet to the ankle bones, hard as a step. At two hundred, it's up past the edge of the plating, onto my shins. Onto skin. Plain pink Bram skin with nothing on it, the kind you can pinch. I feel the difference at once: on the plating it was gripping; on skin it's sucking, sealing, like a cup on a wet table. If it sets there, I won't come out of it with a pop. I won't come out of it.

I keep counting. My voice is doing something wobbly. The stones under my hands have stopped shifting. The water's all going over the lip now, every drop, glittering down into the crack, and the seeping between the stones has stopped, because the grey has filled every gap. It's building its own wall. It's sealing itself off from its own supper.

Two hundred and sixty. It's at my knees.

"Biscuit," I say, very calmly. "Biscuit, I could do with you."

Biscuit is sitting on the lip of the groove, eating the last sweet drips off the stone, ignoring me with its whole body.

Two hundred and eighty. It's over my knees.

"Biscuit."

The water's stopped coming into the basin. Biscuit notices that. It looks at the stones. It looks at me. Its throat goes from amber to a dark, sulky red, and it comes down off the lip in one wet pour and lands on my leg, and it bites the grey.

It bites like it bit the root. It worries at the grey where it's still soft, above my knee, tearing it away in rubbery strips, spitting them out, biting again, all six feet braced on my shin. It's fast. It's faster than the grey is. Every bit that's crept onto skin, it tears off, and every bit on the plating it leaves, and I can't tell if it knows the difference or if the grey on the plating has just gone too hard to bite.

Four hundred. Four hundred and ninety-nine.

Five hundred.

I let go of the stones. They don't move. They're a wall now, a grey solid bank across the groove, smooth as a dam on the mill race, and the sweet water is running politely over the lip of it and away down into the dark towards the river, every drop, and the basin below is still and dry.

I pull my left foot. Nothing. I pull harder, and the plating does its job, and it comes out with a pop like a cork, and then the right one, and I fall over backwards onto the floor of the bowl, which is getting to be a habit.

Biscuit stands on my chest, dripping, red-throated, chewing a strip of grey with deep dissatisfaction.

It says, says the voice, that you taste worse than you smell. And that it would die for you. A pause. Or possibly die near you. Its grammar is mostly teeth.

I lie there and laugh till my ribs hurt and my new skull rings with it.

◆ ◆ ◆

Then I put my teeth to the floor of the bowl, because I can't not, and I listen.

At first, nothing different. The quiet, ticking.

Then, a long way off, at the very edge of it, a thread starts to hum.

One note, like the first bird before dawn. Then another thread picks it up, and another, and the hum is coming in from the edge of the ring towards the middle, slow as a rumour, thread by thread. The sugar is going down the crack into the river, and the river runs past a hundred caves, and the threads at the edges are drinking, and passing it on.

And in front of me, the haystack of grey shivers.

It gives a sound I feel more than hear, a deep wet crack, and a seam opens in its side, and it slumps. Just a little. Like Gran sitting down after the long stairs. A fold of it high on the wall peels away from the rock, and from under the fold a shower of grit pours down.

The voice says, Ah.

The roof ticks.

Not one tick. All of it, the whole grey ceiling of the bowl, like the dome's ribs on a cold night, and the bitter comes down the threads so strong it makes my eyes water. Not from far off this time. From over my head. Heavy, falling, heavy, falling, every living thread around the bowl shouting it and passing it on.

It was holding up what it killed.

I grab Biscuit and run.

◆ ◆ ◆

I run with my mouth open, and every time my feet hit the floor the floor tells me something. Dead, dead, tick, dead. Hum. I go towards the hum. Behind me the bowl comes down with a noise that comes up through my feet and slams my teeth together. The dust comes after us like a flood down a lane. Biscuit is on my head again, its throat blazing, and in its light the grey on the walls is peeling and the rock behind it starting to move.

And the arches light.

Not behind me. In front. A shelf of gills on the right-hand wall flares blue-green, though I'm nowhere near it. Then another beyond it. Then another, further, making a line. The living network, drinking its first sugar in who knows how long, talking, passing it ahead of me in a running ribbon of light, and on the front of my tongue it tastes of one thing over and over.

This way. This way. This way.

I go that way.

The floor drops. I slide down a slope of wet scree on my backside with the roof coming down behind me, and the roar of the river grows from a sound into a wall of sound, and the line of lit shelves bends down and down, and then there's no more floor.

There's water. Black and fast and so loud I feel it in my eyeballs, running through a channel in the rock with the network glowing on the walls above it, blue-green, rushing past.

The ceiling behind me lets go.

"Hold on," I tell Biscuit, which is pointless, because it already has hold of my ears, and I jump.

The cold hits me like Saul's barrow. It's colder than the pond, cold that gets into your teeth and your new skull and your heart and squeezes. The river takes me like a hand and throws me.

I go under. I come up. I can hear the river through my jaw even under the water, every rock, every turn, where it's deep and where it's shallow before I get there, and I kick away from the shallow places, the places that grind. Biscuit is on my head, its webbed feet flat on my scalp, its whiskers trailing in the water beside my ear, tasting it, and every time we go under its throat lights up in the black like a lamp in a flooded house.

And above us, on the walls, the network is lit. All the way. A running ribbon of blue-green, cave after cave, rushing past overhead like the lit windows of the lane when you run home late. The drips ring. The river roars. The light says this way, this way, and the river says it too, and for once, for one long cold rushing minute, everything I can hear is saying the same thing.

That's when I taste it.

Under the cold, under the stone and the sugar and the bitter, carried on the hum through my teeth, very far off and very faint, from somewhere deeper in the network than I'll ever go. Green. Very old. Very patient. A taste I know the way I know Ma's voice through a wall.

It tastes of my pond.

The voice in my head goes absolutely silent. Not bored silent. Not asleep. Silent the way you go silent when someone says your name in a crowd and you can't see who.

Then the river turns, and turns again, and the taste is gone, and there's light ahead.

Not blue-green. White.

◆ ◆ ◆

It's like having a bucket of light thrown in my face.

The river spits me out of the hill in a long fall of white water, Biscuit shrieking on my head, into a pool, and the light is everywhere. It's in my eyes like sand. It's under my eyelids when I shut them. I crawl out onto warm rock by feel, with my hands over my face, crying from the glare and coughing up river, and lie there.

Biscuit climbs off my head and shakes, all over me, like a dog with too many legs. Then it gets into my shirt, out of the sun, and sits on my stomach, dripping, its throat pulsing dull red against my skin, and refuses to come out.

I can't see. I've been in the dark three days and my eyes have forgotten what the sun's for.

But I can hear.

I can hear everything. The rock under my back is warm and it's talking: the waterfall pounding into it, the roots of some tree up the bank creaking as they drink, a beetle going across it a stride from my ear, its six feet like a drumroll. Under all that, the mountain I just came out of, still settling. And, if I press my jaw to the stone, the hum, inside the hill. Wider than before. Coming back.

And I can't stop hearing it. I try. I lift my head off the rock and it's still there, in my feet, in my spine, the whole ground going about its business right through me. I think of plain porridge, and how I can't remember what plain tasted like, and I understand I'm not going to remember what quiet sounded like either.

I tie a knot. Thirteen. By feel.

"More edge," I say to Master Aldous, who isn't here, with my eyes shut against the whole bright world. My voice comes back to me off the rock, through my skull, and it doesn't sound quite like mine.

Then the ground speaks.

It comes from far off, out across the valley, through the stone and up into my back. A single footfall. Deep. Slow. So heavy I feel it in my ribs like the bass note of the Friday thunder. I wait. I count. At fifty-one, it comes again. At a hundred and two, again. Something enormous, walking, one step every fifty breaths, out there on the hills, and I can hear it now, and I'll be able to hear it, I suppose, forever.

Biscuit's throat goes from red to amber, against my belly. Curious.

Bram, says the voice.

It hasn't used my name before. I don't think it ever has.

When you're rested, it says, carefully, the way you ask a thing you're afraid of the answer to. Would you put your hand on the stone again? On the hum. Just for a little while.

"Why?"

It doesn't answer for a long time. Out across the valley, the enormous thing takes another step.

I'd like to taste that again, it says. I think I know it.
