I put my hand on the stone, because it asked.

It's evening before I can open my eyes without crying, and the rock by the waterfall pool has gone from hot to just a rock. I lie on my front with my palm flat and my jaw on the stone, and the hum comes up out of the hill, wide and slow, thread after thread waking up and passing it on.

I wait for the voice to say something.

It doesn't, for a long time. It's just listening, behind my eyes, not hungry for once, as if it's trying to hear one voice in a crowd that's moved off round a corner.

It's gone, it says at last. The taste. It's gone too deep.

"We could go back in."

No. Quick, like Ma pulling Tilly back from the stove. Then, softer: No. Not that way. Later.

"Later where?"

It doesn't answer. Across the valley, the enormous thing takes a step. I count without meaning to. Forty-nine, fifty, fifty-one. Another. The ground carries it into my ribs like a door shutting in a big house.

I tie no knot, because it's still the thirteenth, and I sleep on the rock with the waterfall talking to my spine all night. I dream about my pond, green-gold and much deeper than it ever was, and wake up hungry.

* * *

I smell the Hiveplains before I see them.

It's halfway across the valley on the fourteenth morning, my eyes mostly working again, when the wind comes down off the hills in front of me, warm and sweet and so thick I stop walking.

Honey.

Not like honey. Honey, and hot wax, and crushed green stems, the way the bee shed smells in high summer when Ma has the lids off and the whole yard is drowsy with it. I stand in the long grass with my mouth open and my eyes shut and I'm home for one whole breath, the mill wheel going, Tilly singing the tomato song wrong on purpose, and then I open my eyes and I'm not.

Oh, that's loud, says the voice, interested for the first time all morning.

I go up the valley side through grass to my waist, and come over the top, and there they are.

The hills roll away as far as I can see, and every hill is a colour. Not mixed, the way the Spill is. Blocks. Great straight-sided blocks of flowers laid over the land like quilts on a line: violet, a whole hillside of it; then saffron, deep as an egg yolk; then a blue so blue it hurts the backs of my eyes, still raw from the dark. The edges between the blocks are knife-straight, like the farm cut where the Spill began. Somebody planned this. Somebody stood here with a map.

And standing up out of the blocks, here and there, as tall as the mill and fatter, are the hives.

The air between them is moving, specks, gold and dark, millions, a haze over every block like heat over a road. The sound goes straight into my jaw and my new skull: a hum so big and low it's less a sound than a floor I'm standing on. It's got notes in it. A long held chord, the way the meeting house sounds when everyone is humming the first line of a hymn before they find the words.

It's beautiful. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen, and I've seen the stars now, and the Undercroft lit up like a cathedral.

It takes me nearly an hour, sitting on the hilltop with the sun on my neck, to notice what's wrong with it.

The bees aren't landing.

* * *

I go down into the violet first, because it's nearest and because I can't not.

The flowers come to my chest, each head a ball of little purple tubes packed tight as a pincushion, so loaded with pollen it comes off on my sleeves in gold smears. Under the honey and wax there's a taste on the back of my tongue: pepper, like a cut radish, and under that, tin.

And under that, coming off the whole plain like a cold draught under a door, something I didn't expect.

Hunger.

I know hunger. I've been eating beetles. The whole beautiful place smells like the inside of an empty bowl.

There are bees everywhere. Long as my thumb, gold and black, with furred backs like a bumblebee's and long clear wings that flash when they turn. They fly low over the flowers, so close they ruffle the petals. One hovers a finger's breadth above a violet head, feelers working, tasting. Then it lifts and tries the next one, and doesn't land on that either.

There are dead ones in the stems. Lots. A brittle gold litter on the ground, crunching under my feet.

It's like watching someone walk past a table full of bread, says the voice, saying, "No, no, I'm looking for bread."

At the corner of the block there's an enamel board on a post, gone silver in the weather so the letters only show when I tip my head to the light. BLOCK 14, and CLOVER-VIOLET, and a lot of small print, and at the bottom, in big letters: THE HIVE REMEMBERS WHAT YOU SMELL LIKE.

"Cheerful," I say.

And then the hum changes key.

It's right behind me. I turn, and there's a hive in the next block over, a great lumpy tower of grey paper and gold wax, and a piece of the air in front of it has come loose and is coming towards me. A gold-and-black cloud the size of the bakehouse, with a rising, interested whine in it.

I don't run. I'd like to say I decide not to. My legs just don't get the message in time.

They hit me like warm rain.

All over. Hundreds of little hooked feet, light as dandelion fluff, the weight of them adding up until my shirt sags. They walk across my eyelids. One is on my bottom lip, and I can feel its feelers tapping, tapping, at the corner of my mouth, like Tilly trying to get a sweet out of my fist.

Hands first, says Ma, somewhere a long way behind my eyes, in her bee-yard voice. Worry after.

My hands are already coming up to swat. I make them come down, slowly, as if the air is cold syrup, until they're hanging at my sides with bees walking over the knuckles. The hum round my head is a sort of singing, and inside it I can feel them tasting me with their feet and feelers. Hundreds of little tongues.

They aren't stinging.

"What," I say, without opening my mouth, which comes out as "Mmmh."

They think you're a flower, says the voice. It sounds delighted. A very big flower that walked in from the hills. You smell of their food, a bit, in your sweat. Like a memory. It tastes, through my skin. Honey. You've eaten a lot of honey in your life.

Ma's dark honey, on porridge, in the cake, off the spoon when her back was turned. It tastes faintly of clover, because the bees in Firstfield go to the clover strip behind Saul's east rows, there being nowhere else to go.

The cloud on me shifts and grumbles, working out that I'm not a flower after all, and the singing round my head goes down two notes into something I can only call disappointed. They pour back towards their tower and leave me standing in the violet with my arms at my sides and pollen in my eyelashes and every hair on my body standing up.

I let out a breath I've been holding since Saturday.

That's when Biscuit, who has been entirely still against my stomach this whole time, puts its head out of my collar to see what's happening.

* * *

There is one bee left, on my collarbone, walking in a puzzled circle, and Biscuit's face comes up right beside it. They look at each other from a finger apart.

Biscuit's throat goes amber. Very amber. Its whiskers come forward.

"Biscuit," I say, "no."

Biscuit has never once in its life heard the word no and taken it as anything but advice.

It eats the bee.

Or it tries to. There's a crunch, and a high outraged sound like a kettle that's been insulted, and Biscuit falls out of my shirt into the violet with its paws over its mouth and all four back legs kicking. Its top lip swells as I watch, fat and shiny as a split sausage, until its whiskers stick straight out sideways and its face looks like it's carrying a small pillow.

Then its eyes go slow.

It blinks, very carefully, as if blinking is a new idea. It yawns, which with that lip is a terrible thing to see. It tips over onto its side, all six legs folded, and starts to snore.

I crouch over it with my heart going. "Is it—"

It's fine, says the voice. Numb. Sleepy. The sting doesn't kill. It makes you not care for a bit. It tastes, curious. Mostly lip. It'll be furious when it wakes up.

A bee lands on Biscuit's snoring head, stands there a moment as if to say and another thing, and flies off.

I laugh until I have to lie down too, and the hungry plain goes on humming round us in its sad, enormous chord.

* * *

By dusk I've walked three blocks with a sleeping otter in my shirt.

The hives are built of grey stuff like wasp paper, layers and layers of it, chewed and smoothed, and where the paper's worn thin there's comb showing through, whole walls of it, gold, glowing with the low sun behind. Through it, like looking through a dirty window, I can see bees, thousands, packed on the comb, doing something.

Some of them are dancing.

I watch one for a long time. She runs a little straight line up the comb, waggling her back end so hard she blurs, loops round to the right, runs the line again, loops round to the left. The bees round her lean in and touch her with their feelers. Then they turn away. She's telling them something, over and over, and they've stopped listening.

The wax is warm under my hand and smells of honey and old sweat. With my jaw to the paper, the hive is a drum, a hundred people talking in the next room, and under it, all through the tower, a thin high whine like a kettle left on low. I don't know what it is. I don't like it.

Round the hive's mouth, a ragged doorway as wide as a cart, there are bigger bees, long as my hand, dark, with shiny backs like beetle armour. When I come within ten paces, three of them walk out and stand there, feelers out. Not flying. Just standing, the way Elder Marrow stands at the meeting house door when he thinks you've been running.

I don't go any closer.

Guards, says the voice. They don't know what you are. They're deciding.

"What happens if they decide wrong?"

I'd imagine the rest of the hive comes out to discuss it.

I back away very slowly and find the corner where the hive's shadow falls, and take out Saul's twist of paper. It's so light now I can feel through it how few are left. I push four into the dark earth at the hive's foot, where old fallen wax has made the soil sweet, first knuckle deep and not a hair more.

"In case wherever you're going hasn't got proper ones," I tell them.

Bees, says the voice, don't eat tomatoes.

"Tomatoes don't need bees. Saul shakes them." I pat the soil flat. "He goes down the rows on a Thursday and taps every truss with his stick, like a man telling them off."

Does that work?

"He's got the best tomatoes in Firstfield. He hates it."

That night the hive-towers all over the plain light up gold in the last of the sun, every one, like lanterns hung in a giant's orchard. I sleep in the lee of ours with its thin hungry whine in my spine, and I tie the fourteenth knot.

* * *

There's a honey bog in block fifteen. I find it the hard way.

It's the next morning, with Biscuit still snoring into my stomach, and the grass ahead of me is green and lush and lovely, and I put my foot in it, and my foot keeps going. Under the grass there's no ground, only something thick and dark gold that smells so sweet it makes my teeth hurt, and it's got my leg to the shin and it's pulling, slowly, like a dog that doesn't want to give a stick back.

I throw myself backwards and my plated foot comes out with a long sucking sound and a rope of gold hanging off it. Sitting on solid ground, I look twice, the way Saul says. The grass over the bog is greener than the grass round it, taller, in a perfect circle like a green island, because underneath it honey has been spilling out of some fallen comb for years and the grass has been drinking it.

"Look twice," I say, in Saul's voice, which is mostly a grumble.

You looked once, says the voice, and then you stood in it.

I lick my foot, because I'm starving. It tastes of old honey and dirt and, very faintly, of a bee who has been dead a long time.

It tastes of clover.

* * *

I sit on the edge of the bog with the sticky on my fingers, tasting. Darker than Ma's, but the same note in it, sweet and green and a bit like hay.

I bite a saffron flower. Burnt sugar and the inside of a penny. I go back to the violet. Pepper, tin. I walk all morning with my mouth full of flowers to the blue, and it tastes like cold water poured over hot iron and makes my eyes water.

None of them taste of clover.

They used to, the voice says, quiet and careful, the way it tasted the grazers' guts. All of them. It's underneath. Very faint. Like the writing on that board.

I go back to the board in block fourteen and find the line I didn't bother with yesterday, tipping my head so the light catches the ghost of the letters. ALL BLOCKS SCENT-KEYED TO STANDARD.

"Scent-keyed," I say.

One smell, says the voice. Every colour they liked, all saying the same word, so one kind of bee could feed on all of them. Clever. A pause. Then they went away and left it.

And the flowers got copied, every year, copy of a copy, like the grazers' fermenters. The violet drifting off towards pepper. The saffron towards pennies. The blue towards iron. And the bees handing it down, queen to queen, comb to comb, the one smell that means food, while the flowers walked off and changed their names.

The bees mobbed me because I was the only thing on the whole plain that smelled right.

"They're starving," I say, "in a field full of dinner."

Yes.

"Because nobody told them it's the same dinner."

Yes. A pause. Could you?

I look at the hive. The dancer behind the wax, telling them and telling them.

"I don't speak bee."

No, says the voice. But your mother does.

* * *

The smoker smelled of burning sacking and dry grass, and when Ma squeezed the little leather bellows it went huff and a fat grey worm of smoke came out of its spout and lay down over the hive, slow and lazy, like a cat.

I was small. Small enough that the bee veil Ma had pinned onto my hat came down past my knees, so I saw the whole yard through a fog of black net, like being inside a raincloud.

"Slow," she said. "Everything slow. Bees think fast is the same as cross."

She lifted the lid. The smell came up like the inside of the honey cake, and the hum went up a note and settled. Ma's sleeves were rolled to the elbow. She never wore the gloves. She said they made her hands stupid.

She drew out a frame, slowly, and turned it to the light, and the comb was solid with bees, crawling, shining. "There," she said. "Watch her."

One bee, running up the comb in a straight line, waggling so hard she blurred. Loop round. Straight line again. Waggle. Loop the other way.

"She's telling them where the clover is," said Ma. "The straight line's the way to go. Straight up the comb means straight at the sun. Lean it over, and they lean their flying. And the more she waggles, the further it is." She tipped her head at it, smiling. "She's found Saul's east strip again. She's very pleased with herself."

I didn't believe her. Then I watched the bees round the dancer climb off the frame and fly out, straight towards the east rows, as if somebody had drawn them a map.

"And they talk with their noses," Ma said. "When they're happy it smells like this." Warm wax. Honey. "When they want everyone home, they stick their bottoms up and fan it, see that one, at the door? Smells like lemon balm. Come on. Come home. This way." She sniffed. "And when they're cross."

"What do they smell like when they're cross?"

"Like Master Aldous's drawer."

Pear drops. Sharp and sweet. I was going to laugh, and then something walked across my face inside the veil.

A bee. Inside. It had got in somewhere at the neck, and it walked up my cheek and onto my eyelid, and I felt every one of its feet, and I screamed. My hands went up.

"Bram."

Her hands got there first. Not fast. That's the thing I remember. The hum climbing, the air going sharp and sweet, pear drops, a cloud lifting off the open hive, and Ma's hands coming up through it as slow and steady as if she were lifting bread out of the oven. One on my wrist, bringing my hand back down. One at my veil, lifting the hem, just a finger's width, so the bee on my eye could find the way out.

The bees were all over her arms. Hundreds. A crawling gold sleeve to the elbow. She didn't look at them once. She was looking at me.

"Hands first," she said, quiet. "Worry after."

The bee walked off my eyelid and out under the hem. The cloud settled. Ma lowered her arms, slowly, and the bees on them walked down to her fingertips and dropped off, one at a time, like drops off a wet branch, back onto the comb.

Then she let out a long breath, and I saw her hands shake. Just once. Just after.

* * *

I sit down in front of the hive in block fourteen with my back to the sign and Biscuit still snoring in my lap, and I say it all out loud, so the voice can hold it.

"The dance. The straight line is the sun. The waggle is how far." I hold my hands up and turn them over, like I'm showing Ma they're clean. "Lemon balm is come home. Pear drops is cross. Honey and warm wax is happy. And clover is food."

Yes, says the voice, very still. They say it with their skin. Little pockets that make a smell and let it go. A pause. You've got the same. All over you. The places your sweat comes out.

Dr. Ommery's chart of the flayed man, with Jory's moustache on it. The skin was drawn like a slice of cake, and down in the bottom layer, little curled tubes like the springs in Gran's chair, each with a thread going up to the top. Sweat glands, she said, tapping. Two million of them, or thereabouts. Everyone's a kettle, Bram. That's how you cool.

"Sweat glands," I say. "Two million of them. In the bottom of the skin, coiled up, like springs. With a little pipe to the top."

That I can hold. It sounds pleased with me, the way Master Aldous sounded when you got a long word right. I can teach them new words. You'd only have to want to say it, and your skin would say it. Shall I?

"What does it cost?"

Food. Heat. Sleep. The same answer it always gives, and then the other part, a bit more gently. And it goes the other way too. If you can say it, you can hear it. Everything here talks with its skin. You'll smell what they feel. Everyone. All the time. You won't stop.

Biscuit snores in my lap, its lip still the size of a damson. I look at the hive, at the thin high whine in its walls. I've worked out what that is, I think. It's the sound of thousands of things being hungry at once.

"Yes," I say.

It starts in my armpits.

I'd like that to sound braver. It's an itch like the time Jory bet me I wouldn't roll in the nettle patch behind the mill, and it spreads to my palms and the back of my neck and then everywhere, all two million of them at once. I scratch so hard I tip Biscuit off my lap. I roll in the violet saying words Gran would wash my mouth for, and the hunger comes after it like a cart after a horse, so bad I pull up a violet root and eat it, dirt and all.

Then it stops.

I lie there, panting, sweating. Smelling my own sweat. It smells like me, mostly. Salt and boy and grass.

Then Biscuit wakes up.

* * *

Biscuit wakes up on its back in the violet with its lip like a damson and its whole body remembering, all at once, the bee, and the indignity, and the fact that I didn't stop it, which I did, I said no. It rolls over. It looks at me. Its throat goes a dark, dull, sullen red.

And the sulk comes off it like heat off a stove.

It smells like wet socks. Like Gran's compost cabbage water on the third day. Like the inside of Jory's boot after the harvest dance, and under all that, sour and mournful, milk that's been left in a sunny window and has thought about its life. It's not a smell. It's an opinion. It sits behind my eyes and complains.

I scrabble backwards through the violet on my elbows with my eyes watering.

Biscuit follows me, sulking. It turns its back on me, deliberately, and sits there, three paces off, glowing red and radiating.

Ah, says the voice. I did mention.

"Make it stop."

It's an otter. I can't make it stop. I can only tell you it says it is never, ever speaking to you again, and also, it would like something to eat.

I give Biscuit two beetles off the hive wall and the end of the honey from the bog on my fingers. It eats them with its back still turned. The sulk drops, slowly, from wet socks to something more like a damp dishcloth. I can live with a damp dishcloth.

Then I turn round to the plain, and breathe in.

And the whole Hiveplains talks to me at once.

The violet saying here, here, here in an accent so strong I can only just understand it. The saffron saying it in pennies. The bees overhead, each trailing a thin thread of smell like a voice, nothing, nothing here, sad and dry. And from the hive behind me, so thick I could lean on it: hunger. Not a taste now. A word. An empty bowl with a spoon going round in it, scraping.

And from the doorway, faint, from the dancer's back, from every bee that brushes past her and turns away, the other word. The old one. The one everyone remembers.

Clover. Clover. Where's the clover.

I sit there in the violet, smelling everybody's feelings, until the sun goes down. Then I tie the fifteenth knot, and lie awake with my nose full of a whole hive's hunger, and think.

* * *

I'm up before the bees.

It's the sixteenth morning, grey and wet, the flowers shut tight against the dew. Biscuit's in my shirt, sulking at a low, bearable dishcloth. I've thought it through all night, the way I thought through the grazers. The bees know one word for food. The flowers have forgotten how to say it. So somebody has to say both at once, the new smell and the old word, on one thing, until the bees learn they're the same.

Like Tilly learning her letters: Ma pointing at the picture of the apple and saying apple, and at the word and saying apple, until Tilly read it on her own and was so pleased she cried.

"Picture," I say, "and word."

You're the word, says the voice. What's the picture?

I take my shirt off, with Biscuit in it, and lay it in the grass. Then I wade into the violet, and I lie down, and I roll.

I roll in the saffron, too, and crawl through into the blue and roll in that until my eyes sting. The pollen gets in my ears and up my nose and down my trousers. When I stand up I'm gold and purple and that terrible hurting blue all over, like a painted post at the harvest fair, and I smell of pepper and pennies and hot iron, all three words, all at once.

Biscuit's sulk goes up, briefly, to wet socks. I think that's a laugh, actually. I think it's laughing at me.

"Now," I say. "Clover."

I want to say it. That's all it takes. I think of Ma's honey on the spoon, and the clover strip behind the east rows on a hot afternoon, and my skin, all two million of it, warms and prickles.

And I smell of clover.

Not the faint memory the bees found on the first day. Loud, the way the bakehouse smells of bread from three houses off.

Ooh, says the voice. Well done.

The sun comes over the hills, and the first scouts come out of the hive in block fourteen to look again for a word that isn't there. One turns in the air and comes straight at me. Then a hundred. Warm rain, little hooked feet, and this time my hands stay down. With my new nose I can smell what they're saying to each other as they crawl on me, a quick excited chatter: clover, clover, CLOVER, and then, as their feet find the pollen, puzzled, —and this? —and this?

I stand very still in the morning, covered in bees, saying clover with my whole skin.

Picture, I think at them. Word. Picture. Word.

A scout lifts off my shoulder with violet pollen on her legs, two little purple lumps like saddlebags. She hangs in front of my face, turning, tasting. Then she drops to a nodding purple head, a finger's breadth over it, the way they've been doing for years and years.

She lands.

I don't breathe.

Her whole head goes down into one of the little purple tubes. Her back end waggles, once, as if to settle in. She comes up with her face dusted gold, and goes down into the next.

Over the pepper and the tin and the hunger, faint, from one bee, I smell warm wax and honey.

Happy.

"Yes," I say, out loud, too loud, and the bees on my face all lift and settle again in a little startled wave.

* * *

She goes home. I follow, covered in bees, and put my eye to the wax where the paper's worn thin. I know her by the violet on her legs. She pushes through the crowd on the comb, and she starts to dance.

Straight line. Waggle. Loop round. Straight line. Waggle. Loop the other way.

The bees round her lean in. They touch her with their feelers, the way they've been touching every dancer for years. And this time, they don't turn away.

One of them climbs off the comb.

Then six. And the hum against my cheek, in my jaw, my skull, my teeth, starts to change. The thin kettle whine wavers. Finds another note under it, and another. A chord.

Bees pour out of the doorway past the guards, a gold river, and fly straight out over the violet in a long bright line, exactly as if somebody had drawn them a map.

I sit down where I am, against the hive wall, and watch them land.

It's like rain starting. One flower, then a dozen, then the whole near edge of the block nodding as the bees come down, and the air that's been full of dry sad nothing here goes thick and warm and busy. Here. Here. HERE.

By noon I've stood in front of two more hives, rolling and being crawled on and saying clover until my skin is raw and I'm so hungry I eat honey out of a bog with both hands. I don't know how it spreads after that. Maybe a dancer wanders into the next hive. Maybe it's the smell, rolling over the blocks on the wind. But one tower after another, across the hills, I hear the kettle-whine falter and the chord come up underneath it, and the bees come out in long lines like threads unwinding, and land.

Bee on flower, flower to seed, seed to next year's meadow. A plain that's been in bloom and dying for longer than I've been alive will be here next year.

I lie on my back in the blue, which is starting to smell a little of clover too, I swear it, where the bees have been, and the sky above me is full of gold specks going about their business, and none of them are looking for me any more.

Biscuit climbs onto my chest and sits there, glowing amber. The sulk has gone. It smells of river and lamp oil and something warm, almost like bread.

"You're speaking to me," I say.

It says, says the voice, it isn't. It says you smell like a flower and it's very embarrassed for you.

I tie the sixteenth knot lying down, and I go to sleep in the blue with the hive-towers lighting up gold over the hills like lanterns, every one, and every one of them humming.

* * *

The seventeenth morning, I smell them before I hear them.

I'm at the mouth of the hive in block fourteen. The guards know me now; I've been saying their hive's own smell back to them, and they just touch my feet with their feelers when I go past. Biscuit's asleep in my shirt, and I'm crouched by the four tomato seeds at the hive's foot, seeing if they've split yet. They haven't. Saul's never do, till they're ready.

And the wind brings me cedar.

Clean and sharp, like the inside of the good chest at the meeting house where they keep the Charter. And under the cedar, a dry, hot, dusty smell, like ink that's been cooked.

I know that smell. I don't know its name. I smelled it in the Upper House, in the corridor outside the glass room, the night I heard them say draw from him.

I stand up.

They're coming down the hill through the violet, two of them, side by side but not together, the way Jory's da and his brother walk to the meeting house the week after they've argued about a fence. Tall, thin, in grey suits the colour of the hive paper, not a crease on them, holding their arms up out of the flowers like men wading a cold river. Silver hair combed flat. The same face. Not like brothers: the same, as if somebody had cut one man out of paper twice.

One carries a slim black case. The other has a long grey stick with a silver end, and slung on his back something like a fat short gun with a bell on the end.

Under the cedar and the cooked ink, they smell of other things, now that I've got the nose for it. The one on the left smells of something tight and sour, like vinegar in a cut. The one on the right smells eager and hot, like a pan left on the stove.

I could run. But there's a little grey boat on the hilltop behind them, on legs like a beetle's, with a cold blue light on its nose, and I don't know how fast that is. So I stand in front of the hive with my hands at my sides.

They stop ten paces off and brush pollen off their sleeves with the same flick of the same fingers. Each has a little enamel pin on his lapel. A number. The vinegar one says 4. The hot-pan one says 12.

"Good morning," says the one with the 4. He has a lovely voice. Deep and kind, like the man who reads the lessons at midwinter. "You'll be Reference Twelve."

"Bram," I say. My voice goes up in the middle.

"Of course." He opens the black case on his forearm, takes out a stylus, writes something. "Associate Pell. Recovery. Varro-Tandem Bioreference." He nods his head at the other one, without looking at him. "And Twelve."

There are two of us called Twelve, then. I nearly say so. I don't think either of them would find it funny.

"Associate Pell," says the other one.

"The licence," says Four, still writing, "specifies the Associate. Singular. One of us is the Associate. One of us is a number." He smiles at me, a pleasant smile. "We'll be taking you back now. There's a recovery claim. You're company materials. Nothing personal."

"Everything documented," says Twelve, quickly, and then looks annoyed that he's said it at the same time as Four's mouth was already opening.

The tight vinegar smell comes off Four in a wave.

"I'm not materials," I say. "I'm Bram. Son of Wenna."

"I'm sure you are," says Four, and writes it down.

Twelve swings the fat gun off his back. Close to, the bell on the end isn't a bell. It's a coiled net, grey and fine as a spider's web, folded into the mouth.

I take a step back. My heel hits the hive.

The guards come out of the doorway behind me, slow heavy feet on the old wax, and stand by my ankles, heads turning. Tasting. They touch my feet, and know me, and turn their heads to the two men in grey.

And taste cedar and cooked ink. And something they've never smelled in their lives.

The hum behind me goes up a note.

"That's a hive," says Twelve, with interest, looking past me. "Look, Four. They're enormous. Is that in the brief?"

"If you'd read the brief, Twelve," says Four, "we would have been here yesterday."

"I flew all night—"

"I'll be noting that."

I'm frightened. I want to be clear about that. My knees have gone soft like dough, and I can smell my own fear coming off my skin, sour and thin. I'm frightened, and I'm thinking, the way you think when you've climbed the bell tower on a dare and suddenly remember about the way down.

I think: the hive remembers what you smell like.

And I think: Ma's hands.

"In Firstfield," I say, and my voice nearly works, "when you meet somebody, you say whose you are."

Four looks up from his case, polite. "I'm aware of the custom."

"Bram," I say. "Son of Wenna, of the house of Hale. Grandson of Gran. Brother of Tilly." I wipe my palm on my trousers, very slowly, hands first, worry after, and I hold it out to him. "And you shake hands. It's rude not to."

Four looks at my hand.

The vinegar smell goes up and down. Behind him Twelve has the net gun on my middle and is watching Four sideways, hot as a stove door opening.

"Courtesy," says Four at last, "costs nothing." He tucks the stylus behind his ear and steps forward, two neat steps, and takes my hand. His hand is dry and cool and smooth as paper. "Associate Pell."

I think about pear drops.

I think about Master Aldous's drawer, opened for someone who'd been brave. I think about my palm, all the little coiled springs in it, and I want, very much, to be cross.

My palm goes hot.

Four lets go and steps back and takes the stylus from behind his ear. He sniffs, very slightly, and frowns, like a man who's found a sweet in his coat pocket he doesn't remember putting there.

"And you," I say to Twelve, holding out my hand again.

Twelve looks at Four. Four looks at Twelve. I watch it happen: Twelve's not going to be the one who wasn't polite. Not with Four writing everything down.

Twelve shifts the net gun to his left arm and steps forward and shakes my hand so hard my teeth click. "Associate Pell," he says, loudly, looking at Four.

"Noted," says Four.

I let go. My palm's tingling. They both smell, very faintly, over the cedar and the ink, of Master Aldous's drawer.

"You might want to stand very still," I tell them.

* * *

The guards smell it first.

Their feelers go out, stiff, towards the two grey men. Then all three open their wings, great clear fans with a shine like oil on water, and buzz, deep and angry and close.

The hive behind me answers.

The chord breaks. It climbs, high and sharp, into my jaw and my skull like a saw. The paper walls shiver. And out of the doorway, out of every crack and gap in the grey, out of the top of the tower, the hive comes up.

All of it. Like smoke. Like the whole thing taking a breath out.

"Ah," says Four.

"Is that—" says Twelve.

I put my arms down at my sides, and I say warm wax and this hive's honey as hard as I can with every bit of skin I've got, and I say it to the cloud the way you'd say your own name, and I close my eyes.

They come past me like a river round a stone.

Thousands. The noise of them so loud it's not noise any more, it's weather. Little feet on my face, tasting, ours, ours, lifting off again. Not one of them stings me. They pour past me and they find the pear drops.

When I open my eyes, the Associates have become two tall shapes made of bees.

Twelve fires the net. It goes off with a thwump and opens in the air like a grey flower, and catches about four hundred bees, and wraps round Four.

"Twelve!"

"It was aimed—"

Four goes over, slowly, like a tree, inside the net and the bees. Twelve swings the grey stick, the one with the silver end, and the end crackles blue, and he hits a cloud of bees with it, which does exactly what you'd think hitting a cloud would do. Then he's hopping. Then he's sitting down. Then he's lying in the short grass with his hair still perfectly combed and his face going slowly, gently, puffy, with the stick fizzing in the grass beside him, saying, "Ow. Ow. Ow," in a lovely deep voice, more slowly each time.

The sound starts to come down. The guards, satisfied, fold their wings. The cloud thins over the two of them and pours back into the hive in a long gold stream, still grumbling, and the chord comes back underneath, ragged, then steadier.

Four is lying in the net with his face swelling up like Biscuit's lip. His eyes have gone slow. He's still got the black case. He's opened it on his chest, and he's trying to write in it, but his hand isn't working, and the stylus keeps sliding off sideways and drawing long lines. So he puts the stylus down, very carefully, and starts writing with his finger, in the pollen on the case's lid.

"Twelve," he says, thickly. "I'm filing a grievance."

"I'm filing one," says Twelve, from the grass, with his eyes shut, "against you."

"For?"

There's a long pause. "I'll think of something," says Twelve, and starts to snore.

The vinegar smell coming off Four has gone soft and woolly. But his eyes are on me, slow and puffed, and I realise, looking down, what he's looking at.

My wrist. The green-gold veins under the skin, the leaf-pattern from the pond. They're glowing, like the shelves in the Undercroft, a soft live light running up my forearm in threads, as if my skin is still talking. And my feet, grey-green and plated, ridged like bark.

"That's," says Four. He swallows. His lovely voice has gone thick and slurred. "That isn't. In the brief."

I should run. I don't. He's frightened too, I can smell it under the sleepy woolly stuff, a thin sharp thread of it, like mine.

"You should put something cold on that," I say. "Mud's good."

Then I step over Twelve, very carefully, and walk away through the violet with the bees going about their business all round me.

* * *

I go round a circle of too-green grass on the edge of the saffron, because I know what's under it, and lie flat on the far side with Biscuit beside me, its throat dimmed right down, because it isn't stupid.

From here I can see the hive. The net. The two grey heaps.

My whole body's shaking, knees and hands and teeth, so hard I put my jaw against the earth to stop them, and then I can hear everything: the hive's chord settling; Biscuit's quick drum of a heart; and far off on the hills to the north, the footfall. Fifty-one. Like it's been walking the whole time.

I count to five hundred, the way Gran counts before she peeks at bread.

That, says the voice, and stops. It doesn't usually stop. Bram. That was—

"I shook hands with them," I say into the dirt. "I shook hands with them."

You did. And then it laughs. I've never heard it laugh before. It's not a sound, exactly. It's a warm fizzing behind my eyes, like the first sip of Gran's elderflower when it's gone a bit too far. You said pear drops into a man's hand and then you said please and thank you.

"Ma would kill me."

Your mother, says the voice, would have done it slower.

Down by the hive, something moves.

Four's sitting up, halfway, still in the net, face like a pudding. He's talking into the case. I put my jaw to the ground, and the earth carries it: a slow, thick, careful voice, saying each word as if it's carrying it across a stream.

"Report. Priority. Upstairs." A pause. A breath. "Specimen is not. Baseline. Specimen exhibits. Active modification. Feet. Skin. Something. Under the skin. Lit." Another pause, longer. "Something is writing in him."

The case gives a little chime. Two notes, bright and clean, like a spoon on a glass. It carries a long way over the flowers.

Four lies back down in the net, very slowly, and folds his hands on the case.

I don't know who upstairs is. I know the Upper House had stairs, and that smell. I lie in the saffron with my glowing wrist pressed into the dirt, to put it out, and it doesn't go out, and I think about somebody, somewhere, a long way off, opening a message and reading the word writing, and sitting up.

We should go, says the voice. Quiet again.

"Yes."

Now, I'd think.

"Yes."

I tie the seventeenth knot with my fingers shaking. Biscuit climbs into my shirt and smells, for once, of nothing but river and lamp oil and otter, which I've decided is the best smell there is.

Then I go north, keeping low, through the blue, which smells of iron and of clover now, both, where the bees have been. Over the next hill. And the next, all day.

On the third hill, the wind turns.

It comes up from the far side, from somewhere beyond the last of the blocks, and it's cool, and it's wet, and it smells of something I've never smelled in my life. Salt. Not salt from the pot: a great wide salt, enormous, rotting and green and alive, like a thousand ponds that someone's left out in the sun to think. It's so big I can smell the size of it.

What is that? says the voice. It sounds young. It sounds as if it hasn't smelled anything new in a very long time.

"I don't know," I say. "Let's find out."

Behind me, the hive-towers are lighting up gold in the evening, one after another, every one of them humming. Somewhere back there a message with my name in it is going up a long set of stairs.

Ahead of me, out past the hills, something enormous takes a step. Fifty-one.

I start walking.
