# Chapter Nine: The Third One's for the Sea

*What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? by Vani. © Vani 2026, CC BY-SA 4.0. Much of this text was drafted by an AI (Claude) under a human author's direction.*

> Twice in, twice out, the sea goes round about; once for the fish and once for the tree, and the third one's for the sea.
>
> — skipping rhyme, Highwater; taught to children before they are allowed on the low walks. Nobody at Highwater knows who made it up, and nobody has ever written it down.
>

I walk all night towards the smell.

It gets bigger. That's the only way I can say it. Every hill I come over, the wind off the far side is wetter and saltier, until I can taste it on my teeth, until my lips go stiff with it, until Biscuit puts its head out of my collar, sneezes, and goes back in. The footfall goes on behind me, fifty-one, fifty-one, fainter every hour, like a clock in a house you've left.

Just before dawn I come up the last slope, through grass gone thin and tough and grey, and the ground stops.

And there it is.

I don't know what I thought. Ommery read us the story about the boy on the raft, and she did a voice for the wind, and I suppose I thought the sea would look like the pond, only wider. Like the pond if you stood on Tilly's shoulders.

It isn't wide. It's all. It starts at the bottom of the hill and goes out, flat and grey and moving all over at once like the skin on hot milk, and it doesn't stop. There's no far side. There's no glass. There's a line, a long way off, where the grey of the water meets the grey of the sky, and the line is perfectly straight, and the sky comes down and sits on it.

The sun comes up out of the water.

Not from behind a hill. Out of it, as if it's been down there all night keeping warm. It lays a road across the sea, gold, broken into a thousand pieces that won't keep still, and the road comes all the way to the beach below my feet.

I sit down. My legs decide it.

"More sea," I say. My voice comes out odd. "And then land."

Where? says the voice. It's been quiet all night. It sounds the way I feel. Where's the land?

"Ommery said there is some."

She might be wrong.

The sound comes up the hill in long slow breaths, a hush and a draw. In the back of my jaw, where the Undercroft taught me to listen, it's a deep slow rocking, the whole world turning over in its sleep.

I can't hear the fifty-one any more. The sea's too big for it.

* * *

Below the hill there's a strip of grey sand with dark weed piled on it in lines, like the tidemark round a bath, and past the sand the mud begins. Miles of it, shining, the colour of a slate roof after rain, cut with channels of silver water that wander about as if they've forgotten where they were going.

And out on the mud, a long way off, the trees.

A whole forest, standing in the mud on legs: roots like a spider's, dozens to a tree, arching down so each trunk stands a man's height clear of the ground in a cage of itself. Their roof runs along the coast as far as I can see both ways, a long green cloud on stilts.

The smell coming off it is salt, and rot, and something sweet under the rot, like a windfall apple in long grass.

Fruit, says the voice.

I've had two fistfuls of violet roots since yesterday. I go down onto the mud.

My plated feet spread and grip, and when I go in to the ankle the mud lets me go with a long rude noise. It smells of eggs. In the pools there are things with too many legs that run sideways and wave one big claw at me, outraged, as if I've walked into their kitchen.

I'd like to say I notice the water coming in. I'm looking at the claws.

What I notice is that the channel I stepped over a while back, a trickle I could jump, is now as wide as the lane outside our house. And it's running. The wrong way. Inland, past my ankles, silver and quick and full of turning weed.

I turn round.

There's water between me and the beach, spreading over the mud like milk over a table, coming from everywhere at once. I watch my own footprints fill.

"It's coming in," I say. "Why is it coming in?"

I don't know what it does, says the voice. I've never seen it either.

I run for the trees. They're nearer.

It's at my knees when I reach the first roots. It's at my waist when I'm in among them, grabbing at the arches, slimy, slipping, and it isn't just rising now, it's pulling, a whole river of it pouring sideways into the orchard, and I lose my feet.

I've never been in water over my head.

Nobody in Firstfield has. There's the mill race, which is forbidden, and the pond in the square, which comes to Tilly's knees. You don't learn a thing you'll never need.

It's green and loud and in my nose and mouth, so much salt it burns all the way down, and I come up once for half a breath and a root hits me across the back and I'm under again. My hands close on weed and the weed comes away. Biscuit's gone out of my shirt, a dark shape flicking off through the green, and I think, good, it can swim, good, and then I can't think anything, because my chest is on fire.

Bram. The voice, very close, very fast. Your chest. Tell me what's in your chest. Tell me what it is.

I can't. There isn't a word in me. There's just the burning.

Something has my collar.

Small, and furious, and pulling. An amber glow in the green right by my face, six legs going, teeth in the cloth at the back of my neck. It isn't strong enough. It does it anyway.

My hand hits a root, and this time the root doesn't come away.

* * *

I wake up being sick.

Seawater, a great deal of it, out of my nose and mouth together, onto bark. Everything hurts in the way that means it's still there.

Biscuit is sitting on my back, its heart going like rain on thatch. It smells of river and lamp oil and, very strongly, of fright, like a struck match.

"Thank you," I say into the bark.

Biscuit bites my ear, not hard, and climbs into my shirt.

"It's alive," says somebody above me, and laughs.

I roll over.

There's a girl in the tree.

She's hanging upside down from a branch by the backs of her knees, eating a plum, her hair a dark wet rope dripping beside my head. Her fingers are long. Her toes, hooked over the branch, are nearly as long as fingers. She has freckles across her nose and shoulders, dark ones, in patches, like the bark of a plane tree.

She's looking at me the way Jory looks at a frog.

"I saw that," she says. "You were doing this." She lets go of her elbows and does a sort of windmill with both arms. Then a sort of slap. Then she lets her arms go floppy and sinks them, slowly. "And then this."

"I wasn't—"

"What are you?"

"Bram."

"No, what line." She swings a little, considering me. "You've got feet like a mud-crab, so you're a wader. But you swim like a dropped sack." She frowns, as if I'm a sum that won't come out. "Are you a very bad swimmer of some kind I don't know about? Or a very good one having a bad day?"

"I've never been swimming."

She stares at me. Then she laughs, and the laugh starts before I've finished talking, as if it was already on its way and my words just opened the door. It's a big laugh for someone upside down. She has to grab the branch.

"You came here," she says, "on the tide, and you can't swim?"

"I didn't know about the tide."

That's worse. She laughs so hard the plum falls out of her hand, and Biscuit puts one paw out of my collar and catches it before it hits the water, and eats it.

The laugh stops.

She looks at Biscuit. Biscuit, chewing, looks back, its throat going a slow, satisfied amber.

"That," she says, "was my plum."

"It pulled me out," I say. "It's had a long morning."

* * *

Her name is Sedge.

She tells me while she's climbing down, which she does the way water comes down a wall: no steps, just a long fall that happens to touch every branch on the way. She lands on the root beside me without making it shake.

"Who wrote you?"

"What?"

"Who wrote you." Slowly, as if I'm being dense. "Which company. Which line."

It comes off her then. I've had the nose since the Hiveplains and I can't switch it off. Under the salt and plum and clean wet-wood smell of her there's something thin and alert, like a wire pulled tight. She's laughing at me, and she's also ready to be up that tree before I can blink.

"Nobody wrote me. I'm from Firstfield." It's what you say, so I say the rest. "Bram, son of Wenna, of the house of Hale. Grandson of Ada Hale."

Saying Gran's name out loud is strange. I can't remember the last time I heard anyone call her Ada. She's Gran to the whole of Firstfield. Gran to Ommery. Gran to Jory's da.

Sedge has gone very still. The wire pulls tighter.

"Nobody," she says, "wrote you."

"No."

"Same as us." She looks at my feet, at the green-gold veins at my wrist. "Except somebody did."

* * *

She takes me up.

She makes me climb, and I'm bad at it, and she says so. She goes up round me and over me, showing me where to put things, with Biscuit on her shoulder now, because Biscuit has decided that anyone who drops a plum is worth knowing.

We go through the roof of leaves like going up through the floor of a house, and on the other side of it there's another house.

There's a whole village in the sky.

Platforms lashed across the branches. Houses woven out of living branches, roofed with sailcloth patched with red. Rope bridges from tree to tree, sagging and swaying, some wide enough for two, some only a rope to walk on and a rope to hold. Babies asleep in nets slung between branches. Blue smoke from cookfires in clay pots.

And the noise. Gulls on every high branch, barking like dogs. Creaking: rope, wood, the whole village riding up and down in the wind like the ship in the story. And whistles, from everywhere, short and long, passing over my head and away into the leaves.

"They're telling everyone," says Sedge. "About you."

"What are they saying?"

She listens. One long whistle, two short.

"That you can't swim."

Below us, the mudflat has become a lake with trees in it. Something long and dark slides under the platform without a ripple.

"Snap-eel," says Sedge. "Bites what's quick. Don't be quick."

The houses on the lowest platforms have water in them. A hand's depth, lapping over the doorsills, and people inside lifting things onto shelves without hurrying, the way Ma clears a table. On the biggest trunk in the village, grey and smooth as a wall, somebody has cut notches, dozens of them, from just above the water to just below my feet, each with little marks beside it.

"What are those?"

"High water. The big autumn tide, every year." She points down, near the water. "That one's my gran's gran's. That one's my gran's." Then up, nearly level with my knee. "That one's last year."

It's a long way from the bottom notch to the top. Most of it is near the top.

"It's coming up," I say.

"Every year. The reef's gone soft." She says it the way Gran says the chest's bad. Not news. Weather.

* * *

They don't like me.

I smell it before anyone says a word. It comes off the woman gutting fish, off a boy my size with webbed hands who stops halfway up a ladder to watch me pass: thin and cold and sharp, like the dome on a winter morning. They breathe in as I go by, and their faces change.

By the notched trunk there's a man waiting. Tall, long-fingered like Sedge, grey in his beard, a white scar puckered down from one ear. He has a gutting knife on his belt and doesn't touch it. He doesn't need to.

"Teasel," says Sedge, "this is Bram. He can't swim."

"I heard." He looks at me. Then he leans down, not close, and breathes in through his nose.

His face goes flat.

"He smells of the dome," he says.

"I haven't been in the dome since—"

"Not your skin. Your shirt."

The grey shirt they gave me upstairs. It's been through the Spill and the Undercroft and a meadow of furious bees and the sea. It can't smell of anything but me.

I put my nose to my sleeve.

Under the salt and mud and Biscuit and pollen, faint as the words on a weathered sign: cedar. And cooked ink.

The corridor outside the glass room. The two grey men in the violet.

It's been on me the whole time, in the weave, in whatever they washed it in up there, like a label I didn't have the nose to read.

"We've had that smell here before," says Teasel. "It came in a grey boat. It asked for people by number."

A whistle somewhere up in the leaves, long and falling.

"He's not from them," says Sedge. "Look at his feet. He's running from them."

"Then they'll be following, won't they." Teasel looks up past the canopy. The sky's gone a funny colour while we've been talking, a yellowish brass, and the gulls on the high branches have all turned to face the same way, into the wind. "And it's the wrong night for it." He says to Sedge, not to me: "He goes at first light. On the ebb."

Then he walks off along a one-rope bridge, without holding the other rope.

I take the shirt off.

I don't decide to. My hands do it. They take the reed stem out of the pocket first, with its seventeen knots. Then I throw the shirt as hard as I can out past the edge, and it opens in the air like a grey bird and comes down on the water, and the tide takes it away under the trees.

Sedge watches it go.

"That was your only shirt."

"Yes."

She looks at me a long moment, and the wire loosens, a little, and something comes up under it I don't have a word for yet. Warm. A bit annoyed. Like bread that's caught on the bottom.

"You're a lot of work," she says, and goes to find me another one.

* * *

The new shirt is sailcloth, stiff and pale, patched on one elbow with red, and it smells of smoke and salt and somebody else, and it's too big. Sedge rolls up the sleeves without asking, four hard turns each, like wringing out a fish.

Then she feeds me, because she says I look like a drowned dog and it's putting people off their dinner.

Salt-plums, purple-black, with a white bloom on the skin you could write your name in. I bite one and my whole face folds up, it's so sour, and Sedge laughs at me, and then halfway down it changes its mind and goes sweet, dark and winey, and I eat four in a row with the juice running to my elbows.

Then moonpears.

For those she climbs out along a branch that bends under her like a fishing rod. They're pale yellow-white, the size of my fist, with skin so thin I can see into them the way you see into your hand held over a lamp. In the shade under the leaves, they glow. Just a little. A lamp turned right down.

"We hang them by the beds," Sedge says. "For the little ones."

I bite one. It tastes like cold water with honey in it, and smells like rain.

Oh, says the voice. It hasn't said anything since the water. Oh, more of that.

I'm on my second when I feel Saul's twist in my trouser pocket, soaked through and soft as pulp. I can feel through the paper how few are left. There's a cut-down barrel at the platform's edge, full of dark silt, with something growing in it that smells of aniseed. I press two seeds in beside the aniseed. First knuckle deep and not a hair more.

"What are those?" says Sedge.

"Tomatoes."

"What's a tomato?"

I look at her. She waits.

"It's red," I say. "And when it's warm off the vine and you bite it, it goes everywhere, and it tastes of—" I don't know what it tastes of. It tastes of Saul's east rows in August, and Tilly's joke, and the inside of the summer. "You'll see."

"I will?"

"In case wherever you're going," I tell the seeds, "hasn't got proper ones."

I fold up the twist and put it away. It weighs nothing at all.

Sedge is looking at the barrel the way she looked at me when I said Firstfield.

* * *

In the afternoon the sea goes out again.

It hisses out between the trees in a hundred channels, and the roots come up, dripping, and people come down the ladders with baskets and pick fish off the mud like windfalls.

"Twice a day," says Sedge. "You'd think they'd tell you that, in a dome."

"There isn't a sea in the dome."

"Is it true you can't see the sky?"

"You can see the weather. Not the stars."

"Is it true they watch you eat?"

"Only on Tuesdays."

She thinks I'm joking. I let her.

"Come on," she says. "I'll show you why you're leaving."

We go out along the root-walks, roots bent and tied as they grew into a knotted road above the mud. Sedge doesn't use her hands. I use both hands, both feet and, at one point, my chin.

Then the trees end, and there's the reef.

It's a wall, shoulder-high at low water, along the whole seaward edge of the orchard, and it's alive. Grey roots locked through each other like fingers, and plastered over them, shell, crusted with little blue-black shells packed close as cobbles. On the far side the sea slaps at it white. On this side the water's calm, and in the calm, among the roots, there are thousands of tiny silver fish, hanging like dust in a sunbeam.

"Nursery," says Sedge. "Every fish we eat was born in there."

Where the reef bends out into the sea in a great knotted elbow, the roots are as grey as old stone, and the shell is so thick it's a cliff. No root shows at all, only the blue-black shells, packed tight.

"The Knuckle," she says. "Oldest bit. The rest grew out from it." She works her long fingers in behind one of the shells and pulls. It doesn't come. She braces a foot and pulls harder, and it tears away, and from the back of it hang dozens of fine golden threads, like hair, still stuck to the reef.

"Stitchers. They hang on with those, and grow shell, and the roots feed them, and the shell holds the roots." She holds it out. The threads touch my fingers and stick at once, fine as spider-silk and stronger. "On the big tides their babies come in. Millions, like snow, in the water. They used to land all along the reef and stick. That's how it grew."

"Used to?"

She points along the wall.

Fifty paces from the Knuckle the reef changes colour. The roots go greenish-white, swollen, and the shell on them is thin and flaky, coming away in plates like the crust on old bread. Hardly any stitchers. And a long way further on, the wall has sagged in the middle like the back of an old horse, and the sea is coming over it in white sheets at every wave.

"The Mouth," she says. "It's been opening since before I was born."

"Why don't the snow ones land there?"

"They just don't." She shrugs, too hard. "They come in, and go through, and go out again. Now they only stop at the Knuckle."

Bram, says the voice, quiet. Lick it.

"What?"

"I didn't say anything," says Sedge.

The reef. Here, and then there.

I crouch at the Knuckle and put my tongue on the wet shell.

Salt, of course; nothing here isn't salt. Under it, the slick green taste of the roots. And under that, coming off the roots into the water the way the bees' words came off their skins: something cold and dry and clean. Chalk. The stick of chalk in Master Aldous's hand, squeaking on the board, the dust of it on your tongue when you've been sent to sit in the front row.

"Did you just," says Sedge, "lick the reef?"

I go along to where it turns greenish-white, and lick that.

Salt. Green. And a taste like the chalk, only wrong. Smudged. As if somebody rubbed it out with a sleeve and wrote it again from memory, a bit crooked, and then copied the crooked one.

Drifted, says the voice. Like the flowers. A careful pause. The Knuckle still says it right. The rest is saying it from memory.

"Saying what?"

Here, it says. Stop here. Hold on.

"You're talking to yourself," says Sedge.

"I'm talking to my wrist," I say, which doesn't help.

* * *

She doesn't ask. The wire comes up off her again, and under it something quick and bright, like a green stick cracking.

Then she grins.

"Bet you won't," she says.

I had it ready. I've had it on my tongue since she pulled the stitcher off. She got there first.

"Won't what?"

She points down. On the inside curve of the Knuckle there's a pool, deep and clear, green going to dark, the light coming down into it in long wavering bars and the roots going down and down, crusted with stitchers, to a bottom I can't see.

"Touch the bottom. And stay down as long as me."

She drops off the edge without a splash, like a stone off a wall in a dream, and she's gone.

I count, the way Gran counts before she peeks at bread.

At two hundred I'm on my knees at the edge. At three hundred I've got one foot in the water, when the pool goes bloop and her head comes up, sleek as Biscuit's, breathing slow.

"Your turn."

I go in.

It's warm as a bath that's been standing. I kick the way she kicked and go down, eyes stinging, tiny fish scattering off the stitchers like sparks off a fire.

Ten. Twenty. My chest starts.

Thirty. It's a fist under my ribs, squeezing.

I come up at thirty-four, choking, and hang off a root breathing like Ma's smoker.

Sedge is sitting on the Knuckle with her feet in the water. She doesn't laugh. That's worse. She smiles a small, kind smile, like Ma's when Tilly tries a long word, and says, "Thirty-four's quite good. For a dropped sack."

* * *

I can help with that, says the voice. If you can name it.

Sedge has gone down again, just for fun. I'm on the root with my arms round my knees, dripping.

"My lungs."

That's a word. That's not a shape.

I shut my eyes.

Dr. Ommery, in Gran's room, the winter Gran's chest was bad and wouldn't mend. Gran sitting up in bed with her back bare and her shoulders like a coat-hanger, and Ommery's wooden listening-trumpet with the brass rim, the wide end on Gran's back and the narrow end at my ear. Listen.

It's a tree, Bram, upside down. The trunk comes down your throat and branches, and those branch, smaller and smaller, and at the end of the littlest twigs, instead of leaves, little rooms. Millions, in bunches like grapes, each with a wall as thin as a soap bubble and blood on the other side. The air goes in the rooms and through the walls into the blood. That's all breathing is.

And under the trumpet, Gran's breath, with a crackle in it like paper.

That's the little rooms at the bottom not opening, Ommery said. That's why she's tired.

I say it. All of it. The trunk, the branches, the rooms like grapes, the soap-bubble walls, the blood on the other side.

Yes. The voice is very interested. That I can hold. More rooms. Bigger. But that only holds a longer breath. That's not the sea.

I'm thinking about the Window. The school group from the Changed lands, the day Jory drew the goat, faces pressed to the other side of the glass. The pink slits on their necks, three each side below the ear, fluttering when they got excited, as if they were breathing even while they laughed.

"Gills," I say. I touch my neck where theirs were. "Three each side. The water goes in your mouth and out of them, and inside they've got the same little rooms, only flat, with blood on the other side. And the air's in the water."

The voice is quiet a long moment.

You saw that once, it says. Through glass.

"Can you hold it?"

I can try. A pause. It'll cost. Your chest will want the water. In dry air it'll ache. Gentler: Like hers.

Gran's breath, crackling like paper.

Below me the pool bulges and Sedge comes up, and shakes her hair out, and looks at me sitting there dry and talking to my wrist.

"Yes," I say.

* * *

It starts in my ribs.

A stretch, wide and slow, as if somebody has a hand on each side of me from the inside, pushing. Then a pressure, deep, down where Ommery's trumpet listened, as if the rooms are opening, all of them, room after room, like doors down a long corridor. It doesn't hurt. Then it does. My chest goes huge and tight and I can't get a breath in or out, and I'm on my back on the Knuckle with Sedge's face over me upside down saying what's wrong with you, and I can't answer.

And I'm small, and I'm up high, higher than the bakehouse roof, and the ground's swaying under me, and it isn't ground. It's shoulders. Big ones, warm through wool, a hand round each of my ankles, and under my legs a chest going up and down, humming. Singing. Something low, no words, or words I've lost, and I feel it more than hear it, coming up through his back into my knees, like the dome's own note but warm. We're going down the lane. I've got two fistfuls of his hair. Everything smells of sawdust.

That's all there is. The shoulders. The song through his chest. I don't remember his face.

Then my neck splits open.

Not a cut. Like a yawn, in a place that's never yawned. Three each side, under my ears. Air rushes over them, cold, and they burn and flutter, and when I put my hands up they slam shut under my fingers like little mouths.

The breath comes back. Huge. More breath than I've ever had, going down and down the tree.

And at the bottom, an ache. Dull and dry and scratchy.

I cough. It sounds like Gran.

Sedge is kneeling over me with both hands flat on my chest, as if I might go off somewhere. She's staring at my neck. She smells of fright, the struck match, and under it the green stick, and something new, wide and warm, that I have no word for at all.

"What," she says, "was that."

"I asked for gills."

"You asked—" She sits back on her heels. She opens her mouth. Shuts it.

It's the first time since I washed up that she hasn't got anything to say.

"Bet you won't," I say, "go and touch the bottom again."

* * *

We go in together.

I breathe out on the way down, all of it, the way we do at the Window. Then I open my mouth and let the sea in.

Everything in me says no. My throat closes. My gills slam shut. I'm sinking with a mouthful of salt water and nowhere to put it, and I nearly kick for the top.

Breathe properly, says Ma. Not her bee-yard voice. Her Tuesday one.

I let the water through.

In at my mouth, warm and salt, out at my neck, a soft pushing flutter, three each side, like holding a moth in each hand. And the burning doesn't come.

I'm breathing. Under the sea. With my eyes open.

It's green, and gold where the light comes in, and the fish hang in their thousands and part round me like a curtain and close again behind. The slap and roar of the reef is up there somewhere, a sound in another room. Down here there's a deep slow rocking, and the clicks and pops of a thousand small things eating, and my own heart.

Sedge is beside me, upside down, her hair floating straight up like smoke, her eyes very wide. I go down and touch the bottom. Sand, and something that scuttles off under my palm.

I count. A hundred. Two hundred. The dry ache fades, because it isn't dry down here.

At three hundred, Sedge puts her long hand round my wrist to tug me up.

My gills slam shut.

All six at once, like doors in a wind. I've no idea why. One moment I'm breathing the sea, and the next I'm a boy with a mouthful of salt water at the bottom of a pool, and I go up so fast I crack my head on a root.

I come out choking. She comes up beside me laughing so hard she has to hang onto the Knuckle.

Your heart rate, says the voice, is doing something unusual. It started when she took your wrist. It's still doing it. It sounds puzzled. Shall I fix it?

"No," I say. Out loud. Very loud.

Sedge stops laughing. "No what?"

"Nothing."

"You said no."

"I said it to my wrist."

She treads water and looks at me, her freckles dark and wet, her mouth trying very hard to stay straight and failing at one corner.

"Your neck went shut," she says. "When I touched you."

"It does that."

"When you're scared?"

"When I'm surprised."

"Bram," she says, with tremendous dignity, "you can't swim, you lick the reef, you talk to your arm, and your neck shuts when you're surprised." She pushes off backwards, floating, looking at the sky. "You're the worst fish I ever met."

But what comes off her over the water is the bright one. The green stick, cracking.

I don't say anything. I'm not a fool.

* * *

The sky goes brass, then purple, then a bruised green-black I haven't got a name for, and the gulls stop barking and go somewhere else.

At Highwater everyone is tying things down. Babies come out of the nets and go up the ladders hand to hand. Teasel goes past on the one-rope bridge with a coil over each shoulder and doesn't look at me, and under the cold sharp smell coming off him there's something tight and low that I know from my own skin. Fear.

We're on the high platform, the top of the biggest tree, where they've lit a fire in an iron basket for the people still out on the mud to steer by. The smoke goes straight up a moment, then the wind lays it flat across the canopy like a grey flag.

"Storm tide," says Sedge. "The third one. Big tide, and a storm on top, and the wind piling the water up." She's watching the Mouth, where the sea is already coming over in long white sheets and the water inside is brown and rising. "Last time I was little. It took the bottom two platforms and the school. Teasel's house." She doesn't say what else. "The reef was higher then."

Out past the reef, the lightning comes down.

In the dome we only ever heard it, like furniture being moved upstairs. Out here it cracks down out of the black all at once, a tree made of light, upside down, branching, and for one heartbeat the whole sea is lit white and flat as a page.

Then the sound. It hits me in the new big chest and the jaw and the teeth, and the whole tree shudders.

Biscuit, in my shirt, says something I'm glad I can't translate.

Then the rain, warm and sideways and so heavy I can taste the sea in it, and the wind behind it, and the whole of Highwater starts to creak like a ship.

* * *

It's full dark when I see the snow.

Sedge has sent me down to a low platform, where the water's already lapping over the boards, to hold a rope I don't understand, and I look down into the flood, and it's glowing.

Where it moves, there's a light in it, cold and blue-green as the Undercroft. Every wave that slaps the boards breaks in sparks.

And in the light, falling through the water, there are specks.

Millions. Pale, tiny, turning, drifting in on the tide past the roots and under the platforms and away into the drowned dark of the orchard. Like snow on a still night when they open the dome's top vents in midwinter, falling through the lamplight in the square.

"Sedge."

She's beside me out of nowhere, wet, hair plastered to her face. She looks down.

"Stitchers." Her voice goes funny. "The storm brings them. They'll go straight through."

They turn as they pass my feet, as if they're looking for a word that isn't there.

I've had the word in my mouth all afternoon.

"Could you find the Mouth from here?" I say. "Underwater. In the dark."

Sedge looks at me.

"There's guide-ropes along the inside of the reef," she says slowly. "Knotted. Every knot's different, so you know where you are by feel." She holds up a hand; her long fingertips are calloused hard. "I could find it with my eyes shut. Why?"

"Because the Knuckle still knows what to say to them," I say, "and the Mouth's forgotten. And I can say it."

"You can what?"

"With my skin."

A pause, full of rain.

"That's the stupidest thing anyone's ever told me," says Sedge.

"I know."

"It's a storm tide. In the dark. The water through the Mouth'll pull you through the reef like a cork out of a bottle." She's angry; it comes off her hot as a stove door. And under the anger the bright thing, the green stick, louder than I've ever smelled it. "You've had gills for half a day."

"Bet you won't," I say.

She stares at me.

Then she pulls the knife off her belt, cuts the rope I'm holding, and ties one end round my waist in a knot I've never seen, a knot that takes her one movement and three heartbeats, and the other end round her own.

"Don't let go of that," she says. "And don't be quick."

* * *

We go off the end of the root-walk at the Knuckle.

The surface is a nightmare. Black water coming at us in hills, rain so hard it hurts, the wind screaming in the canopy behind. A wave throws me at the roots, and the rope jerks at my waist, and then Sedge has my wrist and pulls me under.

And it's quiet.

The storm's up there, a roar through a wall. Down here there's the rocking, and the glow. Everywhere we move, we light up: Sedge's kicking legs trail blue-green fire, my hands leave shining smears. And all round us, drifting in through the dark in a great slow storm of their own, the snow.

I breathe the sea in. My gills flutter, and stay open.

The Knuckle rises in front of us, a cliff crusted black with stitchers. I put my face to it and taste.

Chalk. Clean, cold, dry. Here. Stop here. Hold on. And the snow round the Knuckle is coming down onto it, settling on the old shell, sticking. Here, they land. Here, they always have.

I take the taste in and hold it, the way you hold a word you're afraid you'll forget before you get to say it.

Then I tap Sedge's shoulder, and she takes the guide-rope in her long hand, and we go.

She doesn't look. She feels, knot to knot, never hurrying, and I follow her fire through the dark. Something long and slow goes past, close, and turns its head towards my legs, and I make myself go slow, slow as Ma lifting bread out of the oven, and it goes on into the black.

My chest aches. My gills are working so hard they burn.

The roots under my hand go soft. My fingers sink into one like a rotten apple. And the water's running, all one way, through the reef, pulling at my legs, at the rope, at Sedge, until we're not swimming, we're being carried towards a great roaring glowing rush where the whole sea is coming over the wall and down through it at once.

The Mouth.

The snow is pouring through it. Millions, turning over and over in the bright water, on and away into the dark of the orchard, where there's nothing for them to hold.

Sedge grabs a root and wraps her legs round it and hauls on my rope, and I slam into the roots beside her so hard my teeth click.

She puts her face right up to mine. Her hair is streaming sideways in the current like a flag.

She jerks her chin at the Mouth. Well?

I let go of the rope.

I get both hands in among the soft roots, as deep as they'll go, and both plated feet, and I hang there in the throat of the Mouth with the whole sea trying to pull me through, and I do the hardest thing of all.

I keep still.

And I say it.

Not out loud. With everything: the two million coiled springs of me, palms and neck and the backs of my knees. I think of chalk, and the old roots at the Knuckle saying the same thing for longer than Highwater has had a name. Here. Stop here. Hold on.

My wrist lights up.

I see it through my own eyelids. The leaf veins burning green-gold, running up my arm in threads, brighter than they've ever been, until I'm a lamp hung in the Mouth of the reef with the sea pouring past me.

The snow turns.

Not all of it. A drift of it, nearest me, wavers in the current. Turns over. And comes down.

They land on my hands.

Tiny, pale, soft as a breath, a dozen, a hundred, settling on my fingers where they're buried in the roots. And I feel them grab. Each one throws out threads, fine as hair, faster than I can see, and the threads stick and pull tight, and the soft root under my hand tightens with them, one thread at a time, like somebody sewing up a split seam.

I keep saying it. And the snow keeps coming.

Out of the dark, out of the roaring current, the glowing specks peel away by the thousand and swing in to the reef, all along the Mouth, a long bright curtain folding down onto the soft white roots, settling, grabbing, stitching. Every root in the Mouth lights up, crawling with threads, the way frost grew across the dome's glass the hard winter, in feathers, pane by pane.

Something hits me.

A branch, a great dead limb torn loose by the storm, coming through the Mouth end-on like a battering ram. It takes me across the back and rips my left hand out of the roots, and I'm going, turning over in the bright water with the Mouth roaring past.

My gills slam shut.

Breathe properly.

A hand closes on my ankle. Long fingers. Hard as a root.

Sedge, upside down, both legs locked round a root and her whole body stretched flat into the current, has me by the foot. She hauls. I come back into the roots and get my hand in, and she doesn't let go, she just holds on, and holds on, and I open my mouth and let the sea in, and my gills open, and I say it again.

Here. Hold on.

I don't know how long. My wrist burns. My chest burns. I'm so hungry the hunger's got teeth. The snow comes down and down, and under my hands, root by root, a thread at a time, the Mouth stops being soft.

It doesn't close. I won't pretend it does. The sea still comes over the top in a long white roaring sheet. But it comes over the top. Not through. The pull on my legs goes from a river to a stream to a heavy breathing, in and out, the way the sea breathes everywhere else.

And the snow still falling past us on the tide swings in to the reef by itself now, wherever a root has caught the word.

Sedge lets go of my ankle.

She puts her hand flat on the root beside my face, on the stitched place, glowing. Then she puts her hand flat on my chest.

My gills don't close.

* * *

We come up in the lee of the Knuckle in the last of the dark, climb out onto the roots, and lie there.

The storm's gone over. The rain's just rain. Far out, the lightning's still going, silent, too far off for its sound to reach us, over somewhere else. At the top of the biggest tree someone has kept the fire going in its basket, and the smoke goes straight up.

I'm coughing, the dry ache, the paper crackle. Sedge thumps me on the back without being asked, which doesn't help at all.

Then something comes out of my collar.

Not Biscuit; Biscuit is up on the high platform where I left it with three moonpears and a whistle from Sedge that I think meant watch the otter. It's one of the snow. A stitcher the size and colour of my littlest fingernail, that's ridden all the way up out of the sea inside my shirt. It walks along my collarbone. Then it throws out six fine golden threads and sticks itself firmly to my skin, just below my ear, just above my gills.

I look at it, cross-eyed.

"Oh, no," says Sedge, and she's laughing again, flat on her back on the Knuckle with the rain on her face. "Oh, no. It thinks you're the reef."

"Get it off."

"I'm not touching it, it's holding on. That's the whole point."

It's very happy, says the voice. It sounds tired, and pleased, and full. It says here. It says stop here.

"Tell it I'm not a wall."

You were, says the voice. For a bit.

* * *

At dawn the tide goes out, slow and brown and tired, full of branches and torn sailcloth and drowned fruit, and the reef comes up out of it.

I watch from the Knuckle, with the stitcher under my ear like an earring Gran wouldn't approve of. All along the Mouth, where the soft white roots were, there's a crust now. Thin, new, pale gold, dense with tiny shells, each one hung on by its threads. The Mouth's still there, a long dip in the wall like the back of an old horse. But it's a horse that's standing up.

Sedge is asleep sitting up beside me, head against a root, her long toes still curled round another one, in case.

Behind us, people are coming down onto the root-walks to look. The fear's gone off them. There's tiredness, like a wet coat, and something fizzy and disbelieving, the way the lane felt the morning the bread came back; and under it the cold sharp one, but thinner.

Teasel walks the reef with his hands behind his back, looking at the new gold crust all the way to the Mouth and all the way back. When he gets to me he stops, and breathes in.

He doesn't say anything. He takes a moonpear out of his coat, puts it down on the root beside my foot, and walks on.

I eat it. Cold water with honey in it. The best thing I've ever eaten.

Then I take out the reed stem and tie the eighteenth knot, late, with fingers that still taste of chalk.

That's when the voices on the landing go quiet, where the root-walks come in under the trees, and Sedge wakes, and I stand up and turn round.

There's an old woman on the landing.

Small. Not much taller than Tilly. Brown as a nut, white hair cropped short as a field after harvest, one eye milky and clouded and the other black and quick as a bird's. She has both hands on top of a stick and her shoulder against a grey post, and I could swear the side of her that's touching the post has gone grey too, a little, as if the post is coming off on her.

The people on the walk have stopped. They're looking at her the way you look at Elder Marrow when he's about to say something.

She's looking at me.

At the stitcher on my neck. At my plated feet. At my tired, glowing wrist. Then at my face, a long time, head on one side, the good eye narrowing, as if she's reading a sign that's gone silver in the weather.

Then she laughs: short, creaky and loud, like a gate that hasn't been opened in years getting kicked open.

"Well," she says. "You've got Ada Hale's chin."
