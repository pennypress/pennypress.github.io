What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 2 of 24

Chapter One: The Twelfth (Do Not Tap the Glass)

← PrefaceContentsChapter Two: A Discrepancy at Breakfast →

Two hundred people lived beneath this dome for eight generations. Please take a moment here. They were not exhibits.

— Placard, the North Skywalk, Revised Visitor Guide to the Moriel Heritage Preserve (third edition, after the Transformation)

Every morning at nine, the audio guide wakes up before the roosters do.

First there's a click, high up in the ribs of the dome, like someone clearing their throat in an empty church. Then a hiss. Then, from speakers hidden all over the great glass, the warm voice of a woman who died two hundred years ago and sold the rights: "Here we see the Baseline Humans of Firstfield at their morning chores."

Two hundred of us, give or take a baby. Forty-one houses with thatched roofs, a schoolhouse, a meeting house, a mill that turns because Old Saul turns it, and fields of tomatoes that the visitors find very upsetting. Outside, in the Changed lands, tomatoes are blue and the size of your head and they hum a little when they're ripe. We've seen pictures. Ours are red, they're small, and they just sit there.

Above it all, hanging from the dome like the strings of a puppet show, run the skywalks: long glass tubes full of visitors, shuffling slowly over our roofs, pointing down.

"Note," says the audio guide, as Ma throws open our shutters, "the single set of lungs."

Ma breathes in deep, on purpose, very loud. Ma has opinions about the audio guide.

"Morning, Lady Speaker," she says to the ceiling. "Lovely day for being looked at."

◆ ◆ ◆

By then I've already been to the bakehouse and back. Feeding the crock is my job, every morning since I could reach the shelf: lift the cloth, and the smell comes up to meet you, sour and yeasty and cool, like a cellar that's happy. Flour, water, stir, cloth back on. By the time I'm home it's already muttering to itself in little bubbles. Gran keeps her own crock under our stairs, the mother crock, the one that came from her gran, and nobody feeds that one but Gran.

My name is Bram. I'm fifteen. I'm the son of Wenna, who keeps the bees and the books for the mill. I'm the brother of Tilly, who is eight and the worst person alive. I'm the grandson of Gran, who is older than the mill and twice as hard to turn. That's how you say who you are in Firstfield: you say whose you are.

"Bram," says Tilly, at breakfast, with her mouth full. "Bram. Bram. Bram."

"No."

"What do you call a tomato that won't sing?"

"Tilly."

"A tomato," says Tilly, and laughs so hard porridge comes out of her nose.

That is the whole joke. She's told it about four hundred times. It gets funnier to her every time, which Gran says is the mark of either a saint or an idiot, and we'll know which when she's older.

The kitchen smells of oats and woodsmoke and the honey Ma stirs into the pot, dark honey that tastes faintly of clover. Ma is doing three things at once: wiping Tilly's nose with one hand, scraping the pot with the other, hooking a basket off its peg with her foot.

"Hands first, worry after," she says, and the basket lands in my arms before I've finished swallowing. "Saul wants the east rows picked before the Tuesday crowd. And Bram —"

"I know."

"Breathe properly at the Window. Don't do the face."

"I never do the face."

"You always do the face," says Gran, from her chair by the stove, without opening her eyes. "A crooked face makes a crooked day, and a crooked day makes a crooked year."

Gran has a saying for everything. Some of them are a hundred years old, and some, I'm fairly sure, she made up that morning and just says in the old voice so you can't argue.

◆ ◆ ◆

The Window is at the edge of the market square, where the east skywalk dips lowest. For about five metres, the glass tube comes all the way down to the ground, so the visitors can see us up close. It's tradition. When the skywalk crowds come through on Tuesdays, the children of Firstfield line up at the Window and breathe on the glass, big foggy breaths, and draw pictures in the fog with their fingers. A sun. A tomato. A face. The visitors go wild for it. Some of them cry.

We do it because our great-great-grandparents did it, and because it's funny, and because it's the only time you get to be close enough to see a visitor's actual face. The glass is cool against your nose and smells faintly of the polish the night guard uses, and if you press your ear to it you can hear them on the other side, muffled, like people talking underwater.

I've been studying their faces since I was eleven. I keep a notebook, a real paper one, and it was Master Aldous who gave it to me.

He'd brought a jar into the schoolhouse that day, with something in it that had far too many legs. The little ones screamed. Tilly climbed onto her bench. Jory tried to let it out. Master Aldous just held the jar up to the window light and made us say every part of it, out loud, one at a time. Feelers. Thorax. The plates on its back. The little hooks on its feet, which I spotted. By the end, the little ones were crowding round the jar with their noses on the glass, and Tilly had named it Gerald.

"There," said Master Aldous, and tapped the lid. "Name it and you can hold it."

Then he gave me the notebook, because of the hooks. So I name the visitors.

I have categories. Tappers read the sign that says DO NOT TAP THE GLASS and then tap the glass, as if the sign were a dare. Double-lookers have extra eyes and look at you twice at the same time. Whisperers think the glass is soundproof. It isn't. Wavers are my favourite. A waver is someone who forgets, for one second, that we're the heritage, and just waves at a boy with a basket. I wave back. Wavers always look startled, and then they look happy, and then they look sort of guilty, all in about two seconds.

I don't know what I'm studying, exactly. Dr. Ommery says that's how most science starts.

Today's Tuesday crowd is a school group from somewhere in the Changed lands. Thirty kids and one teacher. The teacher has four arms, which she uses very efficiently: two for pointing, one for a tablet, and one permanently on the shoulder of a small boy who looks like he bites. The kids have pink gill-slits fluttering on their necks, or fur in patterns like spilled ink, or long climbing fingers. Every one of them is designed. Every one of them is better than us, by some measure somebody once decided mattered. That's what the visitors are. People who went one way, back at the Great Divergence, while we went the other. Gran says they chose to change and we chose to keep, and neither choice was cheap.

Jory is next to me at the Window. Jory's my best friend, and my worst influence, and the fastest runner in Firstfield, which he will tell you himself. He breathes a great fog on the glass and draws a very rude picture of a goat. Then he looks sideways at me, eyebrows up.

"Jory."

"Bet you won't," says Jory. "Bet you won't do the face."

I do the face. The four-armed teacher gives me a look with all three of her eyes.

A gill-girl at the front, about eight, with a pink bow on one gill, is staring past us at the market stalls. "Why are their tomatoes red?"

"Baseline tomatoes," explains the audio guide, from the skywalk speakers, "were red, small, and silent."

"Why don't they sing?"

"Before the Great Improvements, tomatoes did not sing."

The gill-girl looks at our tomatoes for a long time. Her face does something complicated. Then her lip starts to go.

"That's so sad," she says, and bursts into tears.

The teacher steers her away. The whole tube of visitors shuffles on, and they're gone, through the far door at the end of the skywalk.

That door. I've watched it my whole life.

They come in through the near door in the morning and go out through the far door, and then they're gone, and I don't know where. Back to the Changed lands, Master Aldous says. To Moriel, which is the world, which goes on a long way and then stops. Where does it stop, I asked him once, and he said at the edge, and I asked what's past the edge, and he said more edge, and laughed, and then saw I wasn't laughing and said, more quietly, I don't know, Bram. Nobody's ever told me.

I want to know. I've wanted to know since I was small enough to sit on Gran's lap and watch the skywalks pass over. It's not a grand feeling. It's just a small, constant itch, like a splinter you can't find. Every morning the near door. Every evening the far door. And in between, a whole world going on that I'm only ever allowed to see the visitors of.

"Stop doing that," says Jory.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at the door like it owes you money."

◆ ◆ ◆

Old Saul's east rows are a mess. They always are, according to Old Saul.

"Look at this. Look at it," he says, pointing at a perfectly normal tomato plant with his stick. Saul is the oldest man in Firstfield and grows the best tomatoes in it, and he has never once been happy about either fact. "Blight. Bound to be blight. The whole row'll be gone by Thaw."

The rows smell green and peppery, the way only tomato leaves smell, a smell that gets on your fingers and stays there through supper.

"It's a leaf, Saul. It's got a hole in it."

"Look twice," says Saul. He jabs the stick at me. "Nothing's ever as bad as it is the first time you look at it. Said the same when your friend set fire to my grain chute, didn't I? And we only lost the chute."

I look twice. There's a little green caterpillar under the leaf, very pleased with itself. I pick it off and put it on the fence.

"There," says Saul, satisfied, as if he'd found it. "Not blight. Only nearly blight."

He hands me a tomato without looking, the way he's done since I could walk. It's warm from the day and it bursts in my mouth, sharp and sweet and tasting of the whole summer. The gill-girl, I think, will never know what she was crying about.

The sky dome is doing Tuesday weather, which is clear with light cloud. On Thursdays it rains: a fine mist from sprinklers in the ribs of the dome that hiss on all at once, very good for the tomatoes. On Fridays there's a recorded thunderstorm, which Tilly hides from under the table even though it has been the same thunderstorm, with the same three crashes in the same order, every Friday of her life. Above the weather, at night, the dome goes a soft dark blue, and that's all. Nobody's ever said the word stars to me. I found it in a book once, in the old part of the schoolhouse library, a word in a poem: the stars like dust. I asked Master Aldous what stars were, and he said a kind of old weather, and took the book back to fix its spine, and I never saw it again.

◆ ◆ ◆

In the evenings, Dr. Ommery comes to our house to read. She doesn't come every night; she has forty other houses to look in on. But she comes to ours most, because Gran's chest is bad, and because Ma makes the best honey cake in Firstfield, and because — Tilly's theory — I'm her favourite.

Dr. Ommery is our doctor, and our teacher of medicine, and our keeper, which means she's the one person in Firstfield who goes up and down the long stair to the Upper House. That's where the Preserve is run from, behind the door at the top of the meeting house. She's old. Not designed-old, where you choose your wrinkles. Actually old, slow and grey, with reading spectacles on a chain. She smells like tea and the stuff she cleans cuts with. She sits in the chair across the table from mine, and she always asks the same question.

"What did you learn today?"

"That gill-children cry about tomatoes," I say. "And that Saul's caterpillar is nearly blight."

She laughs. Gran laughs into her shawl. Tilly says, "What do you call a tomato that won't —" and Ma says "Bed," and Tilly goes, at a pace of about one step per minute, protesting.

The dome is cooling. If you're quiet you can hear it: the great ribs ticking, one and then another and then three together, tik, tik-tik, all over the sky, like a clock somebody built too big to wind. Ma cuts the honey cake. It's still warm in the middle, and sticky, and it tastes of the bees.

Tonight Dr. Ommery reads from the Heritage Library. It's about four hundred books, curated, which she once told me means chosen. When I asked what happened to the books they didn't choose, she said that's enough for tonight. The story's about a boy who goes to sea on a raft and comes back a man. There are a lot of storms. She reads the storms well. She does a voice for the wind.

"What's past the sea?" I ask, when the boy finally comes home.

"More sea," she says. "And then land."

"And past that?"

"More Moriel." She turns a page she's already read.

"The world is Moriel, and Moriel is the world," says Gran from her chair, like a prayer. That's the first line of the Heritage Primer. Every child in Firstfield learns to read on that sentence. I used to trace it with my finger.

Dr. Ommery looks at Gran, and Gran looks at Dr. Ommery, and something passes between them over my head that I don't have a category for yet.

◆ ◆ ◆

The meeting house bell rings twice on Wednesday morning, which means a Calling.

Everybody goes. You go to a Calling even if you're sick; Gran went to one with a fever once, wrapped in three blankets, and stood the whole time. The meeting house is the oldest building in Firstfield: dark beams, long benches worn smooth as river stones, a smell of beeswax and old wood. On the far wall, above the elders' bench, there are eleven small brass plaques in a row, polished so bright they shine like coins.

REFERENCE No. 1. Called — Retired. REFERENCE No. 2. Called — Retired.

And so on, each with two dates. Every one of them a young person of Firstfield, called up at their coming of age to go to the Upper House as the village's Reference. It's the highest honour there is. The Reference is the reason Firstfield exists. They're the proof, Gran says, the living proof sent up to show that we've kept faith, that we're still what humans were. They go up the long stair and they don't come down, because the work is holy and it takes the rest of your life, and when it's done they're retired, which means honoured, at rest.

After Number Eleven there's a stretch of bare wall. Polished, empty. Four small holes already drilled, in a neat rectangle.

I've never liked looking at those holes. I didn't know why until Wednesday.

Elder Marrow stands up with the letter from the Upper House. He reads it out in his big slow voice, the voice he uses for weddings and funerals and the price of grain.

"The Twelfth Reference of Firstfield," he reads, "is called. Bram, son of Wenna, of the house of Hale."

The whole meeting house turns and looks at me.

And then they're cheering. Really cheering. Jory is thumping me on the back so hard I nearly fall off the bench. Old Saul is saying "Well, well," over and over, which from Saul is practically a speech. Master Aldous has his hand over his heart. Tilly is standing on the bench yelling "THAT'S MY BROTHER, THAT'S MY BROTHER" at people who already know.

Ma takes my hand. She's smiling. She's so proud she's shining with it. But she holds my hand much, much too hard, the way you hold a rope.

Gran doesn't cheer. Gran looks at the four drilled holes, and then at Dr. Ommery, who is at the end of the elders' bench with her face gone the colour of the wall.

◆ ◆ ◆

Below the eleven plaques, in a frame, under glass, there's a page of the Founders' Charter. Old handwriting. Big, curly capitals. I read it while the cheering goes on around me, because it's easier than looking at Ma's face.

Article One: That the Original shall be kept, in honour and in health, that we may never forget what we were.

Articles Two to Thirteen are about grain allowances, roof repairs, and who minds the bees.

Article Fourteen is one line long, and someone has painted over it. Not crossed out. Painted it, on the inside of the glass, in the same pale brown as the meeting house wall, so you have to stand at an angle in the right light to see there's anything there at all. Under the paint, if you squint, you can just make out the shape of the first word. It starts with a big curly C.

That evening, Dr. Ommery comes to read, but she doesn't read. She sits at our table with her tea going cold, a skin forming on it, and when Ma goes out to the bees and Tilly's asleep and Gran is pretending to be, she says to me, quietly:

"Bram. When you were sixteen, you'd have been entitled to the —"

She stops. Just stops, mid-word, like the audio guide does when the power flickers.

"To the what?"

"To a cake," says Dr. Ommery. "A very large cake. That's all." She stands up too fast. Her hands aren't steady. "Goodnight, Bram."

◆ ◆ ◆

On Thursday, for the first time in my life, I go up the long stair.

The Twelfth has to be prepared. That's all the letter said. Dr. Ommery takes me up, and doesn't say a word the whole climb. At the top there's a door, and past the door there's a corridor with soft grey floors that swallow footsteps, and white walls, and it smells like the stuff she cleans cuts with, and nothing, nothing at all, is historically accurate.

She leaves me in a little room to wait. She says she'll be ten minutes. She looks at me before she goes as if she wants to say something enormous, and then she says "Don't touch anything," and goes.

The room has a vent in the wall. Through it, faint, there's a dark, bitter smell of coffee, and voices.

I'm not proud of it. But Master Aldous says the world is mostly made of things nobody tells you, so I kneel by the vent.

"— coming along beautifully," a man is saying. He has a soft, pleased voice. Later I see him walk past the door, and his coat changes colour as he talks: grey to violet to a sort of gold, like a mood. "Thirteen's a much cleaner baseline. Grown from scratch. Fewer… opinions."

"It's not out of the tank for another year," says a woman. The director, I think. I've heard her voice once before, at a Calling, through the speakers.

"Which is why we take the Twelfth now. Three of the grain lines are already off-reference, the board wants fresh original material by Thaw, and a fifteen-year-old's cells are the freshest you'll get. You draw from him the way you drew from the other eleven, until he's no use. Then he's retired. Same as ever."

Draw from him. I think of the little vials of blood Dr. Ommery takes from every child in Firstfield once a year, labelled and carried up the stair. I think of eleven brass plaques, polished like coins.

"And then?" says the director.

"And then Thirteen comes out of the tank, and you have a reference standard you grew, that you own outright. No families. No Callings." A small laugh. "Frankly, Director, the board's been asking for years why we keep the set dressing."

There's a long pause. It's the kind of pause where somebody reaches for a clipboard.

"The village has a charter," says the director.

"The village has a Heritage Board that interprets its charter. Close it once Thirteen's stable. Resettle them, wherever. It's all very humane."

"Ommery will make a fuss."

"Ommery," says the man, "can be reassigned."

When Dr. Ommery comes back, I'm sitting exactly where she left me, with my hands in my lap. She takes three little vials of my blood, labels them in neat handwriting, and doesn't look at me once. The needle is cold, and then it isn't.

◆ ◆ ◆

That night I don't sleep at all.

I lie in the loft above the kitchen and listen to my family breathe. Tilly snuffling, like a small engine that's run out of fuel. Ma turning over and over. And under it all the dome, which hums. You don't notice it until you've got nothing to do but notice it, and then it's all there is: a long, low, endless note, like the whole world is thinking one thought very hard and never finishing it. Somewhere up in the dark, the cleaning drone whirs along the glass, and now and then a rib ticks, cooling, as if the sky were counting.

I think about eleven plaques. I think about Firstfield closed and resettled, wherever, Saul's tomatoes ploughed under, the meeting house empty, the Window with nobody behind it. I think about Tilly at sixteen, getting a cake.

And under all of it, I'm ashamed to say, I think about the far door.

Because there's an outside. There always was. There's a board, and a man in a colour-changing coat, and grain lines, and money, and a whole world the size of all that, and nobody ever told us. It's terrible, and it's there, and the splinter I've had my whole life suddenly isn't a splinter any more. It's a door standing open, and the only way to save anything — Firstfield, Tilly, Saul's tomatoes — is to go through it and find somebody out there who'll listen.

Here's the thing I've never told anyone: in the front half of my notebook, behind the visitors, I've been keeping track of the cleaning drone.

It does the dome every night. Four hours and eleven minutes, starting at the top and spiralling down the inside of the great glass, a fat silver beetle on suction feet. At the bottom of the spiral it docks at a maintenance hatch in the outer wall, behind Old Saul's east rows, and for ninety seconds the hatch props open while it empties its little bag of dust and pollen and leaf. Then the hatch shuts. Then the drone goes to sleep.

I've timed it a thousand times. For three years. I told myself it was science. It was never science. It was a door I wasn't brave enough to call a door.

Knowing a thing and doing it are different animals.

When I come down the stairs at three in the morning, in my coat with my notebook in the pocket, past the mother crock in its dark corner, Gran is awake. She's in her chair by the dead stove, wrapped in her shawl, and she doesn't seem surprised to see me.

We look at each other for a long time.

"If you're going to the privy," Gran says finally, "take a coat."

"I've got a coat."

"So you have." She reaches into her shawl and holds something out. It's a little paper twist of seeds. Red tomato seeds, from Saul's best plants. I can tell from the way they rattle. "In case wherever you're going," she says, "hasn't got proper ones."

I can't say anything. I take the seeds.

"The root doesn't see the fruit," says Gran, "and the fruit forgets the root, and the tree goes on anyway." She closes her eyes. "Go and see, then. Somebody in this family ought to."

I kiss her forehead. It's papery and warm, and smells of woodsmoke and old wool. I don't look up at the loft where Ma and Tilly are sleeping, because if I do, I'll stay.

At 3:48 I'm crouched in the east rows behind the tomato frames, in the dark, the leaf smell all over my hands, and somewhere above me the drone is on its last low loops, a slow green star sliding down the inside of the dome.

At 3:51 the audio guide wakes up.

I didn't know there was a motion sensor in the east rows. There is. The ribs of the dome click, and the warm dead voice says, at full volume, to the whole sleeping village:

"Here we see the Baseline Human at —"

I freeze.

"— at —"

There's no script for 3:51 in the morning. I can hear it searching. There's a long, horrible hiss of dead air.

"— at leisure," it decides.

Up on the east skywalk, a light swings round. The night guard: a big, slow man with one lamp-eye, an old-fashioned modification, very bright. He walks the skywalks twice a night and mostly spends them eating. The beam slides over the thatched roofs, over the market square, over the empty Window, over the tomato frames.

I'm in a tomato frame. I'm trying very hard to be a tomato.

The beam stops.

I can hear my heart. I can hear the drone. I can hear the little crinkle of whatever the guard's eating.

"Stupid thing," the guard says, to nobody. "Leisure."

The beam swings away.

At 3:59 the drone reaches the bottom of its spiral and the maintenance hatch slides open with a sigh, and cold air comes in. Real air, from outside, and it smells like nothing I've ever smelled in my life.

Ninety seconds.

I go.

The hatch is exactly as wide as my shoulders. I find this out the hard way, at ninety seconds minus four. Head through, fine. One arm, fine. Then my shoulders hit the frame on both sides at once and stick, and I'm half in and half out of the world, and the drone, which has never in four hours and eleven minutes met a boy in its hatch, makes a confused little chirp and starts emptying its dust bag on my head.

Eighty seconds. Seventy.

Hands first, worry after.

I don't think about Ma. I just hear her, the way you hear a song you've known all your life. I get my free hand flat against the outside wall and I breathe all the way out, the way we do at the Window for the visitors, till I'm flat and small and empty, and I push. The frame takes a strip of skin off both shoulders. My coat tears. Dust goes in my mouth, dry and grey and tasting of a whole dome's worth of nothing.

And then I'm through, and falling, and landing on my hands in something soft and cold and alive, and behind me the hatch sighs shut.

I sit there in the dark, spitting out dust, with a paper twist of tomato seeds clutched so tight in my fist it's damp.

I'm outside.

◆ ◆ ◆

And outside is loud.

Not sound, though there's sound too. Everything is loud. The air tastes like fifteen things I have no names for, and one of them might be purple. There's a sweetness like Ma's honey cake, and under it something sharp, like Dr. Ommery's cleaning stuff, and under that a deep green musk that seems to come from the ground itself, as if the dirt is breathing out.

And the sound. Under the dome, night is one note. Out here it's a thousand: a chirring in the grass that rises and falls like breathing, something far off going tok... tok... like a spoon on a pot, a high thin whistle from the trees that's answered, a beat later, from somewhere else. A wind I didn't order moves through all of it, cold on my torn shoulders, and nobody turns it off.

The grass is warm. That's the first thing my hands learn. And it's moving very slightly toward me, the way a dog leans in when you're petting it. When I lift my palm, the blades lift a little after it, and settle.

I stand up. The dome is behind me, enormous, a soap bubble on the hill, glowing faintly from the skywalk lights. From out here you can see what it is. A bowl with a village in it. Two hundred people, asleep, and my mother and my sister in a loft, and Gran by a dead stove, pretending she didn't just watch me go.

I don't look at it long.

A tree has fur. Short and dense and silvery, like the belly of a cat, and when I put my hand on it, it's warm, and something inside it shifts toward my hand. I snatch my hand back and the fur ripples, offended.

The flowers are worse. They're huge, big as dinner plates, pale in the dark, on stalks as tall as me. As I walk past the first one, it turns to watch me. I stop. It stops. I take a step. It turns. And then (I swear this is true) the one next to it turns too, before I've even got to it, as if the first one told it. By the end of that patch there's a hundred pale faces in the dark, tracking me, like a skywalk full of visitors.

I almost laugh. Out here, I'm still the heritage.

Something with too many knees steps out of the trees. Deer-tall, but its legs fold in places legs shouldn't, three, four joints each, so it moves like a folding ruler. It has a ring of eyes all the way round its long, gentle head, like a crown. It considers me with about half of them.

It decides I'm not worth the effort, and folds itself away between the trees.

I've never been so grateful to be boring.

I walk. The ground goes from soft to rooty to a springy moss that sighs when you step on it, a long hhhhh, so I can hear exactly where I've been for twenty paces behind me. There's a vine with pods that split and smoke, very gently, in sweet little puffs. I don't touch it. I don't touch anything after that.

The first dawn comes up somewhere behind me and I hardly notice it. I walk all that day. I walk until the dome is a soap bubble behind the hills, and then until it's gone. I walk until my feet bleed. Baseline feet, as the audio guide would point out, lack the calloused plating of the modern — and then I stop thinking in her voice, because I'm crying, and in fifteen years of narrating us she never once mentioned that we could.

When the light goes, I sit on a root and cry until I'm finished. For Tilly's terrible joke, which I'll never hear again. For Ma's hand, too tight. For Gran. The moss sighs under me the whole time, like it's crying with me, which helps more than it should.

◆ ◆ ◆

The thing that hunts me finds me at the end of the second night.

Dawn out here isn't on a timer. I missed the first one, walking. This one I watch: it comes up slowly and from the side, grey and then pink and then a pale gold I've never seen on the dome, and the whole forest changes colour with it. The furry trees go copper. The flowers close into fists. And in the quiet as they close, I hear it.

A clicking. Low down. Off to my left.

It's long and low, like a dog someone has stretched, and its back is armoured in overlapping grey plates like a slate roof. It hasn't got a face, exactly. Its head is a smooth wedge, and dangling in front of it on a stalk of its own flesh there's a little glowing bulb, soft blue, swaying, the exact colour of the tomatoes in the pictures.

The clicking comes from its plates, rubbing together as it breathes.

Its head splits open sideways, the whole wedge hinging at the middle like a book, and inside there are a great many teeth. A smell rolls out of it, hot and meaty, like the bin behind the bakehouse in summer.

I run.

I've never run, not properly. Not like Jory. My legs don't know how, and my one set of lungs is on fire within twenty strides. Behind me the clicking speeds up into a rattle, and the moss goes hhhhh-hhhhh-hhhhh under my feet, telling it exactly where I am.

The moss is telling it. I veer onto rock, and the sighing stops, and for three seconds the rattling stops too. Then it starts again. Closer. It doesn't need the moss.

I scramble up a boulder and turn around, because I've got nowhere left to run, and it comes out of the trees below me and stops, swaying, and the blue bulb swings round to point at me like a compass needle. I've got a spoon. That's the whole of my weapons. I hold it up anyway.

It's the worst thing I have ever seen, and I am going to die looking at it.

Look twice, says Old Saul.

Not out loud. Not really. But I hear it as clear as if he were standing on the boulder next to me with his stick, scowling at a tomato he's decided is doomed. Nothing's ever as bad as it is the first time you look at it. Look twice.

So I look twice.

The thing's head isn't pointed at me. It's pointed a little to the side, at nothing. It hasn't got eyes. It's the bulb that's pointed at me. The bulb, and only the bulb. When I shift left, it swings left. When I shift right, it swings right. It isn't following my sound. It isn't following my smell. It's following something coming off me.

I'm hot. I've been running, and I'm baseline, which apparently means inefficient, and I'm pouring out heat like Gran's stove.

Dr. Ommery read me a book about snakes once. Some snakes can see warmth. They don't need eyes. They feel the heat of a mouse in the dark.

Down past the boulder there's a ravine, a crack in the ground, and the sound of water at the bottom.

I throw the spoon. Not at the thing. Away, hard, into the trees on the other side, and it clatters off a trunk, and the thing's head jerks toward the sound for one half-second. One half-second is all I've got. I jump.

It's further down than it looked. I hit a slope of loose shale, slide, catch my left leg on something sharp — feel it open, a hot bright line down the calf — and splash into a stream so cold it knocks every thought out of my head.

The bottom of the stream is mud. Black, thick, icy mud. It smells of rot and iron and old leaves.

I roll in it. I shove it on my face, my neck, my arms, over the gash in my leg. It feels like being buried. I lie in the shallows with only my nose out, coated grey-black from head to foot, shaking so hard my teeth click. Click click click, like the thing.

Up on the lip of the ravine, the bulb appears. Soft blue. Swaying.

It swings back and forth. Back and forth across the stream. Across me. Across the stream.

It doesn't stop on me. I'm cold now. I'm mud. I'm nothing it can see.

After a very long time, the bulb withdraws. The rattling fades into the trees.

Not blight, says Saul, satisfied, in the back of my head. Only nearly blight.

I laugh, there in the freezing mud. I can't help it. It comes out cracked and wet and it hurts, and I'm still laughing as I crawl out of the stream, because I'm not sure I can stand, and follow the water down the ravine, leaving a thin red thread behind me.

◆ ◆ ◆

There's a pond where the stream widens. Still, and green-gold at the edges in the morning light, with reeds that lean away from me, unlike the grass. I find out later that's important. I kneel at it to drink, because I'm so thirsty I don't care any more what touches what. The water tastes of stone and something like mint, and it's so cold it aches in my teeth.

The pond looks back.

Not a reflection, though my reflection is there too: a mud-grey boy with a split lip and a scared face. Something under the surface. Soft, green-gold, unfolding like a hand made of moss. It rises toward me slowly, the way the flowers turned. It doesn't hurry. It has never had to hurry for anything.

It touches my wrist.

It's warm, warmer than me, warmer than the grass. And it doesn't so much grip as agree with my skin, all at once, everywhere it touches, like two hands that already know each other.

A voice that isn't in my ears says: Oh.

I should pull away. I don't. I'm too tired, and the warmth is going up my arm, and it's the first warm thing that's held me since Gran's forehead.

Then, kindly, the way you'd talk to a lost child or an old tomato:

You're very simple, aren't you.

"I've been told," I say. My voice comes out cracked.

You're bleeding. The warmth finds my leg somehow, though it's only touching my wrist. Slowly. Inefficiently.

"Is there an efficient way to bleed?"

There's a better way not to. A pause, like it's turning me over in its hand. Your blood is old. The oldest kind I've ever tasted. Everything else out here has been changed so many times it tastes like noise. You taste like a — a feeling, not a word, something like a first note, or a clean page — a beginning.

"Is that bad?"

It's interesting. Most things aren't.

"What are you?"

Hungry, mostly. Small. Curious. The warmth curls round my wrist like a bracelet. I've been in this pond a long time, waiting for something worth being curious about. You're cold. Your leg is open. There's an animal up there with a blue light that will come back when you warm up again, and you will warm up again, because you're alive, and that's what alive things do.

Somewhere up on the ridge, something clicks.

I could fix that, it says.

"Fix what?"

All of it. The leg. The cold. The lungs. The simple.

I think of the eleven brass plaques, and the four drilled holes. I think of the Original shall be kept. I think of Ma saying breathe properly, and of what properly will mean, after.

"Would I still be me?" I ask.

The warmth goes still, as if it's actually considering the question, which is more than anyone in the Upper House ever did.

Who's that? it says.

And I open my mouth to answer the way you answer in Firstfield. Bram, son of Wenna, of the house of Hale. Brother of Tilly. Grandson of Gran. But they're two days behind me under a glass sky, and I don't know if a person who's left can still say whose he is.

So I don't answer. I hold the seeds in one fist, and the sun comes up gold through the reeds, and up on the ridge the clicking comes closer.

And God help me, I'm fifteen and alone and the grass is leaning in, so I say, "Okay."
