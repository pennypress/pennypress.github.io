Chapter Sixteen: The Better Sum

THE UPPER HOUSE · HIGH GARDENS · A dinner for the Examiner. Roast white root of the Spill, brown butter · Clear broth of the Undercroft · Salt-plums of the north-west coast, with river trout · Bread of the Pale, sector 9 · Clover honey of the Hiveplains, set as a custard, with moonpear.

— menu card, cream board, printed in grey; beneath it in ink, in a small upright hand: Every course is from a place you have cost me. I thought you should taste what you've been defending. L.V.

The skiff went up the ridge into the cloud and the world shut like a book.

For a long minute there was nothing in the windows but white, with enormous trees going past in it like people in a corridor. Sedge sat with her knees together and her right wrist pressed flat under her left hand, the way you'd hold down a bird.

"It's warm," she said. "In here. It shouldn't be warm."

"They heat the cabins."

"No. Her." She lifted her hand off her wrist and put it back. "She's never been up this high. She wants to look."

Then we came out of the top.

I have worked on a good many worlds and I had never seen a cloud from above at night. The full moon stood clear of the Pale behind us, and under it the cloud lay over the whole ridge as flat and white as milk in a dish, so still that the tallest crowns stuck up through it like black rocks out of a sea. And out of that sea, white and smooth and much bigger than they had looked from the grain, stood the towers: four of them, slender, slightly curved, like the bones of a hand somebody had buried up to the knuckles. At the top of the nearest a room of glass was lit gold, like a lantern set on a post.

Sedge put her face to the window and forgot to hold her wrist.

"You can see the stars to the bottom," she said. "Down to the white. There's nothing in the way."

"Never be the tallest thing on a hill," I said, mostly to myself.

"What?"

"A Survey cook. Be the second tallest, next to the tallest." I looked at the gold room. "He's built himself the tallest thing on the tallest hill on Moriel and put the dinner table in it."

"Is that bad?"

"It's lightning-shaped," I said, and she laughed, which was what I'd wanted.

◆ ◆ ◆

There was a narrow gallery round the glass room, railed, with round fan housings breathing out warm air that smelled of cedar, and a door, and three Associates beside the door with their hands folded in front of them like ushers at a wedding. Company grey, silver hair combed flat, the small enamel numbers at their lapels: 15, 17, and 22.

"Examiner," said Pell-22. "Good evening. You have my launcher."

"It's evidence."

"Of what?"

"Of you," I said, and went in.

The glass room was warm and gold and smelled of cedar and cooked ink, the smell of every office that has ever kept a file on somebody. A long table with a white cloth, set for three; on every side, the sea of cloud.

And at the far end of the table, getting up, a man in a long coat.

I had built him in my head for days, the way you build a suspect, out of what he leaves behind, and I had built him taller. Lisle Varro was perhaps sixty, not much bigger than me, with grey hair cut close to a round skull and a plain, pleasant, considering face, the face of the clerk you'd trust with your mother's pension. His voice was the one off the disc: soft, pleased, unhurried. His hands were square and very clean, with a pen callus on the middle finger of the right, and when he came round the table to us he held them a little away from his sides, open, the way you approach a horse.

The coat was the thing. It was long and high-collared and plain in cut, and it was the colour of good honey, and as he looked at Sedge it went a shade warmer, the gold coming up through it like light through a held hand.

Sedge stared at it.

"It's a company line," said Varro, as if she'd asked. "Bred for beekeepers in the Hiveplains, originally, to read the skin and show the hive whether you were frightened. It reads mine." He smiled at her. "I've never been able to hide anything from it. I stopped trying in my thirties. It's been very good for my negotiating."

"Who wrote you?" said Sedge.

He didn't miss a beat. He'd read every file I'd filed, which meant he had read three hundred and three answers to that question in Highwater's own words.

"My mother," said Varro. "And twenty years of the Moriel Bioreference Index. Will you sit down? There's pudding."

Sedge looked at me. I could see her decide that a man who knew about the pudding was more dangerous than she'd planned for.

She sat down.

◆ ◆ ◆

He fed us our route.

That was the joke of the menu, and he watched us find it out course by course with the coat going faintly gold at the cuffs. The fat white roots of the Spill in brown butter, tasting of wet bread and pennies. A broth as clear as window glass that tasted of cold stone and the Undercroft's dark, so that my nails, under the cloth, glowed a little in sympathy. Salt-plums and trout. Then the bread.

Sedge had eaten everything as if someone might come and take it. She stopped at the bread.

It was a good loaf to look at, brown, the crust split, from Pale grain, sector 9, where the harvesters had gone into the ditch. She broke it. She smelled it. Her right wrist, on the white cloth, came up faintly green-gold, like a lamp turned a quarter-turn.

Varro saw it. His hands went still on the cloth.

"I'll come to that," I said.

"Please do," said Varro. "I've been waiting all evening." He poured himself water. I had my flask beside my plate, and when I unscrewed the cap the smell of tar and woodsmoke and burnt raisins went down the table and met the cedar coming the other way, and he breathed it in with an odd, appreciative expression, like a man smelling a bonfire from a train.

"Tell me about the grazers," he said instead. "My drones lose them in the cloud."

So Sedge told him, because he asked her and not me: the boy feeding seed mash into a downed grazer's mouth like a mother bird, and the burp. His face did nothing at all while she told it. His coat went the colour of a fire's heart.

"Thirty-one roof claims," he said, when she'd done. "Do you know how the farms file them? I looked it up this morning. Damage by falling livestock. The Heritage Service pays out on the grazers as if somebody owned them." He turned his glass a little on the cloth. "Nobody does. They're the only thing on this ridge I haven't got a claim on. I find I rather like them for it."

"Livestock," said Sedge, with deep scorn.

"Legally," said Varro. "On a roof."

◆ ◆ ◆

He waited until the plates had gone. A Pell took them, without a word, and stood back by the door with the others.

Then Varro put his clean square hands flat on the white cloth either side of the place where his plate had been, and looked at me, and the coat cooled, slowly, from gold to the dull dark of old brass.

"You think I'm a villain because I put a number on a child," he said. "Fine. Let me put the number on the table for you, because someone has to.

"Nine million, one hundred and six thousand, four hundred and twelve. That was this morning's count of engineered lives on this planet: grain and bees and trees and fish and grazers and the people who were made to work among them. None of them asked to be made. Every one of them is drifting, a little each generation, a copy of a copy, because nothing living holds still without a reference to hold it to. I have watched it happen. My mother kept bees in the Hiveplains, line twenty. When I was nine I watched them forget what the flowers smelled like. You've seen what that looks like now. I saw it start."

His hands didn't move.

"Your village, sweet as it is, is the reference. Eleven young people in eight generations, a twelfth this year, and in return I can stop the drift for everybody else. You will tell me the old way was a covenant and that it was honoured. I'll tell you it was honoured with a plaque. I'm not asking you to like the sum. I'm asking you to say which of the nine million you will tell, to their faces, that the calibration will not be done because you could not bear the arithmetic."

The fans hummed. Somewhere below us in the white, too faint to be sure of, a grazer piped.

"If you have a better sum," said Lisle Varro, "I'll fund it. If you have only a better feeling, you have not answered me."

He hadn't raised his voice once. And I sat there with the taste of the broth still in my mouth and found that my hands, in my lap, had gone cold.

Not because he was wrong. Because I had heard it before.

Someone has to put the number on the table. A different table. A tired man at the end of a long day, saying it kindly. It went through me the way cold water goes through a dry field, straight down to the old low place, and I felt everything else I knew start to run that way after it.

Fallow taught me to sit down first. I was sitting. I put my hand on the sailcloth bundle beside my chair and held on to it until the cold stopped.

◆ ◆ ◆

"I'll answer you in arithmetic," I said. "Since you ask."

I unwrapped the survey on the white cloth. Wheat-coloured boards; the title pressed into them; the inky thumb on the first page. I turned it round so that it faced him and opened it to the preface, and I didn't read it to him. I'd learned that from him, in a way, off a grey disc on a doorstep: you let a good sentence do its own work.

He read it. Let no child of ours ever be kept for a copy. I watched his eyes go back to the start and read it again.

"Your sum has two terms," I said. "The drift, and the reference. The drift's real. I've walked it for twenty-two days and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. Your mistake is the second term. You've assumed the reference has to be a living child, because a living child is the cheapest clean copy you could find. It isn't the only one. It was never meant to be any. And the tank in your annex doesn't change the sum, Mr Varro. It only takes the asking out of it."

I turned to the maps. Seven ponds across the face of the world, each with its reeds leaning away from a little inked sun.

"The founders built seven archives. In each lives a partner: a Librarian, they called it, built to hold the first copy of everything they brought. The grain. The crock. Your mother's bees and the flowers they forgot. The village was only ever meant to remember the old ways by choosing them. Not to be the copy." I put my finger on the bearings. "Your Register filed the ponds as lapsed wetlands, because nobody went to read them any more. This morning I joined its seven to her seven. They match to a stride. It's in my Chief Examiner's tray. The reinstatement's pending."

"A book," said Varro gently, "is a lovely thing. I have a great many."

"Yes," I said. "So I brought the pond."

Sedge, who had promised me on a rise in the Pale that she would say what she said at dawn and nothing else, and who had never once done such a thing in her life, put the broken bread down on the cloth in front of him.

"It's tired," she said.

He looked at the bread. Then at her lit wrist.

"Right at the bottom," said Sedge. "The bottom page. All the bread on this table's gone tired at the bottom, like the grazers' bellies were, before Bram." She frowned at it, listening to something none of us could hear. "She's got the first one. The first crock. She says it's warm." She looked up at him with the frank contempt of the young for an adult who has missed something obvious. "It's in a pond. It's been in a pond for two hundred years. You could've just asked."

The coat went a colour I hadn't got a name for. Something between slate and the blue at the bottom of a flame.

"What did it cost you?" he asked her. Not me. "Saying yes."

"It was freezing," said Sedge. "And I missed breakfast. And I'm not on my own any more." She considered. "Neither's she."

He was quiet for a long time. When he spoke again it was to me, and it was the voice of a man going down a column with his finger.

"Seven partners. You have one. Where do the other six come from?"

"From six people saying yes."

"And if they don't?"

"Then you'll have to ask nicely. You found eleven children in eight generations without asking any of them. I expect you can find six who'll be asked."

"Calibration from a pond is slower than a draw."

"Yes."

"How much slower?"

"I don't know yet. Slower. You get to keep the children."

"The drift doesn't wait for your yet, Examiner."

"No. It waited two hundred years for somebody to read the first page." I put my hand flat on the open book, beside his. "You asked which of the nine million I'll tell, to their faces. I'll tell all of them. I'll tell them the founders did the sum before any of us were born and wrote the answer in the front of the book. And signed it." I turned the page back to the preface, to Maud Ferrier, surveyor, for the Library. Witnessed by all at the Landing. "That isn't a feeling, Mr Varro. That's a better sum. Signed. You said you'd fund it."

He looked at the signature for a long time. His hands lay either side of the book, and the pen callus on the right one rubbed, once, slowly, against his thumb.

Then he laughed. It was small and dry and entirely unhappy.

"I did say that," said Lisle Varro. "I put it on a disc. I'm told I keep my word." The coat had gone to an ash grey with a faint warmth somewhere deep in it, like a banked fire. "It would be very convenient for me just now not to."

He held out his hand, palm up.

I took a blank from the pad in my inside pocket, the paper kind that doesn't need charging, and wrote it for him plainly, with the time, the way Fallow taught me to write anything that might one day be argued over. I undertake to fund the waking of the seven ponds of the Library of Moriel, with willing partners, in place of the draw. I turned it round and laid my pencil on it.

He read it twice. He changed nothing. He signed it, L. Varro, small and upright, and pushed it back across the cloth with one finger as if it had bitten him.

"There," he said. "You've cost me something I can learn from. I hope you're pleased."

"Not yet," I said. "Ask me when it's done."

I folded the undertaking into the pocket over my heart, with Sedge's yes.

The chime came while the Pells were bringing in the pudding.

Two notes, from a grey disc set into the cloth by Varro's right hand, and the pudding stopped in the doorway on its silver tray: a pale custard trembling in a dish, with slices of moonpear round it glowing faintly in the shadow of the rim. Sedge's whole face changed at the sight of it.

Varro touched the disc and read what came up in it. I watched his hands, because I had told Sedge to.

They didn't move at all.

The coat went white. Not silver, not cloud: the flat chalk white of a wall, from the collar down, as if something had been drained out of it.

"Well," he said. He read it aloud, in the same soft voice, as if it were a menu. "Board of Varro-Tandem Bioreference. Resolution, nine fifty-two pm. The executive's undertaking of this evening exceeds his delegated authority and is set aside. Recovery of materials to proceed: archival reader, juvenile, with contents. The Examiner to be detained as a witness to the recovery pending counsel." He put the disc face down. "I have waited twenty years for someone to cost me something. They waited eleven minutes."

"They can't set aside your signature," I said. "Only your authority."

"Yes. You'll find that's a distinction they've been paying lawyers to ignore since before you were born." He stood. He took the survey off the cloth, closed it, wrapped it in its sailcloth with his clean hands, and gave it back to me. "This is yours. Evidence, I believe."

The Pells had come away from the door.

Sedge was on her feet. The pudding stood in the doorway on its tray, abandoned, wobbling gently.

"I gave you my word," said Varro, "that the boy and the Proxy would be safe below the cloud. That one's still mine. I'll keep it." He looked at Sedge, and at her wrist, and something went across his face that his coat didn't have time to show. "I'm sorry, my dear. It isn't personal."

"That's theirs," said Sedge furiously, with her chin at the Pells. "Get your own."

He went out through the inner door and up a stair, towards the terrace above us, and didn't look back.

Pell-17 reached for Sedge's right wrist.

She had two fingers in her mouth before he got there, and the whistle went out of her, Highwater's whistle, the one for the grey boat, long-short-long, so high and hard it rang off the glass on every side and went out into the night through the fan housings and came back off the other towers from four directions at once.

Pell-17 took her wrist. The whistle stopped halfway.

And I stood there with the survey in my arms and three Associates between me and the door, and I was so angry I could taste it, like a coin under the tongue.

◆ ◆ ◆

They were very polite about it. They always are.

They took Sedge's knife and logged it. They took my warrant and gave it back again, because Pell-22 read it twice and could find nothing in it that said they had to stand aside from any of the doors it opened. My slate said NO RELAY: the towers were a private station above the cloud, and the Register, which reaches every drawer on Moriel, did not reach the room I was standing in. They left me my flask. Pell-15 sniffed it and decided it was a beverage.

"Transfer is at first light," said Pell-22. "Level nine. The young lady will be quite comfortable. The draw-room chairs are very good."

Sedge sat down on the floor with her back against the glass and her knees up, and began, silently, to cry with rage.

I looked at the door to the gallery. Glass, in a white frame, with a small dark plate beside it at hand height and a hum under it: a magnetic latch, the kind that holds as long as the current does. I'd fitted a hundred of them on Survey stores. Every one of them is built to fail open, because a door that stays locked in a fire is a coffin, and even companies have to sign the fire code.

Two hundred volts would do it. Or a short. A compliance wand has a brass cap.

I filed that.

Then I looked at three Associates, the door, the drop beyond the rail, the girl on the floor, the pudding where somebody had finally set it down on the end of the table, and I did the sum, and every move I had ended with me on the floor and Sedge on level nine. My hands were still cold. And under the cold, under all of it, the old low place in me was still filling, someone has to, Pastern, the whole evening running downhill into it the way rain finds a ditch.

You can't do a sum with a head like that. You can't see the board.

I pulled out a chair from the table, and turned it round, and sat down on it, slowly, the way you sit on any chair you don't know.

"What are you doing?" said Sedge, through her teeth.

"Sitting down first," I said, and shut my eyes.

◆ ◆ ◆

I had never done the Reading on myself. You aren't supposed to. The Manual has a line about it, in the chapter nobody reads, to the effect that an Examiner who turns the discipline on her own recollection will find no drawers there, only weather, and is advised to stop.

I went in by the oak door anyway, because it's the only door I have. Iron-bound. Beeswax and cold stone. My palm on it.

There was no hall behind it.

There was a moor.

Open ground under a low sky, grey-green to every horizon, smelling of every first morning on every world I've surveyed: wet earth, crushed stems, somebody's cook fire. And crossed with paths. Thousands, from a faint parting a fox might have made once to tracks worn to bare earth by years of feet.

I knew what they were before I'd taken a step. Fallow had said it to me once in the back office, years ago, over a pasty, and I hadn't understood him: Records are drawers, Pastern. Heads aren't. In a head, the thing you remember is the way you went to it.

A path is what's left where something has been walked. Walk it twice and it's easier the third time. Walk two together often enough and they wear into one. Nobody decides any of it. The feet decide.

Here was the tea: a short, bright, scalding track, and running hard beside it, worn into the same rut, Big Imre's coffee pot going end over end into the dark. Here was chair first, which went everywhere; half the paths on the moor had a stretch of it in them somewhere. Here was a thin new track, the grass only bruised, from a red-stemmed seedling to a notched stick to a reed with eight knots.

And then I felt the ground tilt.

Everything on that moor ran one way. Not the paths: the water. It had started to rain in the Reading, a fine grey drizzle, and every drop that fell ran off the grass into the nearest path, and every path ran downhill into the next, and all of them, every one, ended up going the same way, down and down, toward the low place in the middle of the moor that I had felt open at the dinner table.

I walked down to it, because that is what you do with a Discrepancy.

It wasn't a path any more. It was a gully. A sunken lane worn shoulder-deep into the moor, steep-sided, raw at the edges, with water running in the bottom of it, brown, from every direction at once. You couldn't stand on that moor and not feel it pulling. Varro's voice ran into it. So did Halloran's, I've read your file. So did a kind, tired surveyor on a brown river world signing a terrace sound. So did every closed case I had ever looked at sideways. So did my own name, said by anyone, in a certain tone.

At the bottom of the gully stood a room. A plain office at the end of a long day, the lamp lit, the rain on the window. A desk. A file on the desk, mine, thick, and across the front of it in another hand, No discrepancy. And Fallow, getting up from the desk on his bad knees, who looked at me with great kindness and great tiredness and said one word.

Pastern.

That's all. That's the shape of it. A report I was right about; a teacher I trusted, who signed it closed over me. In the third spring the lower town flooded, and nobody drowned, because the settlers had read their own trees and moved; and four hundred families lost their homes. I asked for the hardest posting there was. I have not spoken to him since. He taught me everything I was using to stand there.

I knew the room very well. I had been coming here for seven years.

What I had not seen, until I stood in it with the Reading on, was the ledger.

It lay open on the windowsill, a fat account book, every page in my hand. Day such-and-such: he didn't look at me when he said it. Day such-and-such: he'd have known about the river. Day such-and-such: Halloran said Pastern in the same voice, and it was the same. Each time I had come down the gully, I had added a line. The principal was a few hours on a wet evening seven years ago. The rest was interest, compounding, and the gully was as deep as it was because I'd been digging it.

The rain came down. The water ran past my boots, brown, carrying everything on the moor with it.

This part Fallow didn't teach me. I had it later out of a book, the way you learn anything after the person who should have told you is no longer someone you speak to. When you walk a memory, really walk it, with your eyes open, it goes soft under you for a while, the way a cut bank does in the wet. While it's soft, it can be laid differently. Then it sets again, and what sets is what you walked last.

On the Survey we re-cut ditches in the wet, never the dry. In the dry the bank's like iron and you'll break your spade. You wait for the rain, when the water's already moving, and you open a new channel with one stroke, and the water does the rest.

I was standing in the rain, at the bottom of my own ditch, with the bank going soft.

I didn't fill it in. You can't; I'd have had to take out every path that ran through it, and every one of them was something he had given me. Chair first. Nobody runs from a man eating lunch. Paper doesn't need charging. The oak door I had come in by. The moor itself.

So I stood still and let the room be what it was. A tired man, wrong. Not a wicked one. The lamp, the rain, the file. Four hundred families, which was true, and which I would carry, and which would never be smaller than it was. And nobody drowned, which was also true, and which I had stopped putting in when I told it.

And from somewhere up on the moor, not loud, in the wrong place entirely, a voice came down the gully on the wind. Hoarse, a village voice, a boy's, with a cough kept in its pocket: on the bank of the Fifth Pond that dawn, with his arm round a crying girl, a whole life ago.

It doesn't go. But it gets to be yours. Then it's not so much.

I went to the windowsill.

I didn't close the account. I'm not there yet, and I won't pretend I am, not even on paper, not even to myself in the Reading, where paper weighs what it should. I took the pencil and I wrote one more line at the foot of the last page, plainly, with the time, the way he taught me:

Day 22, late. Interest: none further. O.P.

Then I walked back up the gully, slowly, the whole length of it, so that the last thing set in that soft ground was me walking up and not down.

At the top I looked back. It was still there. It will always be there. But the rain was running a little differently on the moor already, finding its own ways across, some of it down that new bright track toward the seedlings and the knotted reed, some of it toward a doorstep with a brass disc in it, some of it nowhere in particular, the way rain does on open ground when nothing is pulling it.

I left by the door I'd come in by. My palm stuck to it with the cold.

◆ ◆ ◆

When I opened my eyes I was shaking so hard the chair legs tapped on the floor, and I had a headache like a drawer slammed on my fingers, and I was hungrier than I had been since the Pale. The moon had moved a hand's width over the cloud.

Sedge was staring at me.

"You were gone," she said. "Your nails were going. You said something."

"What did I say?"

"Interest." She wiped her nose on her borrowed sleeve. "I thought you'd gone mad from the dinner."

There was a basket of the Pale bread still on the table. Tired at the bottom, every loaf. I ate two rolls, one after the other, and drank a capful of tea so hot it took the roof off my mouth, and then a third roll, and the cold went out of my hands.

The pudding sat at the end of the table, untouched, a skin forming on it. Sedge looked at it with an expression of deep personal betrayal.

"Don't," I said. "It's the only thing in here they haven't logged."

Then I stood up and went down the room to the door, where the three of them stood, because the board was suddenly very clear, and I needed all three of them looking at me.

I put one hand flat on the end of the long table and talked to him about the law, which I can do for a very long time. Pell-22 answered every point courteously and wrote it all down; the other two listened with the glaze of men who have been in a great many meetings. I wasn't trying to win. I was trying to be the most interesting thing in the room.

Behind me, very faintly, something tapped on the glass. Tk.

I didn't turn round. I watched Sedge, in the dark of the window over Pell-22's shoulder, because the glass gives you back the room at night if you know how to look.

She turned her head. And her face, which had been wet and furious for an hour, opened into a grin.

◆ ◆ ◆

I let my eyes go to the reflection, slowly, the way you look at a bird you don't want to frighten.

Down at the foot of the glass, by Sedge's shoulder, where the gallery floor met the edge of the dark, a pair of hands held on to the edge. Long hands. Grey, the pads dull and ridged. And between them, coming up out of the cloud, a face with eyes that caught the gold of the room and gave it back silver, like a beast's at the edge of a fire.

It took me a heartbeat to know him. I knew the way he held his head.

Then a pair of polished company shoes came round the curve of the gallery above him and stopped. A hand with a grey pistol in it reached down. I saw the boy's face jerk, once, and go slack.

I turned round. I couldn't help it.

He was hanging there. That's what I remember. Everything in him had gone soft at once, arms and shoulders and neck, his head tipped back, his legs swinging out over the drop, and his hands had not let go. They stayed on the gallery's edge, flat and grey and caught, as if they belonged to someone else who was still awake.

His chest didn't move.

I counted. It is a Survey habit, and I'd have done it anyway.

At forty, Pell-15 had a hand on my arm. At ninety, the Pell on the gallery crouched with his ledger on his knee and began, with great interest, to write. Sedge had her palm flat on the glass and was saying his name without any sound. Two hundred. Two hundred and forty. I had stopped breathing myself.

At two hundred and sixty, a hand's width from Sedge's shoulder, Bram's face began to change. The cheekbones lifted, slowly, like something rising under a sheet. The skin greyed and went mottled, green over grey, the colour of the moss on the trunks below. The jaw narrowed. The chin, the square stubborn chin he'd come out of the orchards with, drew in to a point, the way wax goes when you hold it near a flame, until it was nobody's chin I had ever seen.

Then his chest heaved.

He hauled in air like a man come up from the bottom of a pond, and his head came up, and he looked straight through the glass at the room with a stranger's face and his own expression on it.

The Pell on the gallery said something to him. He said something back. I couldn't hear either through the glass, but I saw the Pell stop writing, and look at him, and not write it down.

Then the boy came up over the rail in one movement, the way Sedge goes into a tree house, and something small and brown came out of his shirt like a cork, and they were both gone round the curve of the glass.

◆ ◆ ◆

First, below us, a long way down in the white, a sound like a great sheet being pulled off a bed: a huge soft tearing that went away and away through the forest from tree to tree. Pell-17 went to the glass and looked down and said, "Oh dear."

Then the cloud began to give up grazers: one, a dozen, more than I could count, rising round the tower like bubbles in a pan with the white coming off their backs in threads, piping soft as owls, the babies blundering up beside their mothers. The glass room was suddenly a lantern in a flock. And even through the glass, even over the cedar, I could smell it: warm, yeasty, ridiculous. The whole top of the High Gardens smelled of a bakehouse at dawn.

Then something came up very close past the window, enormous and slow, with a man sitting on its back.

He had his ledger in both hands. His pin said 26. His mouth was open. He went up past the gold glass with an expression of profound administrative concern, and out of sight above us, and there was a pause in which all three Pells in the room and Sedge and I looked at the ceiling.

Then the Pell came down on the glass roof from about the height of a hay cart, and lay there, flat, directly above the dinner table, looking down at us through the glass with his nose pressed sideways.

After a moment he sat up, opened his ledger, and began to write.

"That," said Pell-15, in a voice of real outrage, "is Twenty-six."

He and Pell-17 went for the inner door and the stair to the terrace at a run. Pell-22 turned his face up to the roof, to see what his colleague was writing about him.

He had turned his face up for slightly under a second. That's plenty.

There was a Survey cook on an ice world in my fourth season who had once been something else, and she had taught me how to put a large man down without hurting either of us much: his weight, his own knees, the floor. Pell-22 went down on the dinner-room carpet very neatly, which I think he would have appreciated, and his compliance wand came away in my hand.

I turned it, put the brass cap against the dark plate by the gallery door, and pressed the stud.

There was a crack and a smell of hot metal, and the hum under the plate stopped, and the door, as the fire code requires of every door on every world, fell open.

The cold came in like a slap, and the smell of bread with it, and the sound of the flock: hundreds of them, piping, all round us in the stars.

I looked over my shoulder at Sedge, on the floor by the glass, and said the only word that seemed to cover it.

"Livestock."

She was past me and out on the gallery before I'd finished turning round.

◆ ◆ ◆

He was there by the rail. He'd come back round the curve with the otter on his shoulder, and he was crouched on the walkway steaming in the cold like a horse after a race, and the face turned up to us in the gold light out of the glass room was the face I'd watched arrive. Long, mottled, narrow-chinned. Silver at the back of the eyes.

Sedge had stopped dead.

I looked at him once, all of him, the way I look at a drawer that doesn't match its label, and found I couldn't do it. Not the looking. The not-knowing. It was him. I'd watched it be him.

"Later," he said.

It wasn't his voice. It was low and rough and had a hiss at the edges, like wind going through something with slits in it.

"Two each," it said. "The bread ones. Hold the strong ones."

I looked at the flock. I looked at the rail, and at the white sea of cloud under it, very flat and very far down, with a forest inside it I had never seen.

"I am not—" I said.

"Two each."

One of the grazers came down to the rail, and I knew it from Bram's telling at Highwater: a nick out of the edge of the left wing. It had a baby tucked under its side. Behind it came others, high and heavy and smelling of home, their floats tight as drums.

Hask has an item for this. Number two thousand and six. It protects the bearer from livestock falling on her. It is entirely silent on the subject of the bearer falling with it.

I took a breath. I set my hands in the loose warm skin at the base of two grazers' necks and got a proper grip, the way you'd hold a form in a high wind that you have no intention of letting go of.

And I stepped off the tower.

◆ ◆ ◆

There's a moment before the float takes your weight where you are simply a woman falling off a building. It's long enough to have one clear thought. Mine was that I had left the pudding.

Then the grip took hold, and the two grazers sagged, and piped, and held.

We went down. Not falling: sinking, swinging a little, the grazers' bellies warm against my forearms and the bread smell coming off them in waves. Above me Sedge came off the gallery with a yell like a gull, two grazers in her fists and her hair straight up, and then the boy behind her, quieter, the otter down his collar.

And above them, at the very top of the tower, on a terrace in a fall of gold light from an open door, a man in a long coat stood at the rail and watched us go.

He didn't shout. He didn't call his Associates. He stood with his square hands on the rail and his face in shadow, and his coat was gold again, as gold as I had seen it; I don't know what he'd been watching to make it so. As I watched, it went slowly from gold to the silver-white of the cloud, as if it were trying to come with us.

He did not raise his hand.

Then the white closed over us.

◆ ◆ ◆

The cloud from inside, at night, is like falling through wet wool. I couldn't see the grazers I was holding. A waterfall came and went somewhere close, cold mist went up my sleeves, and the flock's piping came from everywhere at once.

"Left!" Sedge shouted, from somewhere. "Bram, it's left—"

"It's right." The rough strange voice, close below. "Sound lies up here. Smell."

I breathed in. Wet stone. Moss. And under it, very faint, something that should not have been on a mountain: the green, new, unlikely smell of a leaf growing out of a crack between two carved stones.

We came out of the bottom of the cloud like coming out of a sack, and the scree was right there, and the moss, and Hask, his top stone tipped all the way back, watching the sky.

My feet touched. My knees went.

Somewhere above us in the white, a dart pistol went tik. Tik.

Hask came down over the three of us like a roof falling in slow, stone by stone, with a sound like a great door closing, and it was dark under him and smelled of lichen and old rain. Something bounced off his top stone. Then something else. Then nothing.

"I have checked," said Hask, above our heads, "and only one of you is my bearer." A grinding pause. "I have decided not to mind."

◆ ◆ ◆

There was a pool below the lower waterfall, black and round among ferns. We found it in the grey before dawn, the flock settling round us on the slope and the babies folding into a heap like rolls cooling on a tray. The towers' lights went out above the cloud one by one.

I watched him find his face in the water. I watched Sedge ask him who he was, and him not know.

When someone can't answer a simple question about themselves, you don't fill the silence for them. You make sure they have something to write on. I knelt in the ferns in front of him and held out the pad of blank paper forms, the ones that don't need charging, and a pencil, and said nothing at all.

He wrote. It took him a long time. The new fingers caught on the pencil and peeled off and caught again. When he gave it back, the list went all the way down and up the side.

Bram. And under it, whose. Among them, Odile's.

I read it once, all the way down and up the side, and didn't let my face do anything. Then I folded it in half, and in half again, corner to corner, like a sheet off the line, and put it in the inside pocket of my coat, over my heart, beside a girl's yes and a rich man's signature.

"Filed," I said.

Afterwards, when the grazer had burped in his face and he'd laughed his own laugh and Sedge had found him by it, I took my coat off and put it round him, pockets and all, because he was shaking, and because it was the safest place I had.

Sedge sat down on a wet rock, put her chin in her hands, and stared at the cloud where the tower was.

"We never got the pudding," she said.

She said it with such raw grief that Hask turned his entire stack to look at her, and Bram laughed so hard he had to put his head between his knees, and I found that I was laughing too, helplessly, in the cold, with no coat on.

◆ ◆ ◆

"Examiner," said Hask, when the sun was up. "I must report a bud."

It was at the joint between his seventh and eighth stones, among the four leaves: a small hard green knot on a stalk, closed tight, like a fist round something it hadn't decided to show anyone.

"When?"

"I believe at about the time I was a roof." A pause. "Over the right person, for once. Over three, in fact. Only one of whom is enumerated." The top stone turned to me. "I do not know what it will open into. I have never had anything open."

"Will you declare it?"

"Not yet," said Hask. "I find I want to see it first."

Halloran found me on the relay at the bottom of the scree, where the cloud stopped and the world came back. Bacon frying. Tock.

"Pastern. Varro-Tandem's board filed a complaint against you at four this morning. Assault on an Associate, damage to a door, theft of a compliance wand, and," the sound of paper, "removal of materials by livestock." Tock. "They're very upset about the livestock."

"Yes, sir."

"Their own executive filed something else at four fifteen. His record of an undertaking, signed, witnessed by an Examiner. To fund your library. In place of what he calls the draw." A long frying pause. "I've been in this chair thirty-one years, and I've never seen a company file against itself before breakfast."

"He keeps his word, sir. They don't."

"Well, the Chancery's read both, and your seven ponds, and your fifty-nine-P." Tock. "A ruling under an open case can propose a schema change. You've been waving that at me since the orchards. The Chancery doesn't like it, but if it's going to be heard, they want it heard where the Discrepancy is. Not in my office. Where it happened, with the people it happened to watching."

I looked up at the towers coming out gold above the cloud, and past them, over the ridge, to where the Preserve lay, and in it, though I couldn't see it from there, a dome with no stars in it.

"The hearing's called, Pastern. Firstfield. Under the dome. Day twenty-five." Tock. "Two days. Bring your evidence."

Behind me, my evidence was sitting on a rock by a black pool in my coat, with a stranger's face and an otter asleep in his shirt, eating chewed seed and laughing at something Sedge had said, in a voice that was not his, with a laugh that was.

"Yes, sir," I said. "I'm bringing all of it."
