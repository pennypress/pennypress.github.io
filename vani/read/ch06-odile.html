What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 7 of 24

Chapter Six: Biological Status: None

← Chapter Five: Keep MovingContentsChapter Seven: The Hive Remembers →

UNDER. Dead rock is quiet. A living cave drips, ticks, breathes and talks to anyone with a hand on it; a dead one only waits. If the cave stops talking to you, stop talking to it, and leave by the way you came. [pencilled in the margin, in a large untidy hand:] And if it hums, say thank you.

— Field Primer of the Survey, fourth edition, the chapter headed "Under"; a crew copy, Vessany lanes

The Chief Examiner called at dawn, which is how I knew he hadn't been to bed.

I was sitting on the boy's stone by the pond with the flask between my knees and the seven tomato plants at my feet when the slate gave the three flat notes of a relay call from Margin Station, nine hours of hop away. It sounded like somebody frying bacon in a tin bath.

"Pastern."

"Chief Examiner."

There was a pause, and in it, very clearly, I heard a small dry tock: a sunflower-seed shell landing in the tin on Halloran's desk.

"You opened a Discrepancy," he said, "on a boy."

"On a record."

"On a boy who, as of last night, the Register has down as Retired." Tock. "Which means there's no discrepancy, Pastern. The Upper House says he's finished. The Register says he's finished. Retired is a status, not a whereabouts. Close it."

The grazer that had marked me came down the bank while he was talking and wiped its face along my jaw again, ear to chin, as if it suspected I'd been washing. Hay, wet wool, honey gone to vinegar, and under it, faint, the loaf.

"Sir," I said. "The record says Retired. And then there's no next line."

"There often isn't."

"There always is. Retired to a pension. Retired to the stud book. Retired, decommissioned, rendered. The Register is a filing system; it has never in its life left a thing without a drawer. This one hasn't got one." "And I have his living trace on a sky-grazer twenty-two metres up, confirmed by assay, entered under seal, eleven hours before somebody upstairs signed him off. Either he was retired while he was still floating about the Spill on the back of a goat, or the Upper House filed a form about a boy it didn't have. One of those is a Forgery, sir. Possibly both."

Tock.

"You've got a goat," Halloran said at last, "that tastes of a dead boy."

"Sky-grazer. And he isn't dead. He planted tomatoes."

That pause was longer.

"Ten days," said Halloran. "From now. And no budget, Pastern. None. Don't send me receipts. I don't want to see a sandwich. If you can't put it on a form in ten days, you close it yourself and you sign it yourself, and it stays on your record."

"Yes, sir."

"And Pastern." Tock. "The company's filed. Varro-Tandem. Some sort of recovery claim on the remains. They'll have someone on you by lunch." He spat another shell. "Be civil."

"I'm always civil."

"I've read your file," said the Chief Examiner, and the call closed on a crackle like a log falling in a grate.

Ten days and no budget. In eleven years Halloran had never once said well done, and he had never once given anyone ten days.

"Hask," I said. "We've been given a great deal of trust and no money at all."

"I have never had money," Hask said, from the shade of the fur-tree, where he had spent the night with a leaf in his joint and a crack in his third stone. "I have found trust heavier."

◆ ◆ ◆

The company came at noon, by air, in grey.

The skiff was the colour of a filing cabinet and nearly as quiet, and it set down beyond the farm cut without disturbing a single seed-head. A man got out and walked up the valley towards me with the step of a person who has never in his life arrived anywhere early or late.

He was tall, and thin, and very neat. Company-grey suit, still without a crease after half a kilometre of Spill, which ought not to be possible. Silver hair combed flat to his skull, though the face under it couldn't have been much past forty. He carried a slim ledger case and a stylus, and when the grass leaned at him he looked down at it and wrote something. He smelled of cedar and of the warm breath of a copier that has been running all morning.

"Examiner Pastern," he said. A pleasant baritone, the kind they use on the hop to tell you the oxygen masks are perfectly routine. "Associate Pell. Varro-Tandem Bioreference. Recovery." He inclined his head to Hask. "Proxy. I'm told the Orrun prefer not to be touched, so I shan't offer."

Hask regarded him from his full height. "You are well informed."

"I am documented," said Associate Pell, and smiled.

On his lapel there was a small enamel pin, white on grey, with a numeral on it. 7.

"Seven?" I said.

Something went across his pleasant face and was filed away. "Associate Pell," he said again, a little more distinctly. "There are, I'm afraid, a number of us. The licence calls each of us the Associate. The pin is a convenience for accounting." He opened his ledger case on his forearm. "Examiner, I'll be brief; I know your budget is nil. My company holds a claim for recovery of materials in respect of the specimen designated Reference Twelve, status Retired. Retired specimens revert to the licensee. I'm here to recover them."

"Them?"

"The materials. I wouldn't dream of asking you to alter evidence, Examiner. But I notice that you've entered the boy's traces. You haven't entered the boy." He looked up, mild as milk. "He isn't evidence. He's materials. And materials, wherever found, are ours."

It was exactly the hole I'd have gone through myself.

"You can't recover what you can't find," I said.

"No," Pell agreed. "But I can document where I've looked. Our survey drones picked up a heavy, low-flying shape at dusk on the night of the ninth, leaving this valley with the flock. North, over the ridge. I imagine you saw it too." He had read my notes, of course; filing is publishing, if you know where to stand. "I've staked a route. I'll be ahead of you, Examiner. Nothing personal."

"Everything documented," I said.

His face did the thing again. "That's mine," he said.

"I read it on your skiff."

He wrote that down. Then he closed the ledger case with a precise click, bowed to Hask, and went back down the valley. At the edge of the trees he took a slim grey peg from his case, pushed it into the earth with his heel, and walked on.

"Hask," I said. "Did he just mark his trail for us?"

"For the company," said Hask. A long grinding pause. "Following it is not," he added, with satisfaction, "enumerated."

The skiff driver came at four with the last hamper I could not afford. I asked for rope. She looked at me for a long time, then fetched forty metres of good brown line, coiled like a sleeping snake, and dropped it at my feet.

"Rope's yours," she said. "Bill's under it."

◆ ◆ ◆

We went north the next morning in a cold wind, up the stream, past the ravine, and onto the ridge.

The pegs were every hundred metres, exactly: slim grey stakes with orange caps, each stamped VT and a number, running up the slope in a line so straight it looked ruled on. There's a particular pleasure, for a woman with no budget, in being guided across a wilderness by her opponent's surveying.

The fur-trees grew shorter and shaggier as we climbed, and then gave up and became a tufted scrub that rattled in the wind like a crowd shaking dice. The air tasted thinner and colder, resinous, like a pencil being sharpened.

Two-thirds of the way to the top, the pegs swung east in a long careful curve, though the slope ahead was easy, and I stopped.

The grass was pointing.

Not at me; not since a grazer wiped its face on my jaw. A patch fifty paces up the slope was leaning, every blade, inward at a tangle of fern and broken stone, like iron filings round a magnet. And in the shadow under the fern, swinging very slowly from side to side, was a small blue light.

Pell's drone, I thought, had seen the heat from the air and drawn a polite curve round it. Pell had not been wearing goat.

The wind came down the ridge at me, and I smelled it: a butcher's drain in high summer, with a new note now of something scalded.

"Examiner," said Hask, very low. "I am obliged to mention that I have already measured that one."

"I know."

"I may act at once."

"Don't," I said. "It doesn't know we're here."

It didn't. I walked up that slope in plain sight, the grass round my boots standing straight up as if I were weather, and the blue bulb swung across me, and paused, and swung on. It was waiting for something that would make the grass point. I passed it at twenty paces. I could hear it breathing, a dry tic... tic... of plates. My own heart was going so hard I thought it must show on the grass from a kilometre off.

The grass didn't care. To the Spill, I was flock.

Hask came after me in a long slow detour, and when he was well past he said, "Item three thousand three hundred and one does not cover passing it."

"No."

"I find I should like it to."

The ridge top was all wind and a view that knocked the breath out of me: valley after valley to the north, folded like a blanket kicked off a bed, green going blue going violet, a river laid along one like a dropped silver chain. On the far side the slope went down in deep moss, and below the crest the moss held a long dent.

It was the length of a boy lying down. Grazer down was caught in it, a whole drift, and when I put my face close the moss still smelled, four days on, faintly and unmistakably, of bread.

I wrote: Landing site. I wrote: Two grazers, or one big one. Then, because I could, I wrote: He flew.

Twenty paces further up there was a ring of fern, and inside the ring of fern, the moss simply stopped.

It was a hole, round and black and wider than a kitchen, with roots hanging into it like unravelled rope, and out of it, in long slow breaths, came cold. It smelled of a cellar: stone and wet and mushroom, and something sweet underneath, like apples bruised in the bottom of a sack.

Pell's pegs ran straight to it and stopped, and at the end of the line, on a folding stool beside a lamp and a coil of orange company line, sat Associate Pell, writing.

"Examiner," he said, without looking up. "You came through the warm patch. How very brave. I've entered it." He turned his ledger case round so I could see the Register entry glowing on it.

CLAIM UC-114 (Ridge Undercroft, north face). Workings: suspended. Wall consolidant: BINDER line B-4, self-setting, sugar-fed; left in place. Biological status: none (subsurface; inert). Renewal: automatic.

"A mining claim," said Pell pleasantly. "Unworked for sixty years, but very much on the books. Inert. Which means whatever I find down there is nobody's business but mine. I can net it. I can blast for it." He capped his stylus. "There's nothing down there to object."

The hole breathed out at us, slow and cold and sweet.

◆ ◆ ◆

Hask couldn't come.

We had the argument at the lip of the sinkhole in the twilight, while Pell pretended to write and listened with every inch of his neat grey suit.

"Old workings," I said. "Undercut rock, sixty years untended, and you weigh a third of a tonne."

"Three hundred and forty kilograms," said Hask, "less the leaf."

"I've been underground with the Survey. What brings a ceiling down is weight on a hollowed floor. I'm not having you go through it."

"Item one thousand one hundred and seventy," said Hask. "Entombment, partial or entire." He brought it out slowly, as if it hurt. "It is enumerated, Examiner. I cannot shield you from it from up here."

"Then shield me from up here as best you can, and I'll try very hard not to be entombed."

"It is not enumerated," he said at last, "that I should be left behind."

"No," I said. "I'm sorry, Hask."

"I shall walk above you," he said. "Stone hears stone. If the hill moves, I shall know where." And then, after a pause so long the first stars came out over the ridge: "Take the rope."

I took the rope.

We went down at first light, Pell and I, a little apart. He had his company line, his lamp, a long grey case on a strap that held a net-launcher, and on his belt, discreetly, a short black rod with a brass cap, which the catalogues call a compliance wand and everyone else calls a stun baton. I had forty metres of brown rope over my shoulder, my coat, my slate, my flask, and a pad of blank paper forms, because Fallow taught me that paper doesn't need charging.

The roots were wet and stringy in my fists, and the cold climbed my legs like water filling a bath. The circle of sky shrank to a plate, then a coin. Pell's lamp made a hard white tunnel through the dark and showed me nothing but the next root.

Then my boots found grit, and I let go, and took one step off the bottom of the roots, and the cave lit up.

◆ ◆ ◆

It did not light for Pell's lamp. I want to be exact about that. His lamp swept the walls and showed wet black rock and pale shapes on it, dull as dried mushrooms in a jar. But at my shoulder, a shelf of those shapes began to glow. Blue-green, soft as the last of a summer evening, and the glow ran away from me up the wall, shelf after shelf, until it was over my head and then gone round the curve of the roof. I took a step and it followed me, lighting ahead and fading behind. I stood still and it lowered itself, slowly, the way you turn down a lamp for somebody sleeping.

It liked warmth. It liked me. It didn't care for a white beam in the least.

"Fascinating," said Pell, and wrote, and turned his lamp off.

It was a room the size of a cathedral, except that cathedrals are made to look large and this was simply large, the way weather is. The walls went up and leaned in and met far overhead in ribbed arches, and the arches were fungus: great pleated shelves, each pleat brighter at the edge than the middle, like the inside of a shell held to a lamp. Through the rock ran thousands of pale threads, branching and rejoining, and down from the roof hung roots, the roots of every living thing on the hill above us, wound about with those threads, so that I was standing under the whole of the Spill and looking up at its underside.

Drips fell everywhere, and every one rang, tink, tonk, never the same twice, and under it all ran a long roar like a crowd in another street. Water. A great deal of it.

The air tasted of stone, of wet keys and clean cold, and when I drank from a pool it hurt my teeth to the roots. I put my bare palm flat on a furred patch of wall, purely for the record.

It was faintly warm, soft as the inside of a mitten, and something came up through it into my wrist and settled in my back teeth: a hum, very low, steady and slow, like a mill wheel felt through the floor of the mill.

"Biological status," I said, "none."

"Inert," Pell agreed placidly, beside me in the blue dark, and wrote it down.

◆ ◆ ◆

We walked for most of a day. Pell followed a map from the claim file, drawn in a fine dead hand; I followed the glow, which went more or less where his map did; and we bickered the whole way at a low pleasant volume, like two clerks sharing a desk.

"Sixty-three exhibits under one number," he said, stepping over a rail rusted to lace. "I've filed a note that it's improper."

"Which Pell's note? Yours?"

"The Associate's note," he said, with frost on it.

He saw things; I'll give him that. He wrote down the number of every rod and every plate bolted to the wall, furred over like coat buttons. He did not see that one of them, at shoulder height, had been rubbed clean. A sleeve's width of the fur wiped away, and the iron plate under it bare and dark, and on it, scratched with a nail by someone long ago:

B4 HOLDS. KEEP MOVING.

The fur was creeping back only at the edge of the wipe: a few days' growth. Someone shorter than me had stood here and wondered what it meant.

I said nothing to Pell. He was writing down the number of the plate.

We came to the skylight in the afternoon, if there were afternoons down there.

A column of real light stood in a cave like a white tree, coming down a shaft where something big had torn its roots out of the hill. It landed on a mound of leaf-litter, and the mound was green, ferns and moss and tiny pale things straining upward. I put my hand in the light. It was warm as a windowsill, and I hadn't known until then how cold I was.

Up the shaft, a long way off, a bird I didn't know said something in four notes, and stopped, and said it again.

I nearly walked past them, the way I nearly had at the pond. They did nothing.

On the top of the mound, in a little row, there were five dimples in the soil, where a finger had gone in first knuckle deep and no further. Four of them had cracked open, and out of each crack there came a tiny bent stem, folded over like the handle of a walking stick, with its two seed leaves still caught in the husk. The stems were red. A dull, dark, earthy red, the colour of a rained-on brick.

In the middle of the row, one dimple had been dug up and roughly patted shut again, and beside it in the soft earth was a print I didn't know: small, webbed, with a ragged scuff of toes behind it, as if something had walked through on more feet than it strictly needed.

"Weeds," said Pell, behind me, looking at the stems.

"Tomatoes."

"Is that significant?"

"They don't move," I said.

He wrote tomatoes down, with a small doubtful frown, as if he suspected me of a joke at the company's expense. I took out my slate and added them to the open case: Five plants, baseline, red-stemmed, planted by the subject; one disturbed and replanted. Then I sat on my heels in the warm light with the bird singing its four notes, and for a moment I didn't want to be an Examiner at all. I wanted to see his face when he knew they'd come up.

◆ ◆ ◆

The quiet started an hour past the skylight.

By then I was trailing my palm along the furred walls for the hum, and then the wall was cold, slick as the outside of a jug of well water. No hum. When I stopped, the glow didn't come.

The drips didn't ring here. They knocked, flat, like knuckles on a table, and the air smelled of nothing at all: a closed-room nothing, like the bladder of the fallen grazer below 40-K. Something had stopped here.

Pell turned his lamp back on. The arches overhead were dead, grey at the edges like burnt paper, and over everything hung something else: grey, smooth, rubbery, like the skin on boiled milk if boiled milk were the size of a barn, lying over the pale threads the way wax lies over a jar.

Only it was coming off.

All along the gallery the grey was peeling, folds of it hanging slack like wallpaper in a house nobody has heated for a winter, and under the folds the threads showed pale again. I put my palm to a bare patch and it was cold, and then, very faintly, it wasn't. A hum, thin as a single violin string in another room.

"B-4," said Pell, reading his claim file. "Wall consolidant. Self-setting." He sounded, for the first time, slightly uncertain. "It's meant to be holding the roof."

"It isn't holding anything. It's starving."

"How on earth would you know that?"

I didn't, then. I knew it twenty minutes later.

The gallery opened into a field of rubble, fresh, the dust still soft and pale and unmarked by drips. A whole cave had come down here days ago, not years. Pell's lamp swept over it and the dust rose in the beam like flour.

At the near edge of the fall, a grey lump stood on the floor, hard as a step, and in the top of it was a hole.

I knelt by it. The hole was the exact shape of a heel. Not a boot heel: a bare one, small, and every ridge of a fine broad tread was printed into the grey, sharp as a seal in wax. I had seen that tread in the mud of a stream a day's walk south, coming out of a run of bloody bare prints and going on as if the boy had grown boots.

I put two fingers into it. Cold, smooth, perfect. Something had grabbed his heel here and set, and the heel had come out like a cork from a bottle and left this behind like a calling card.

"Footprint," said Pell, reading over my shoulder. "Of what?"

"Of materials," I said, and he had the grace to say nothing.

The other thing was in the far wall.

Above the rubble, where a slice of the old cave wall still stood, a thin trickle of water was weeping out of a crack at about the height of my head and running down a groove worn smooth in the rock. The air over it was sweet. Not the bruised-apple sweet of the glow. Sticky sweet: fruit and tree sap and honey left in the sun. I put my finger in it and tasted it, which you should never do in a cave, and which I'll do again.

Sugar.

Low down, where the groove met the edge of the fall, someone had built a wall across it. Flat stones from the old roof falls, laid slantwise, the way you'd lay a sod to turn rain off a garden bed, and the gaps between them packed solid with grey binder gone as hard as mortar. A dam, the length of my arm, smooth and grey and neat. The sweet water hit it and ran politely sideways, over the lip of the groove, and away down a dark crack in the rock, glinting, towards the sound of the river.

In the top of the dam there were two more holes. A left heel and a right heel, side by side, ridged and perfect.

And all over the grey round them, low down, there were small sharp bite marks, dozens of them, and strips of grey lay in the rubble below, chewed and spat out, as if something with small teeth had been eating the stuff and thinking very poorly of it.

I sat down on a rock with my palm on the wall, where a pale thread was showing through the peeling grey, and let the logic come.

The grey fed on sugar; the claim said so. The sugar came down from the Spill's roots on the hill above, and it should have gone out along all those threads, and instead it had all been running into one mouth. Then a boy who knew how to turn rain off a garden bed had laid a wall of stones across the groove, and when the water kept shifting them he had stood on them, still, and let the grey do the one thing it was made for. It set round anything that stayed still. It set round his stones, and his feet, and sealed itself off from its own supper.

He had stood in it long enough for it to set. I thought of what it would have done to the bleeding pink feet from the stream, and had to look at the bite marks instead. Not alone, then. Something with small teeth, on his side.

After that the sugar went down the crack to the river, and past a hundred caves, and the threads were drinking again; and the grey, starved, was letting go of the roof it had been holding up.

Under my palm the hum came up into my teeth, a little stronger than before. I dug my thumbnail into the bare pale fur at the edge of the grey to take a sample. It came away cold and faintly greasy and packed itself up under my nail, and the wall glowed round my hand, briefly, like a cat leaning into a stroke.

"He fixed it," I said.

"Who did?"

"The boy. Your materials." I held up my thumb to the lamp. "Biological status: none. The whole mountain's been talking under your feet since we came in. He found the one thing killing it and starved it with a wall of stones and his own two feet. That's a living network, Associate, and somebody put it in the wrong drawer sixty years ago and let it renew itself every year since."

Pell looked at the dam and the heel marks for a long time. I watched him want to write something down and not know what.

Then the rock under his feet went quiet.

◆ ◆ ◆

The hum in my teeth stopped. Not faded: stopped, as if a hand had been laid flat on a string. And in the silence, from somewhere under the floor, a tick. A dry small tick, like a stove cooling. Then another.

The drips round Pell's feet were knocking flat on dead stone. There wasn't a glimmer on the walls on his side, not a thread showing. The grey hung over him in slack folds, letting go.

On my side, a pace behind me, a shelf of gills glowed faintly blue.

"Pell," I said. "Step towards me."

He looked up from his ledger case. "I beg your pardon?"

"Now. The rock's dead under you."

I saw the tiny pleasant pause in which an Associate decides whether an instruction from an Examiner constitutes a recordable event. He opened his mouth. I believe he was going to object.

The floor went.

It tipped, the whole slab under him, with a crack like a branch, and slid. Pell went with it, lamp swinging, and I was off my rock and across the gap before I'd decided anything, the way I went for the guard at the tank-room door, and I got a fistful of grey company suit at the collar and threw my weight backwards.

It was not enough. It was a good deal better than nothing.

The slab went out from under both of us and we fell together, a long sliding drop down rough stone in a roar of grit and dust, his lamp spinning off and going out with a small final pop, and then we hit bottom, hard, and the dark came down on top of us like a blanket.

◆ ◆ ◆

For a while there was only rock settling over our heads, a long grinding complaint, and pebbles bouncing down the walls. Then there was only the river.

It was very loud. It was below me.

I lay on cold wet stone and took stock. Knees scraped. Left elbow unhappy. Ribs sore but present. The flask, still dented in four places and no more. The rope, still over my shoulder, bless the driver and her bill. I couldn't see my hand in front of my face. Black like the inside of a closed fist.

"Examiner." Pell's voice. Calm, pleasant, and some way off. Further than it should have been. "Are you injured?"

"Bruised. You?"

"I've lost my lamp, and the stylus, which is company property, and I seem to be sitting on the edge of something with water under it."

I felt round me on hands and knees. A narrow shelf of stone; a wall on my left, slick and cold; another on my right, close enough to touch with my boot. A slot, a long narrow cleft in the mountain, and we were at the bottom of it. I crawled towards his voice.

The floor stopped.

It ended in a sharp edge, and below it came cold air, and spray, and the roar. I dropped a pebble. It took a long time to hit water.

"Pell," I said. "Keep talking."

"About what?"

"Anything. Your grievances."

He began, in a perfectly level voice, to list them. They were mostly against Pell-4. It gave me time to work it out with my hands. I was on a shelf at one end of the slot; he was on a shelf at the other. Between us the floor had split into a black crack too wide to jump even in daylight, and far down it the river was going through the mountain like a train.

I reached up. The walls rose further than I could reach, a metre apart on my side, so I could press a palm on each. On his side, by the sound of him, they flared wider.

"Associate," I said. "Can you reach both walls?"

A pause, and the faint scuff of someone neat trying to touch two things at once. "I can reach one wall," said Pell. "The other appears to be a theory."

"Then we have a problem."

"I'd noticed." I heard a case unclip. "I have a proposal, Examiner. My net-launcher fires a weighted line. I believe I can put a line across this crack, and we could rig a crossing, and then—"

"And then?"

"And then I would, of course, have to log what the net had recovered." A small embarrassed cough in the dark. "It's not my decision. The launcher records it. It's company equipment. Anything it brings in is entered as recovered materials."

I sat on my shelf in the dark, knees bleeding through my trousers, and laughed until my ribs hurt.

"You want me to be salvage."

"It would be purely a matter of record," said Pell, wounded. "Nothing personal."

"Everything documented."

"That's mine," he said, with real feeling, and then, after a long pause in which the river went on, in a different voice, much less pleasant and much more honest: "Examiner. I don't know how to get out of here."

"No," I said. "I do."

And then I looked down at my hands, because something had changed, and I couldn't at first tell what.

They were glowing.

Not enough to light the slot. But under each nail there was a faint blue-green crescent, soft as the gills on the cathedral wall, and the thumb I'd scraped the sample with was brightest of all. Ten small moons in the dark. I moved them in front of my face, and they moved, and I could see them and nothing else in the world.

Something had come in under my nails while I had my palms on the wall, and liked it there, and stayed.

"Examiner?" said Pell. "You've gone quiet."

I took my slate out of my coat. The case had cracked in the fall; the light behind the screen was dead, and the screen was black. But a slate's screen doesn't need its own light, any more than a page does. It only needs some.

I spread my fingers over it like a woman warming her hands at a stove, and in the blue-green glow of my own fingernails the letters came up out of the dark, one line at a time.

CLAIM UC-114. Biological status: none (subsurface; inert).

I could read it as clear as a ledger on a sunny desk.

"What," said Pell, from across the dark, in a voice I would remember with pleasure for the rest of my life, "is that?"

"Evidence," I said.

I found the line, and touched it, and opened a Discrepancy, there in the bottom of a mountain, with the river under me and my hands lit like a ghost's.

In the matter of CLAIM UC-114, Ridge Undercroft. The Register holds the subsurface inert. The world holds a living network, fungal, carrying sugar and signal over kilometres, joined through root to the Spill above, and repairing itself after damage. The Examiner has felt it, been lit by it, and is presently reading by it. Class: Misfiled. Correction: biological status, LIVING NETWORK. Case open.

For evidence I entered the dam, and the heel casts, and the five red-stemmed seedlings under the skylight, and a sample of the network taken under my own right thumbnail, still glowing. Then, after a moment, I entered myself, as the witness who could prove the glow was alive: one Examiner, in situ.

The Register thought about it for no time at all.

CLAIM UC-114. RECLASSIFIED: LIVING NETWORK (subsurface). Workings may not resume. Blasting, netting and removal prohibited without survey. Renewal: SUSPENDED.

There was a short silence on the far side of the crack. Then, very faintly, I heard the soft chime of a ledger case receiving an update.

"Examiner," said Pell, at last. "That's very irritating."

"I know."

"I'll have to file something."

"I'm counting on it." I put the slate away. My nails glowed in the dark like ten small coals. "Now I'm going to climb out, Associate. And then I'm going to get you out. In that order. Don't move."

"I wasn't going to," said Pell. "I've filed a note against moving."

My first season with the Survey, I was twenty-one, on a limestone world where the rain ate the ground like sugar. One night the cook tent went into a sinkhole with the cook's boy inside it. The only person small enough to go down after him was the rope boss, who was sixty and had lost two fingers to a winch, and the only person small enough to go down after her was me.

Her name was Rook. She smelled of tobacco and lanolin. The hole was a slot like a letter box, black and dripping. When the boy had been hauled out, swearing, the rope was too frayed to take the two of us, and she looked at me in the light of her headlamp and said, "Right. We'll walk."

"Walk where?"

"Up." She put her back flat against one wall of the slot and her boots against the other, and hung there, sitting on nothing, comfortable as a cat on a chair. "Never climb with your hands, Pastern. Your legs are twice your arms. Hands are for keeping your balance and pointing at things. Push, don't pull. Back on one wall, feet on the other. Then walk up the inside of the world."

She went up a hand's breadth at a time, talking the whole way about a man she'd nearly married. I went up after her, shaking. Halfway up I looked down, and she said, without looking, "Down's not a direction, Pastern. It's a mood," and I laughed so hard I nearly fell, and didn't, and at the top she gave me a sip of something from a tin cup that took the skin off my throat.

I put my back against the near wall of the slot. I put my boots flat on the far one.

I pushed, and my weight came off the shelf, and I hung between the walls, sitting on nothing, over a river I couldn't see, my glowing hands flat on the rock beside my hips.

Push, don't pull.

Back up a hand's breadth. Boot. Boot. My thighs burned after twenty; after forty they were on fire. Where the walls were furred, the slot glowed a little round my fingers and showed me the next half-metre of black shining rock, and went dark again. I climbed up the inside of the mountain in a little travelling pool of my own light.

Below me, Pell had stopped listing grievances. I could hear him breathing.

"Examiner," he said, once. "How far?"

"Down's not a direction," I said, through my teeth. "It's a mood."

"I'll write that down," said Pell, faintly.

At the top there was a lip, and rubble, and air that moved, and far off the faint blue-green of a living wall. I got an elbow over, then a knee, and rolled onto broken stone and lay with my legs shaking like a new foal's and my ten fingernails glowing at the roof.

Then I got up, because there was a man in a hole.

I worked along the top of the slot in the dark until Pell's voice came up from directly below me. On the rim there, half buried in the rubble, was an iron rod driven into the rock: one of the old consolidation rods, with its bolt-plate furred over like a button. I put my weight on it. It held. B4 holds, I thought, and nearly laughed again.

I tied the driver's rope round it with a hitch Rook would have checked twice and passed, and paid it out until I felt it go slack on his ledge.

Then I took out my pad of paper forms and a pencil, and knelt on the rubble, and wrote by the light of my own hands.

INSPECTORATE OF DISCREPANCIES. Form 9: Assistance Rendered. One (1) line, forty metres, brown, privately owned, lent to Associate Pell (7) of Varro-Tandem Bioreference at the bottom of a hole in CLAIM UC-114, now LIVING NETWORK. The line is lent and not given. The Associate is to acknowledge receipt below. The Associate is not recovered materials. He is a person who fell in. Signed: O. Pastern, Examiner.

I folded it once, and tied it to the rope with a twist of thread from my cuff, and let it slide down.

After a moment the rope twitched.

"Examiner," came Pell's voice, up the slot, in the dark. "There's a form."

"There is."

"I'm being asked to sign for it."

"You're welcome to object."

A long pause, filled with the river. Then the faint scratch of a spare pencil, because he is the sort of man who would carry one.

"For the record," Pell said, "I'm still going to recover him."

"For the record," I said, "you're going to have to climb out first. Hands for balance. Push with your legs."

And I left him there, with my rope and my form, and walked away towards the light.

The skylight was closer than I'd thought. The living wall led me there, glowing ahead of me, the way you'd show a guest to the door, and I came out into the white column of real light beside the mound with the five red stems in it, blinking like a mole.

The roots of the fallen tree hung down the shaft like a ladder somebody had stopped making halfway. Above them, a disc of evening sky the colour of a bruise.

And in the disc, a shape, leaning over.

"Examiner," said Hask, from the top of the shaft, in the voice of a mountain that has spent a long day trying not to clear its throat. "The hill moved."

"It did."

"I heard it in my third stone. It is the cracked one. It hears very well now." A grinding pause. "I came to where it moved."

I climbed. The roots were a staircase after the slot, but near the top my legs went, and I hung with one arm through a root and couldn't find the last two metres. Then Hask's top stone came down the shaft on its long ledge of others, the notary's ell, like an arm if you were generous about arms, and I put both my hands on it, and he lifted me out of the hill like a tooth.

He set me down in the moss. I lay in it and looked up at him, and at the bruise-coloured sky over him, and at the first star.

Between his seventh stone and his eighth, beside the first pale leaf, there was a second. Smaller. Still folded, like a ticket in a fist. The cold breath of the shaft had been coming up over it all afternoon.

"Hask," I said. "You've got another one."

"I am aware," said Hask. "I shall need to amend the amendment."

I laughed, and held my hands up against the dusk. They were dirty and grazed across every knuckle, and under every nail glowed that soft cold crescent of blue-green, like the hands of somebody who has come back from somewhere they ought not to have.

"Examiner," said Hask, after looking at them for some time. "Your hands are haunted."

"They're evidence."

"Is everything evidence?"

"Today," I said, "yes."

The grey skiff came over the ridge as the stars came out, quiet as a filing cabinet, and set down on the moss without disturbing it, and a man got out.

Tall, thin, very neat. Company-grey suit without a crease. Silver hair combed flat. Cedar and warm toner. On his lapel, a small enamel pin, white on grey.

12.

"Examiner Pastern," he said, in a pleasant baritone. "Associate Pell. Varro-Tandem Bioreference. I'm so sorry to disturb your evening." He inclined his head to Hask. "Proxy. I'm told the Orrun prefer not to be touched, so I shan't offer." "I understand there's been a slight setback in the matter of the recovery. I've been sent as a backup."

There was a sound from the shaft behind me: a scrape of good shoes on root, a grunt, a long, effortful, deeply undignified scramble. Then a hand came over the lip, and an arm, and Associate Pell, number seven, hauled himself out of the hill and onto the moss on his elbows.

He had lost a shoe. His suit was grey now in a quite different sense, dust from collar to cuff, torn down one sleeve, with a faintly glowing blue-green smear on the knee. His silver hair stood up in a crest. He had my rope coiled neatly over one shoulder, because of course he had, and my form folded into his top pocket.

He got to his feet. He saw Pell-12.

They looked at each other across three metres of moss, two identical men in the starlight, one perfect and one filthy, and the temperature on that hillside dropped by about ten degrees.

"Twelve," said Pell-7.

"Seven," said Pell-12. "I'm relieved to see you. HQ was concerned."

"Backup," said Pell-7.

"A precaution. Nothing personal."

"Everything documented," said Pell-7, and his pleasant baritone cracked across the middle like a slab of dead rock. "That's mine. You were issued after me—"

"The licence," said Pell-12 gently, "refers to all of us as the Associate."

"I am filing," said Pell-7, "a grievance."

"I've already filed one," said Pell-12, "against yours."

I sat in the moss with my glowing hands round the flask and did not help. Hask stood beside me with two leaves in his joint, enjoying himself in a way no stone in the Tally has any business doing.

Pell-12 turned to me with the air of a man who has decided to be the more professional copy. "Examiner, as a courtesy," he said, "that the company has a fix on the subject's living trace, as of this afternoon. Forty kilometres north, and moving." He smiled, very pleasantly. "Where the flowers are. I'll be going ahead by air. Seven, I'm afraid, will have to follow on foot. There's only room for one in the skiff."

He walked back to the grey skiff. Pell-7 watched him go with an expression I have only ever seen on Hask when someone sits on him.

The skiff lifted without a sound and went north, a grey speck against the stars. Behind me the hill breathed out its cold sweet cellar breath, and when I laid my palm flat on the moss the hum came up into my teeth, wider than it had been that morning.

I unscrewed the flask. Steam came up, and tar, and raisins.

"Item fourteen," said Hask.

I drank it anyway. Then I took out my slate, and held my hand over the cracked black screen, and by the light of my own fingernails I read, in the dark, on a hillside, with no budget at all, that I had eight days left.
