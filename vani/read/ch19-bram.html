— Placard, passenger cabin, Moriel to Margin Station hop (Inspectorate charter, cabin two)

At nine o'clock in the morning, the ceiling says nothing.

Tilly waits for it anyway. She's on her stool at the bakehouse shelf with the cloth off the crock and the flour scoop in both hands, and she stops with it in the air, looking up at the ribs, the way you wait for a sneeze.

No click. No hiss. No warm dead woman telling the sky what we're doing.

"It's broken," says Tilly.

"It's off."

She thinks about that with her whole face. Then she tips in much too much flour, stirs, and says to the crock, in a perfect audio-guide voice, "Here we see the Baseline Tilly feeding the crock. Note the single stool." She looks at me sideways to see if I'll laugh. I do. The crock gives a fat sour burp of agreement, more, warm, more, that I taste at the back of my tongue like an old friend clearing its throat.

The bakehouse door is propped open with a brick. Mistress Thorne has given up fighting it. The whole lane smells of the morning bake going out sideways instead of up, crust and steam and the scorched-sugar edge of the first loaves, carried off toward Saul's rows by air that came in from the hills. The light through the glass isn't Tuesday light or Thursday light. It's just light. There's a cloud going over that nobody chose.

Yesterday a big grey man in a wet-slate coat stood up in the meeting house and said my name, and two words after it, and my row in the Register stopped saying RETIRED. Last night the far door stood open till the stars came out, and nobody went through it. And nobody shut it.

Tilly smooths the cloth flat with both hands, the way I used to.

"You're going tomorrow," she says, to the cloth.

"Who told you?"

"Nobody. You looked at the door all through breakfast like it owed you money."

That's Jory's. She's been listening to Jory.

"Right," says Tilly, in Ma's voice exactly, and puts both floury hands on my sleeve. "Then you'll want the joke."

"Which joke?"

"Mine. The proper one. You've got to tell it to them out there. The four-eyed ones and the ones in tanks and all of them. And you've got to say it's mine."

"Tilly, they won't know what a tomato is."

"Then you'll have to tell them that," says Tilly, as if I'm slow, "first."

◆ ◆ ◆

Ma's at the hives.

The clover strip behind Saul's east rows is loud this morning. The wind's brought in smells the bees have never met, and every hive mouth has a crowd of guards on it, fanning and arguing. I can hear them from the end of the lane through my soles, a hum under the hum, like a kettle just before.

She doesn't turn round. She's got a frame up out of the third hive, held to the light, bare-handed as always, bees walking over her knuckles and into her sleeves like it's a road they know. The smoker on the post beside her breathes out burnt sacking and dry grass.

"When?" says Ma.

I stop.

"Tomorrow. Noon. Odile's going back, and the Chief Examiner, and Hask. There's a seat." I swallow. "It's mine if I want it."

"Do you want it?"

"Yes."

Ma turns the frame. Capped honey one side, pale as a fingernail. Brood the other, and nurses all over it, heads down.

"Hold this," she says.

So I hold it. Her hands come off and mine go on, and a whole frame of bees stops what it's doing and turns and tastes me, all at once, the way the meeting house turned to look at me at the Calling.

And I can smell them back. Warm wax and honey: content. A thread of pear drops off a guard at the corner, who's this, going out of her as she decides. And then, all over the comb, something I know. Clover. Ma's hands. Me.

"They know you," says Ma. "You were in a basket at the end of this strip before you could sit up. You'd scream if I went out of sight, and they'd come and walk on your blanket, and you'd stop." She takes the frame back and slides it home. Her hands are steady as two shelves. "You'll have to tell them."

"Tell them what?"

"That you're going. You tell the bees. Births and buryings and goings. Your gran told them for your da. I told them for you, the night you went out the hatch, with Tilly holding the lamp. If you don't, they think they've done something wrong, and they sulk, and the honey goes thin."

You don't laugh about bees. Not with Ma.

I put my hand flat on the lid of the third hive. The wood is warm with them. Through my palm, ten thousand small lives going about their work, and the note of them all together, which isn't the dome's note. It's warmer. It has opinions.

And I tell them. Not out loud. The way the Hiveplains taught me: in sweat and skin, what Ma has done all her life with smoke and voice. I think it as hard as I can and let the voice behind my eyes write it out of me. Warm wax, and the strip, and me in it. Then the smell that came in on the wind three days ago, dust and dry grass and far off the green musk of the Spill, and me in that too, going away along it. The tracery on my wrist glows green-gold in the shade of my sleeve.

The hive goes quiet.

Then a thread of something comes up out of the entrance, thin and cool and sharp. Then more. Guard after guard turns at the mouth with her tail in the air, fanning, and the smell comes off them in a stream past my face and up into the wind.

Lemon balm.

Come home.

The second hive does it. Then the fourth. Down the whole row, every guard at every mouth, fanning lemon balm out over Saul's tomatoes until the east end of Firstfield smells like the herb bed behind the schoolhouse after rain.

They're not asking, says the voice behind my eyes, very softly. It isn't a question. It's directions.

Ma stands in it with the bees going round her. Her face doesn't do anything for a long time. Then she puts the lid on the hive, very carefully, and wipes her hands down her apron.

That's when they shake.

Not in the hive. Never in the hive. Afterwards, standing in the clover with nothing in them, they shake so hard she has to fold them together, and that doesn't help either.

"Hands first," says Ma, looking at them. Her voice is quite steady. "Worry after." She breathes in. "Well. It's after."

So I take them. Both. The way she took mine three days ago in the square. I turn them over: small and brown and freckled all over with old stings, warm, shaking. I hold on till they stop.

"Ten," I say.

Ma laughs. It comes out wet.

"Whose are you, then?" she says. "Out there. When they ask."

So I tell her, standing in the lemon balm. Wenna's, and Ada's, and Tilly's. Saul's, Jory's, Master Aldous's, Dr. Ommery's. Lenna's. Teasel's, because he gave me a pear. Hask's. Odile's. Sedge's. Biscuit's, probably.

Ma listens to the whole list with her head on one side, the way she listens to a hive.

"That's a lot of people's," she says.

"It's still you first."

"I should hope so. I did the work." She pushes my hair off my forehead and finds the bump in my nose with her thumb on the way down. "Bring them. Some time. All that list. I'll want a look at them."

"Hask can't fit through the door."

"Then I'll feed him through the window," says Ma, "like a horse."

◆ ◆ ◆

Gran and Lenna Brook are sitting in the sun at the open end of Saul's rows, where the wind comes in through the gap.

Gran's in her own chair, carried out from the stove, with the dip in the seat. Lenna's on the schoolhouse stool with a cushion. As far as I can tell they have not stopped arguing since Lenna got off the Chief Examiner's ship.

"It's the fruit forgets the root," Gran is saying.

"It's the fruit forgets the root, the silly thing," says Lenna. "Your mother said the silly thing. I heard her."

"My mother never said silly in her life."

"She said it about you."

Lenna is leaning on the arm of Gran's chair, and her skin's gone, slowly and patiently, the dark polished brown of the old wood, and where her cheek rests on Gran's shoulder it's going the grey-brown of the shawl. It's like being glared at by a very small old woman made of furniture.

I sit down on the soil between them, by Saul's last bed.

"You're off, then," says Lenna.

"Tomorrow."

Her one good eye goes over me, plated feet to long grey hands to the gill-slits. Her mouth does something complicated.

"Sixty-two years," says Lenna, "every time that bell rang twice, I'd count off the years and go and wait where they leave you. Out there, in the long grass." She points her stick at the hills. "I've dug up four. I sat with one for nine days." The wind moves her short white hair. "You're the first I didn't have to go and get."

I can't say anything.

Gran puts her hand out sideways without looking and finds Lenna's on the chair arm. Lenna's fingers go, slowly, the colour of Gran's.

"Go and see, then," says Gran.

"Somebody in this family ought to," says Lenna, at exactly the same moment.

They both stop and look at each other.

"That's mine," says Lenna.

"You said it badly."

"I was a girl. You were crying on my good shoes."

"I was not."

"You were snivelling, Ada Hale, all the way down the stair, and somebody had to—"

"Gran."

They both look at me as if they'd forgotten I was there, which they had.

Gran reaches down into the side of her chair, where she keeps her carving knife and her bits of wood and apparently everything else, and brings out a jar.

Stoneware, brown, the size of my fist, with a cork and a scrap of cloth tied over it with string. I know before she gives it me. I can taste it through the stoneware: sour and yeasty and cool, the little lives in it saying what they've always said. Two hundred years old and not one day tired.

"One spoonful," says Gran. "The winter the crock died, that's all it took. One spoonful, and the bread came back." She closes my long fingers over the jar. "In case wherever you're going hasn't got proper bread."

Oh, says the voice behind my eyes. She's giving you the first copy.

"Feed it every morning," says Gran. "Don't let the otter at it."

"I won't."

"You will," says Gran. "But try."

Lenna is wiping her good eye with the back of a hand gone the colour of a chair.

"Old crow," she says, thickly.

"Old gatepost," says Gran.

And they're off again, about whose mother said what on which stair, and I sit between them in the dirt with the jar in my lap and the lemon balm still drifting over from the hives, and I don't go anywhere for a long time.

◆ ◆ ◆

The rest of the day goes the way the day before a Calling goes. Everybody wants a bit of you.

Old Saul is on his knees by the three flat mounds, with his stick laid in the dirt beside him, which he never does.

"Nothing," he says. "Told you. Too cold. Too much wind."

I kneel beside him and put my palm on the bed, and listen. Under my hand, first knuckle deep and not a hair more, something so small I nearly miss it. A creak. A seed that's drunk its fill and is pushing at its own coat from inside, like Tilly in a jumper that's too small.

"They're not dead," I say. "They're swelling."

Saul looks at my hand on his soil. He looks at me. Then he looks again.

"Not up," says Saul.

"Only nearly."

He grunts. Then, without looking, the way he's done since I could walk, he reaches into his waistcoat and holds out a paper twist, new and white and folded tight. It rattles.

"This year's," he says. "Off the old plant we sat up with. Stubborn." He goes back to scowling at the bed. "Find somewhere that's never heard of a tomato. Then look twice."

Master Aldous gives me a book across the schoolhouse table, without a word. Small and green and old, with a new cloth spine, carefully glued. I know it before I open it: the book from the old part of the library, with the poem in it, the one he took away to mend.

"It took me rather a long time," says Master Aldous.

"How long?"

He checks his silver watch, as if it will know. "Four years. I'm a very slow binder."

On the page with the poem, he's pressed a clover flower, gone brown and flat as paper. The stars like dust, it says, right underneath.

Dr. Ommery comes to read that evening, the way she used to. She doesn't read. She sits at our table with the honey cake and her hands round her cup a bit too tight, and Gran watches her from the stove, and Lenna watches Gran.

"What did you learn today?" says Dr. Ommery. Exactly the way she always asked it.

There's a lot I could say. That you can tell bees. That seeds creak. That two old women can argue for sixty-two years with a gap in the middle and not lose their place.

"That nobody minds," I say, "if you mend things slowly."

Dr. Ommery puts her cup down. From the stove, Gran says, "Eat your cake, Margit," which is a name I didn't know Dr. Ommery had. And after a moment, she does.

◆ ◆ ◆

Jory catches me in the lane at dusk. He falls in beside me with his hands in his pockets, kicking a stone, and we walk to the end of the lane and back, twice, the way we did on nights before a Calling when we were too small to be called and too big for bed.

"Bet you won't come back," he says at last.

"Bet I will."

"Bet you won't bring me anything."

"Bet I will."

He kicks the stone into the mill race. Plop.

"Then it's me," says Jory. "Standing up in there. Keep, or go and see."

"Bet you won't."

He turns round. His eyes are red. He's grinning like a fence with planks out.

"Bet I will. Bet I'll be faster at it than you. Bet I'll get a ship with two stone men."

"There's only one Hask."

"Then I'll get the second one," says Jory, "and you can have the dust off him."

◆ ◆ ◆

There's a glow in the bell tower.

Not a lamp. Up in the belfry, where it's open on four sides to the sky. When I stop at the foot of the tower and put my hand on the stone, I can hear two hearts up there. One big and slow, with a hitch every few beats like a cart wheel with a flat place. One quick and light and odd, the way a bird's is odd.

Davin sleeps up there. He won't sleep under a roof he can't see the sky through. And every night since the hop came down, Toby Marrow has climbed the stair, with that hip, with a blanket under his arm, and sat on the boards beside his son till morning. Nobody says. Everybody knows.

"Bram." Davin leans out over the rail, mottled like leaf-shade in the dusk.

"Davin."

"Going," says Davin. He doesn't use questions much.

"Tomorrow."

He tips his head one way, then the other. The green-apple smell comes down the wind from the belfry, twice over, from two people.

"Whose?" he says. It's what he asked me at Highwater, every time, when he couldn't answer it himself.

"Lots," I say.

Davin nods, satisfied, and goes back in. I hear him settle, and his father's heart do its flat-wheel hitch, and settle too.

"Me too," says Davin, out of the dark. "Now."

◆ ◆ ◆

Sedge is up the apple tree.

She's been up it every night. It's the only thing in Firstfield with proper branches, and she says the houses make her feel like she's been put in a drawer.

I climb up. The catch-pads find the bark and hold, tk, tk, and I come up through the leaves to the fork near the top where she's sitting with her back to the trunk and her long feet hooked under a branch, looking up through the glass.

Third night of stars. Firstfield has got used to them about as much as you'd get used to being hit with a pan. There are still people out in the lanes with their heads back. With my night eyes the stars are silver, and so is she.

"You're going," says Sedge.

"Tomorrow."

"I know. You looked at the door all through breakfast."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because you did."

She's got her right wrist up, turning it, watching the tracery on it, faint green-gold like leaf veins. Mine is on my left. When we sit like this, the two of them face each other.

"I'm not coming," she says.

"I know."

"She's been on her own two hundred years." She says it the way you'd say my gran's chest is bad. "I'm not doing that to her again for a view. And Teasel's roof'll come down the next big tide if nobody's there to shout at him. And Wren's got to learn the knots. And Odile says I'm a person on paper now, so somebody has to be one."

"I know."

"Stop saying you know."

"I don't know what else to say."

"Then don't say anything." She looks at me, finally. She smells of a wire pulled tight, and under it, faint, of a green stick cracking. "You'll be the worst fish they ever met out there. You'll drown in your first bath. Your neck'll slam shut and some four-armed lady'll have to fish you out."

"Bet I won't."

Sedge goes very still.

"Bet you won't," she says.

"Won't what?"

She doesn't say. She just looks at me, her mouth trying very hard to stay straight and failing at one corner, the way it did in the sea. The tight-wire smell goes tighter.

And I understand what she's daring me to.

Oh, says the voice behind my eyes.

"Bet I will," I say.

And I try. I lean in, and she leans in, and we both lean the same way, and my nose hits her cheekbone with a small tock, the bump first, and all six of my gill-slits slam shut at once, flap, so loud in the quiet that a bird goes off out of the next tree.

Sedge makes a noise into my face that is not a word.

She's laughing. She has to grab the branch. I'm laughing too, my donkey-being-surprised laugh, and the whole tree is shaking, and leaves are coming down on the lane.

"Your neck," Sedge gasps.

"I know."

"Hold still. You're terrible at this. Hold still."

So I hold still. She puts one long hand flat on my chest, over my heart, as if she's holding a door open, and leans in slowly, the way you come up on a bird, and kisses me.

It's very short. Soft, and a bit crooked, and she tastes of the apple she stole off the tree we're sitting in. My gills stay shut. My heart is going like something you could hear through a wall.

She sits back against the trunk. Her face has gone red in patches, the way her freckles go in sun.

"There," says Sedge. "Done. Don't look at me."

"I'm not."

"You are."

Bram, says the voice behind my eyes. Puzzled, and patient, and something else I've never heard it be. Your heart rate is doing something unusual. It started in the sea. It's been doing it ever since. A pause. Shall I fix it?

"No," I say. Out loud. Quietly, this time.

Good, it says.

Sedge looks at me. "No what?"

"I said it to my wrist."

She looks down at her own wrist. The tracery there is glowing a little brighter than it was. Her mouth goes crooked again.

"Tell it mine says the same," says Sedge.

She takes a bit of brown cord out of her pocket, the sort the Unwritten use for everything, and ties it round my left wrist over the tracery, in one movement and three heartbeats, a knot I can't see the start of.

"So you don't forget your neck."

I go through my pockets. Gran's jar. Saul's twist. A moonpear stone. And the reed: not the first one, that's in Odile's evidence with its eight knots, but the one after, that's been through a cave and a hive and the sea in my pocket. Brittle now, grey-green, as long as my forearm, a knot for every day, the last ones tied in a hurry by a black pool, and in a loft, and in a loft.

I hold it out. "Keep count. Till I'm back."

Sedge runs it through her long fingers, reading the knots the way she reads the guide-ropes in the reef.

"You tie them badly," she says.

"I know."

"I'll tie them properly. One a day. And when you come back, I'll hit you with it."

"That's fair."

From our loft window, across the lane, at the top of her voice, Tilly says: "I SAW THAT."

Sedge goes the colour of the shawl.

Then Ma's voice, from inside, very tired: "Bed."

And Tilly goes, at a pace of about one step per minute, protesting.

◆ ◆ ◆

In the morning, Ma gives me a coat.

It's been on the peg by the blue door every day of my life, under Gran's shawl and Tilly's cloak, and I never once thought whose it was. Heavy grey-green wool, gone thin at the elbows, patched with brown, big in the shoulders.

"It was your da's," says Ma. She doesn't look at me. She's cutting bread. "I took it in last night."

It fits. Nearly. It smells of the peg, and wool, and woodsmoke from a lifetime by our stove, and under that, so faint it's nearly not there, in the seams: sawdust.

Gran opens one eye from her chair. "If you're going to Margin Station," she says, "take a coat."

"I've got a coat."

"So you have." She looks at it a long time. "So you have," she says again, differently.

I kneel and she takes my chin and turns it to the light, left and right. Her fingers smell of woodsmoke and old wool.

"Hale chin," she says. "Go on, then."

I kiss her forehead. Lenna's asleep on the bench by the wall, gone the colour of her blanket.

We'll say goodbye at the house, everybody said. Not at the Window, like the Shining folk, with glass in the way. But when I come out of the blue door, the lane's full. And the square's full. And all along the east walk where it dips to the ground, on the inside of the glass, there are children. Lined up. Ready to breathe.

The Window's open. The Window's a door. They could just come out. They don't. They stand where they've always stood, and as I go up onto the east walk and along it, slow, toward the far door, they breathe on the glass, all at once, great foggy breaths.

And they draw.

A sun. A tomato. A dome with a door in it. Bet draws a goat, very rude, and Jory beside her says, "Good." Somebody I don't see draws a pile of stones with a tiny bud on top.

Hask is walking the tube behind me, his top stone scraping the curve of it. He stops where three of the smallest children have their hands flat on the glass at his height, and lowers his top stone against the other side of their palms.

"Item eighty-eight," he says, to nobody. "Unwelcome hands." A long pause. "These are not."

At the end, where the glass comes lowest, Tilly has her nose already flat on it. She breathes the biggest fog of her life, and in it, with her tongue out, she draws backwards, so I'll see it the right way round: a tomato with a mouth. Open. Singing.

She puts her hands flat on the glass. So I put my palms against hers, and listen the other way, through the bones.

"—mine," says Tilly, clear as if she's in my skull. "Say it's mine."

"I'll say it's yours."

Ma comes up behind her and puts her hand on the glass beside Tilly's. Bare, brown, freckled with stings. Steady.

I put my other hand to it. And through the glass and the bones of my arm and the hinge of my jaw, Ma's voice comes, not crying, just the way it comes across the kitchen with a basket in the air.

"Breathe properly," says Ma.

I laugh, like a donkey being surprised. And I breathe. A great foggy breath from the bottom of my one good set of lungs, onto my side of the glass, between our hands. And in the fog, with one long grey finger, I write, the right way round, so she'll see it backwards and know.

A B. With the bottom loop too big.

Then I go out through the far door. It sighs. On this side, there's a doormat.

I step over it.

◆ ◆ ◆

I get as far as the tram.

The Preserve tram runs from the foot of the hill to the spaceport, an hour along the valley, a long white car with grey seats that sigh when you sit in them. Odile sits opposite me. Hask rides the open flatbed at the back, with his top stone turned to the sky. Chief Examiner Halloran takes two seats and most of the aisle, grey all over, slow as a cart horse, and opens a dented biscuit tin with a faded lighthouse on the lid. He cracks a sunflower seed in his teeth and spits the shell in. Tock.

My shirt squeaks.

I go very still.

Something under the sailcloth, against my ribs, turns round twice. And out of the collar of Da's coat comes a soft amber glow, like a lamp under a blanket, and then a whiskered head, which looks straight past me at Halloran's tin with an expression of total love.

"I left it," I say. "I gave it to Tilly. Last night. I put it in her arms, it was asleep, she promised she'd—"

"She hugged you," says Odile. "At the bottom of the lane. With her arms right inside the coat."

I remember. Tilly hitting my middle like a sack of flour off a cart, and holding on much too long.

It says, says the voice behind my eyes, translating, that the small loud one put it in. It says she told it to look after you because you're useless. It says it agrees. A pause. It says you smell like a disappointing fruit. It says it would have come anyway. It says, mostly, it would like a seed.

Biscuit flows out of my collar, across the aisle, and up onto Halloran's stomach, where it sits on the lighthouse with its throat glowing, looking up at him.

Halloran looks down at it. He looks at Odile.

"Pastern. Is this on the manifest?"

"It's evidence, sir."

"Of what?"

Odile thinks.

"The Undercroft," she says.

Halloran considers the otter. He takes a seed out, cracks it, and holds out the half without the shell. Biscuit takes it with enormous delicacy and eats it with its eyes shut.

"Then file it," says Halloran, "as luggage."

◆ ◆ ◆

The spaceport is a long glass vault with a garden down the middle, and I stop in the doorway so suddenly Hask nearly walks into me, which would have been the end of me.

Fur-trees. A row of them, short and silver and dense as a cat's belly, and when the air vents come on they ripple, and give off a warm smell like a dog asleep in the sun. A small sign says PLEASE DO NOT PET THE TREES.

I walk straight over and put my hand on one.

It purrs. The sound they make in the Spill when they cool, the one I slept in for nine nights in a root hollow with the grazers piping overhead. Its sap says something slow and warm about rain, and about me.

"Bram," says Odile, resigned. "The sign."

"I slept in one."

"I know. I found the hollow." She looks at the tree, which has gone smooth and flat and pleased all down one side. "They always look smug. Somebody always pets them."

And the hall. It's full of people, and the people are full of everything. A man so tall he stoops under the departures board, with skin like a lizard's and a baby in a sling with exactly the same skin. A girl with feathers that aren't in her hair; they are her hair. Two old men arguing in a language made of clicks.

"Breathe," says Odile.

The customs queue snakes between the planters. Ahead of us, a family is arguing.

You can't hear it. They're tall and narrow and soft-looking, like something folded out of felt, four of them, two big and two small, with long quiet faces. They stand close with their heads bent, completely silent, and the air round them is roaring.

Citrus, peel-sharp, off the biggest. Then back from the next: burnt sugar, hot and dark and offended. Citrus. Burnt sugar, more.

The smallest one sneezes.

"Kasper-Tul," says Odile, low. "They argue in scent. It's considered private. Don't stare."

I'm not staring. I'm smelling. And I can read some of it. Their scent-words aren't bees' words, or the Undercroft's, or Highwater's, but under the citrus there's a thing I know from the hives, you, pointed. And under the burnt sugar there's a thing I know from Tilly at breakfast: I did NOT.

They're arguing about who packed something, says the voice behind my eyes, fascinated. The small one's allergic to both of them.

The smallest one sneezes again, miserably.

And I can't help it. I've spent three days in a village where every feeling in the air was about me. Here's a sneeze I can do something about.

So I think it, in sweat and skin, the way I told the bees. Honey and warm wax, content. The bakehouse at six in the morning, the first loaves, all's well, all's well. And let it go out of me in a little cloud toward the smallest Kasper-Tul.

It stops sneezing.

It turns its long soft face round. So do the other three. The whole family, mid-argument, turns and looks down at a village boy in his da's coat.

The air round them goes completely quiet.

Then, from the biggest one, slow and careful, as if it's trying out an accent it learned off a tin: a smell. Faint. Warm. Unmistakable.

Fresh bread.

The smallest one makes the first sound any of them have made: a high wheezing hiccup, over and over, its whole felted body shaking. The middle one puts a long hand on its head and smells, very gently, of oranges left in the sun.

It's laughing, says the voice behind my eyes. I think you told it a joke. A pause. I don't know which.

Odile has her flask halfway to her mouth.

"What did you just say to them?"

"Good morning," I say. "I think."

"In Kasper-Tul."

"In bakehouse."

Behind us, Halloran cracks a seed. Tock. "Pastern. Is the luggage always like this?"

"Yes, sir," says Odile, and drinks her tea, and winces, because it's too hot, and from three planters back, where the queue has gone round him like a stream round a rock, Hask says, "Item fourteen."

◆ ◆ ◆

Above the queue a crawl slides by, a strip of light with words in six kinds of writing, one under another. I can read one. The others look like birds' feet, and knots, and rain on a window, and a very small map.

TRANSIT TOLLS FALL ON THE VESSANY LANES · MORIEL: CHANCERY ADOPTS SCHEMA REVISION 59 · "CORRIDOR OF HEARTS" SEASON 341 BEGINS TONIGHT

"What's Corridor of Hearts?"

"Don't," says Odile.

"Is it a book?"

"It's a story, on screens. A hospital ship. People who are in love with each other in very complicated ways. It's been going for three hundred and forty-one years."

"I have seen two hundred and nine seasons," says Hask.

Odile turns all the way round. "Hask."

"It is not enumerated as a harm," says Hask, with great dignity. "I have considered petitioning." A pause like a landslide thinking better of it. "Doctor Vell is not dead. I wish the record to show that it was said."

And while I'm trying very hard not to laugh in a customs queue, it comes up through the floor.

A hum. Low and huge, through my soles and up my shins into the hinge of my jaw, like the dome's note but warmer, and rising.

Behind us in the queue there's a tank on a little floating sled, with a woman in grey walking beside it holding a lead the way you'd walk a dog. In the tank, hung in clear water, there's something like a sunrise: pink at the bottom going gold going pale, slow folds of light turning over and over on themselves, like the sky over the hills the first morning I ever watched one.

"Lumen-choir," says Odile. "A courier. They carry messages between the big houses. In the song."

The people near it are rubbing their jaws. That's all they get, I think: a buzz in the back teeth. But my ears are only the porch. The house goes further in. And I hear it all.

It's carrying something enormous and complicated across the whole of everything, for somebody. And underneath, low down, the part it isn't carrying for anyone, the part that's just it, there's a tune.

Three notes up and one down, and round again, like the mill wheel.

And I'm up high, higher than the bakehouse roof, with two fistfuls of hair and a big hand round each of my ankles, and coming up through a warm wool back into my knees, wordless, that song. We're going down the lane. Everything smells of sawdust. I'd lost the tune for ten years. All I had left was that there was one.

I don't decide to. My mouth just does it.

I hum it back. Out loud. Badly, through the nose. Three up, one down, round again.

The tank stops.

All that slow light stops turning. Goes still. Gathers itself at the top, gold, into a point.

And it sings it back to me.

Not the message. That. My da's tune, bigger than anything I've ever heard, bigger than the bell, through the floor and the walls and the glass roof of the hall, so that every fur-tree down the row ripples at once, whhhh, and the whole queue stops with their hands to their jaws, and the clicking men stop clicking, and the lizard man's baby wakes up and doesn't cry.

Three up. One down. Round again. Once.

Then the light turns over, pink to gold, and takes up its message where it left off. The woman in grey looks down at her tank, then at me, with her mouth open.

"It's never done that," she says.

I can't answer. Biscuit's throat, through my shirt, is lit so bright it's nearly white, pulsing slow, in time.

Odile has put her flask away.

Halloran has stopped cracking seeds. "Pastern," he says at last, quietly, for him. "You said he reads things."

"Yes, sir."

He looks at me for a long time. Then at the tank. Then he puts a seed in his mouth and doesn't crack it.

"Well," says Halloran. "Well."

Which from Halloran, Odile tells me later, is practically a speech.

◆ ◆ ◆

The customs officer has gills, a fan each side of her neck that flutters when she talks. She looks at my papers: one sheet, in Odile's hand, with a seal the size of a dinner plate. Then at me.

My gill-slits flutter back at her. All six. They do that now when I'm nervous, instead of slamming.

Hers go still. Then, very slightly, they flutter back. A small sideways thing, like a nod between two people from the same lane.

"Anything to declare?"

My shirt glows. A whiskered head comes out of the collar, sneezes on her desk, and goes back in.

"Luggage," says Odile. "Filed by the Chief Examiner."

The officer stamps the sheet. "Fold safely," she says.

◆ ◆ ◆

The ship is a long grey thing with blunt ends, sitting on its belly in a bay at the end of the hall: the plainest made thing I've ever seen. A tin for keeping something in.

Inside, it smells of other people's journeys. Every padded grey seat, worn shiny on the arms, smells of somebody: a woman's soap, a child's sweets, oranges, worry, a hundred thousand people who sat here and went somewhere and left a little of the going behind. There's a screen at the front of the cabin as big as the meeting-house door, and on it, the outside.

Hask settles into the cargo-rated seat behind us with a sound like a cart being unloaded. The bud between his seventh and eighth stones is fatter than it was in the cloud forest, and still shut tight, a fist round something.

The floor starts to hum: hard and bright, like a wasp in a jar. I put my hand on it.

"It always sounds like that," says Odile.

"It's happy. It likes this."

She looks at me sideways. "That's not in the manual."

Then the floor pushes. Up, all at once, like a hand under me, like the grazers lifting off the tower, a great soft shove through the seat and my bones that goes on and on, and on the screen the spaceport drops away, the hall, the valley, the Preserve, all going small, and for one second, on a hill, far off, catching the morning like a drop of water—

The dome.

A soap bubble on the hill. Then a glint. Then not even that.

I watch it go. I want to see it go.

And then I see the rest.

Moriel. Not a place. A ball, curving across the bottom of the screen, blue and green and gold and white, turning slow. The gold is the Pale, flat and enormous to a line where it stops. Dark and rough as moss north of it, the Spill. A frayed coast with the sea coming in on it in white lines, and somewhere in all that green a tree with a fire basket on top and a girl who'll be tying my knots tonight. A ridge with cloud sitting on it like a held breath.

I could cover the lot with my hand.

The world is Moriel, and Moriel is the world.

And past the edge of it, where Master Aldous said there was only more edge—

The stars.

Not like the dome these last nights, coming out one at a time over the ribs like shy people at a Calling. Out here there's no air to shake them, and they don't twinkle. They burn, steady as nail heads in a door: white and gold and blue and a hot dark orange like the inside of the smoker. So many that between them there isn't any dark, only more of them, smaller, and a long pale smear across the middle like flour spilt on a black table.

I make a noise. I don't know what noise.

"Forty thousand worlds in the Tally," says Odile, beside me, not looking at the screen. She's looking at me looking at it. "Those are the ones the Register has rows for. And past them, things it hasn't got a word for."

Bram, says the voice behind my eyes. Not hungry. Not even curious. Small. Bram. There's so much of it. I've been in a pond for two hundred years, and all that was out here. A long pause. Can we read it? All of it?

"One at a time," I say.

◆ ◆ ◆

Nine hours is a long time to be amazed. Somewhere in the third I fall asleep against Da's coat and wake with Biscuit across my throat like a scarf, snoring, full of sunflower seeds. Halloran snores too, with the lighthouse going up and down on his stomach. Odile works on her slate the whole way, her thumbnail glowing, her tea going cold and her drinking it anyway.

"I have crossed between the stars eleven times," says Hask, behind me, out of a long silence. "In the holds of ships. This is the twelfth. It is the first time I have had a seat."

"I'm the twelfth too," I say.

"Yes," says Hask. "I had noticed."

◆ ◆ ◆

The light over the screen goes amber.

Odile puts her flask away. After a moment, without looking, she puts her hand on the armrest between us, palm up.

I take it.

"It's only the fold," she says. "It's very quick. It's just your stomach—"

The ship folds.

The stars on the screen go, all of them, in one direction, like water down a drain. And I go too. The ship goes one way and I go the other, and then I'm the right way and the ship's the wrong way, and the seat's above me and below me at once, and the floor's hum goes up so high it's a colour, and my stomach—

My stomach decides it would rather stay behind.

It stays. Somewhere back there by Moriel, in the black, among the nail heads. I feel it go, a long way back, like a bucket dropped down a well.

And the voice behind my eyes goes silent.

Not quiet. Gone. For the first time since the pond. And I'm alone in my own skull, and it's so empty and plain and simple, it's like the dome at night before I knew what stars were—

Then everything slams back the right way, like a door in a gale. The screen. The stars on it. My stomach, a long way after the rest of me, sulking.

Bram. Shaky. Small. Bram, where did everything go?

"Odile," I say, in a voice like a gate somebody kicked, "where did everything go?"

"Nobody knows. That's the fold. You go round the long way without going any distance." She hasn't let go of my hand. She flexes her fingers, because the catch-pads hold even when you don't mean them to. "It always comes back. It always comes back sulking."

"Item three thousand and forty," says Hask, gravely. "Displacement of the bearer's organs, temporary." A pause. "It is enumerated. I can do nothing about it. I have never been able to do anything about it. I mention it so that the record will show that I minded."

Biscuit wakes up on my throat, looks at the screen, and is sick down Da's coat.

◆ ◆ ◆

The stars are different.

That's what I notice when I've cleaned the coat with a napkin and some of Odile's tea. The long flour-spill is tipped over sideways, and there's a sun off to one side, small and hard and white, that isn't Moriel's.

And in the middle of the screen, there's a light that grows.

A point. A handful of sparks. Then a shape: a wheel of lights round a dark middle, turning slow, the way the Lumen courier turned, and along its rim other lights coming and going, ships big and small, like bees at a hive mouth. I stop counting them at forty.

"Margin Station," says Odile.

"How many people?"

"About eleven thousand. Give or take a ship."

Rows and rows of lit windows, gold and white and the blue-green of the glow under Odile's nails, and behind every one of them, I think, somebody looking out.

I put my hand flat on the wall of the ship, and listen.

Nothing. Our own floor, humming its happy wasp hum. Halloran breathing. Biscuit. Out past the hull, a whole town going round in perfect silence, because there's nothing out there for a sound to walk on. It's still the strangest thing I've seen: eleven thousand people going about their business, and the dark between us swallowing every word.

"Bram," says Odile. "You've stopped breathing again."

So I breathe. Properly.

The station fills the screen, then it's bigger than the screen, then there's only one part of it, a great lit mouth with clamps round it like a hand opening. We go in, slow. The clamps close on us, and a sound comes through the floor and the seat and my teeth, clunk, all the way down to my plated feet.

And with it, the hum.

It comes in through the clamps. Metal touching metal, the way Tilly's voice came through the Window glass, the way the bell comes up a tower: a road for it, at last. Up the hull, into my palm, through the bones of my arm, into the hinge of my jaw. Low, and enormous, and endless.

The dome's note was one note. The whole world thinking one thought very hard and never finishing it.

This isn't one note.

It's eleven thousand. Pumps and engines and fans, and voices, and hearts, and things that aren't hearts, and things I haven't got a word for yet, all going at once, all over each other, arguing. Eleven thousand thoughts at the same time, finishing none of them, and not minding.

Oh, says the voice behind my eyes.

Biscuit puts its head out of my collar and looks at the screen. The station's light isn't sunlight. Biscuit approves. Its throat goes slowly amber, the colour of curious.

Halloran wakes, looks at the screen, and cracks a seed. Tock. "Home," he says, to nobody, the way Gran said it to the ceiling.

Hask unfolds from his shelf, stone by stone, like a quarry getting out of bed. The light over the screen goes green.

I stand up in Da's coat, with the crock in one pocket and Saul's seeds in the other and Tilly's joke in my mouth and an otter in my shirt, a knot on my wrist and my reed in a girl's shirt a whole fold away, and the voice behind my eyes humming three notes up and one down. And I look at the door at the end of the cabin.

It's just a door. It's grey. It's got a handle.

It's the first door I've ever been on the right side of.

"Where are we?" I say. Though I know.

Hask's top stone turns toward me, slow as a sunrise.

"Beyond the Shining," says Hask. "Mind the step."
