What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 9 of 24

Chapter Eight: Variance Within Tolerance

← Chapter Seven: The Hive RemembersContentsChapter Nine: The Third One's for the Sea →

POLLINATOR LINES 18–26 (Hiveplains reserve) · STATUS: STEADY · FIELD VARIANCE: 0.0% · SOURCE: TOWER TELEMETRY, CERTIFIED Where the record holds, the world holds.

— Moriel Bioreference Index, public bulletin no. 6,940, as carried on the news crawl at Margin Station

The bees had been angry for about an hour when we came over the last hill, and Associate Pell, in two copies, was lying in the violet arguing with himself about whose fault it was.

I heard the bees first. Two days of walking north from the Undercroft had taught me the plain's sound the way you learn a neighbour's mill: by the second evening it was simply the floor of the world, a long low chord like a chapel humming the first line of a hymn before anybody remembers the words. On the morning of the seventeenth the chord had broken. What came over the hilltop to meet us was high and thin and climbing, a saw going through tin, and it got into the roots of my back teeth and stayed there.

We had left the ridge on the fifteenth, Hask grinding along behind me with two leaves in his joint. Twice he stopped dead, and when I asked why, he said, "Something walks. Every fifty-one of your counts. A long way off." I put my palm to the ground and felt nothing, and wrote it down anyway. Two hills behind us, all that day and the next, a small grey figure followed our trail, limping with great precision.

On the evening of the sixteenth the wind turned and smelled of honey, and hot wax, and crushed green stems, and we came up onto a crest in the last light and saw the towers. Fat and lumpy and taller than anything in Firstfield, standing out of hills laid in blocks of colour with edges like a ruler's, violet and saffron and a blue that hurt, and every tower lit gold from the inside by the low sun, like lanterns hung in a giant's orchard. The air between them was full of sparks, which were bees going home.

I sat and watched until dark. Then I read, by my own fingernails, that I had six days left.

◆ ◆ ◆

So: the seventeenth, and the saw in my teeth.

Below us lay a hillside of violet, flowers to my chest, and at its foot a tower of grey paper and gold wax with a doorway as wide as a cart. Round its top and over the flowers in front of it, the air was boiling: a cloud of bees the size of a barn, turning on itself like smoke off a bonfire somebody has thrown wet leaves on. Under the cloud lay two grey heaps. One of them was in a net.

"Hask. Stay here."

"I should like to point out," said Hask, "that there appear to be several million of them."

I went down slowly, because I'm not a fool, and then, because I'm not perfect, I did a foolish thing. A bee hung in front of my face, gold-furred, as long as my thumb, the light coming through its wings in two little rainbows, and before I'd thought anything my hand went up to brush it away.

It stung me on the back of the hand.

It went in like a hot needle and then stopped being anything. My hand went numb, then my wrist, a warm spreading nothing, and with the nothing came a feeling I can only call cheerfulness, idiotic and total, climbing my arm. I stood in the violet and thought it was the funniest hand I had ever seen.

Ten more bees came to look. I lowered the hand to my side the way you lower a full cup, and held still, and after a while they lost interest and went back to the cloud.

So. Slow was not cross. Fast was.

I wrote that down left-handed, because my right had become a cheerful stranger, and went on down through the violet like a woman carrying soup.

◆ ◆ ◆

Pell-12 lay on his back by the hive's doorway with his eyes shut, his silver hair still combed flat, his face swollen like a pudding. His compliance wand lay in the grass beside him, fizzing faintly blue, like something muttering in its sleep.

Pell-4 was in the net. It had wrapped him from knees to ears, and inside it with him were perhaps four hundred bees, trapped and saying so. He had his ledger case open on his chest and was writing, very slowly, with one finger, in the pollen on its lid.

The air round them smelled of cedar and warm copier, and of something sharp and sweet I didn't expect: pear drops, like the ones my grandmother kept in a tin for visitors who had been good.

"Examiner," said Pell-4, without turning his head. His baritone had gone thick, every word carried carefully across a stream. "How. Nice."

"Are you hurt?"

"I am. Recovered." His finger crept towards the net-launcher, lying two metres off with its little screen lit. "The launcher. Logs. What it brings in."

I bent, slowly, and read it.

RECOVERED MATERIALS: 1 (one) ASSOCIATE, grade 4. Approx. 400 (four hundred) BEES, ownership not established. Entered to company stock 9:14 am.

It is not right to laugh at a man who has been stung four hundred times. I want that on record. I bent over the launcher with my numb hand pressed to my mouth and laughed until the nearest bees came to see what the matter was, and I had to stop, and stand very still, and think about sad things.

"It was aimed," said Pell-12, eyes shut, "correctly."

"It was aimed," said Pell-4, "at me."

"It was aimed at the boy, and you were in the way, because you were shaking his hand—"

"I was. Being. Civil."

"You were being first."

Pell-4's swollen eyes found me. "He shook. Hands," he said, and under the sting he sounded honestly puzzled, a man handed a form in a language he had studied and still could not read. "He said whose. He was. Then he held out his hand. And then." His finger moved in the pollen. "Pear drops."

The bees were on Pell-4's cuffs and lapel and the backs of his hands, and nowhere else. I didn't understand it. I wrote it down. Both Associates smell of pear drops. The bees are only on the pear drops.

And under it, because I couldn't help it: He shook hands with them first.

◆ ◆ ◆

It took most of an hour to get Pell-4 out of the net, and I did it the way you'd defuse a bomb made of honey.

Every fold I lifted let out twenty bees with the saw in their voices. I learned fast, because the price of learning slowly was a second sting, on the side of my neck, which turned half my face into a warm idiot and made me very fond of Pell-4 for ten minutes. After that I moved as if the air were cold syrup. Lift a fold. Wait. Let them walk off my fingers. Lift the next. The sun climbed, the violet steamed, the scent came off it so thick I could taste it, honey and something peppery underneath like a cut radish, and the gold dust stuck to the sweat on my arms until I looked like a saint on a cheap icon. Dead bees crunched under my knees. I noticed that, and put it away for later.

Hask, who had come down against instructions, stood ten paces off and watched a different sort of bee walk out of the doorway towards him.

It was as long as my hand, dark, with a back of shining plates like a beetle's, and it didn't fly. It walked, heavily, and two more came out behind it and stood on the threshold with their feelers out. Guards. The first climbed onto Hask's bottom stone and began, very thoroughly, to taste him.

Hask went completely still. Then his top stone came down on its ledge of the others, the notary's ell held level like the arm of a careful man measuring a doorway, and stopped a finger's breadth from the guard.

A long grinding pause.

"A little more than one tenth of an ell," said Hask. "Snout to tail-root. Item three thousand three hundred and one does not apply. It is not of the larger sort." Deeper, from the bottom of the stack: "I find I am relieved."

"Because you won't have to act?"

"Because," said Hask, "I was concerned I might have to measure all of them."

The guard found him to be a rock, which was correct, and walked back inside, unimpressed.

When Pell-4 was free I fetched mud from a seep and put a cold handful on his face, and on Pell-12's, and on my neck and hand.

"He said mud was good," said Pell-12 dreamily.

"Who did?"

"The boy. Before he walked off." Pell-12 smiled, slow and stupid, through the mud. "You should put something cold on that."

I sat back on my heels in the violet and looked at the two of them for a while.

"Did he," I said.

◆ ◆ ◆

Pell-7 arrived at half past two, in one shoe, and went into the honey bog.

I watched it from the edge of the saffron. He came over the hill at his precise limp, gold to the knee, his hair still standing up in the crest the Undercroft had given it, my forty metres of brown rope coiled neatly on his shoulder. He took the straightest line towards us, across a lovely little circle of lawn, greener and taller than any grass round it.

The lawn took him to the knee.

"I'd look twice at that," I said.

He stood sunk in something dark gold and slow, and looked up at me with the face of a man to whom nothing will ever again be a surprise.

"Old honey," I said. "Comb gone down under there years ago, leaking. The grass grows greener over it. It's drinking." I lay flat at the edge and held out the end of my own rope. "Push with your legs. It holds harder if you pull."

He pushed. The bog let him go with a long, bottomless suck, like a giant finishing a drink through a straw, and his remaining shoe stayed behind. The gold closed over it with a small, contented gloop. I put a finger in the honey on his trouser leg and tasted it, because I couldn't not: dark and old and soft and the best I'd ever eaten, with a green summery note in it, like hay, from a flower I couldn't place.

Pell-7 sat up in his socks, coiled the rope, and handed it to me with both hands, the way you'd return a borrowed book, with my Form 9 on top. Under his signature was a new line: Returned in full, Hiveplains, day seventeen. Condition: honeyed.

Then he walked down into the violet and stood over his colleagues.

"Seven," said Pell-4.

"Seven," said Pell-12.

"I am filing," said Pell-7, in a voice like a door shut very gently on a very cold room, "a grievance. Against Four, for being recovered. Against Twelve, for recovering him. And against both of you," he took out his pencil, "for getting here first."

◆ ◆ ◆

I left them composing and went to look at the plain, and found I'd been looking at the wrong thing all morning.

In front of the block-fourteen hive, the bees were landing. All along the near edge of the violet they dropped onto the purple heads and went in to the shoulders, and the air over the block was thick and warm and busy, and the chord was back, ragged, like a choir after a fright.

But two blocks over stood a second tower, and its bees were not landing. They flew low over the flowers, a finger's breadth above each head, tasting, lifting, moving on. When I put my ear to that tower's wall the paper was dry and warm and smelled of old wax and nothing else, and inside, under the hum, ran a thin high whine, like a kettle left on low in an empty house. Beyond it a third tower, humming its chord. Beyond that a fourth, whining. A patchwork. As if something had walked across the plain and touched some towers and not others.

I went back to block fourteen and dug.

The Survey teaches you that what's under your boots is a library, if you'll get on your knees and read it. I cut out a square of turf, and another under it, and the side of the hole was a book in layers. On top, a crisp gold litter of the newly dead. Under it a brown mat, wings gone brittle. Under that another, and another, each a pressed brown seam no thicker than a page, with thin grey bands of soil between. Lower down the seams grew thick, almost nothing but bee. I counted them with the point of my knife.

Nineteen, before plain dark earth. The hole smelled of dust and cinnamon and old paper.

I asked the Register what this place was.

HIVEPLAINS RESERVE (former pollination plantations, owner lapsed). Pollinator lines 18–26: STEADY. Field variance 0.0%. Source: tower telemetry, certified. See MORIEL BIOREFERENCE INDEX.

I had seen that word before, on the news crawl over the customs queue at Margin Station, sliding past in six scripts between a toll rise and a soap opera: MORIEL BIOREFERENCE INDEX STEADY. I'd thought nothing of it. It is a word designed so that you'll think nothing of it.

The enamel board at the corner of the block, gone silver in the weather, said this tower's bees were line 22. It also said NO SUDDEN MOVEMENTS, which I could have used at nine that morning, and, at the bottom, in letters the weather hadn't managed to rub out, THE HIVE REMEMBERS WHAT YOU SMELL LIKE. I opened the Index and pulled line 22's daily readings. Hive weight. Forage flights per hour. Hum, as a frequency. Every day for nineteen years.

They were the same.

Not similar. The same, to the last decimal. Hive weight 4,212.6 kilograms, summer and winter, for nineteen years. I scrolled until my thumb ached and the numbers never so much as twitched.

"Hask. What does that look like to you?"

Hask leaned over the slate, which took a while. "It looks very consistent."

"Nothing alive is consistent, Hask."

"I am."

"You're a cairn."

A grinding pause. "I have a leaf," said Hask, with dignity. "Two."

Somewhere at the top of every tower, if the Index was honest about its own source, something was weighing the hive and counting the bees and sending it home; and somewhere between the tower and the Index, the numbers had stopped moving. One good full day, nineteen years ago, picked up and copied out, and copied out, and certified, while the hive under it ate itself down to the comb and the dead piled up in pages under the flowers.

I looked up at the block-fourteen tower. Close to, its paper lay in thousands of chewed swirls like the inside of an oyster, worn so thin in places that the gold comb showed through, and on the comb, like shadows behind a curtain, bees danced: a straight run, a body shaken to a blur, a loop, the run again. Out of the very top, twenty-five metres up, stuck a thin dark spike like the wick of a candle. An iron mast; the bees had built round it. On its tip sat a grey box with a cracked solar cap, the same make as the post at 40-K, and on the box a small green light blinked. Once. A long while later, once.

Still awake. Still writing, every hour, to somebody who had stopped reading.

Four hundred metres west stood another tower, taller by a third, and black. It rose out of the saffron like a burnt tree, split down one side, the wax of its upper half melted and run and set again in drips like candle-grease down a bottle, its mast bent over at the top like a fused fishhook. No bees. The breeze brought me old char and caramel and something like a blown fuse.

"Lightning," said Hask, "hunts the tallest thing."

At the foot of our tower, in the corner where the fallen wax had made the earth soft and sweet, lay a row of four small dimples. A finger had gone in at each, first knuckle deep and not a hair further. They hadn't split. They did nothing whatsoever. I crouched beside them a while and didn't write anything down. I knew what they were.

That evening the towers lit gold again, the humming ones and the whining ones alike, every one beautiful, and I sat with my back to the warm paper of ours and the dead in their nineteen pages under me, and listened to the chord through the wall.

Five days left.

◆ ◆ ◆

Halloran called at dawn on the eighteenth, bacon frying.

"Pastern." Tock. "You queried the Bioreference Index."

I hadn't told him. I'd filed the query. Filing is publishing, if you know where to stand.

"I pulled a series."

"You pulled nineteen years of a series, on an Inspectorate seal, at eleven at night, and somebody at Varro-Tandem's field office was awake to watch you, and now somebody at the Chancery's awake as well, wanting to know why an Examiner on a dead case with five days left is tugging the one number on Moriel that half the quarter's credit hangs on." Tock. "Nine million lines of stock, Pastern. If the Index says steady, the money sleeps at night."

"It's been printing one day for nineteen years. Sir, I've dug a hole. There are nineteen years of dead bees in it."

A silence, with frying in it.

"Then it's Chancery business," Halloran said, quite quietly. "Not yours. You've got five days, and they're for a boy. Send the Chancery a note." Tock. "If you're going to call a thing the size of the Index Forged, you bring me the pen it was written with. Not a hole. Not bees. The pen. Can you?"

I looked up at the mast, and the box, and the faint green blink.

"I don't know yet."

"Then send a note," said Halloran. "I've read your file," which was not the same as goodbye, and the call closed like a log falling in a grate.

Send a note. It was reasonable. It was what I'd have told a junior Examiner myself. Put your name on the bottom, send it up the line, and it sits in a drawer, and the Index goes on saying steady, and everybody sleeps.

I had done it before.

◆ ◆ ◆

My third season on the Survey I was twenty-three, on a slow brown river world in the Corvel reach, surveying a terrace for a settler charter. Two hundred families had paid for a spring landing. The terrace ran four kilometres along the river like a shelf on a wall, and the soil was so black and soft you could push your arm in to the elbow.

The survey lead was Corran Teague. He was kind. He showed you pictures of his three children whether you asked or not, and every season he brought a jar of pickled plums into the field and shared them out on the last night, one each, like sacraments.

On the second-to-last day I was taking cores under the trees at the back of the terrace, and I put my hand on a trunk, and it came away grey.

Silt. A level band of it round every trunk, higher than my head, like a bathtub ring. Above it, in the forks of the branches, hung rags of river weed dry as paper, and wedged in one, bleached white, the shell of a crayfish as big as my hand, left there by water that had stood two metres deep where I stood. The lichen had only just begun to grow over the silt line. A year. Two at most.

I took it to Teague that night in the mess tent, which smelled of paraffin and plums. He turned the crayfish shell over for a long time.

"Pastern," he said. Tired and kind, the way you'd say it to a child holding up something sharp. "Two hundred families need a terrace by spring. The river hasn't been up there in fifty years."

"It was up there two years ago."

"Once. A freak." He gave me back the shell. "Put it in your notes, if you like."

I watched him type it. Terrace: sound. Flood risk: negligible. I watched him sign it. He gave me a plum, and I ate it, and I said nothing.

Eleven years later I looked it up, at a desk at Margin Station, at night, with nobody watching. The settlement moved uphill after its second spring. Some of the houses are still there, the record says, standing in the river to their windows. Nobody drowned. That was luck, and none of it was mine.

Years after the plum, somebody else said my name in that same tired, kind way, at the end of something. That's another file. I keep it shut.

◆ ◆ ◆

I found I'd been gripping the flask so hard its dents had printed themselves in my palm.

"Hask," I said. "I'm going to need the pen."

He looked up the tower for a long while. "The guards have tasted me," he said, "and found me to be a rock. They have tasted you and found you to be an Examiner. I do not believe they hold Examiners in high regard."

"Then I'll have to change their minds."

Before I could start, the Associates left, and I want it recorded, because it was the funniest thing I saw in the Hiveplains, and I'd seen a man entered as stock.

There were three of them and one skiff, on the hilltop on its little beetle legs, with a seat for one. All three stood round it in the dawn and each explained, in a perfectly pleasant baritone, why he should sit in it. Four had seniority. Twelve had flown it there. Seven had suffered most recently. Four observed that he had been formally recovered, was therefore company property, and company property travels by company transport. Twelve observed that Four had just declared himself luggage. Grievances chimed on all three ledger cases until the hilltop sounded like a small, very polite bell tower. In the end Four sat in the skiff, closed the canopy on the other two with a click, and lifted off north without a sound.

"He'll be noting that," said Twelve.

"So shall I," said Seven.

They set off north on foot, side by side and very far from together, one in a perfect suit with a face like a pudding, one in his socks.

Hask watched them out of sight, and made a small sound deep in the stack that I had never heard before and couldn't have described for the Register under oath.

"Hask. Was that a laugh?"

"It is not enumerated," said Hask, "that I may not."

The plain's gold dust had settled thick in his joint overnight, and two bees were walking about in it like clerks in an archive. Beside the first leaf and the second there was a third: a pale twist no bigger than my little fingernail, pushing up through the pollen.

"Hask. You've got another one."

"I am aware," said Hask heavily. "The amendments are becoming a hedge."

◆ ◆ ◆

The wind came up at noon out of the south-west and smelled of salt.

It flattened the violet in long silver waves and lifted the gold dust off the blocks in sheets until the whole plain smoked. I wetted my scarf and tied it over my mouth and breathed pollen and wet wool and sneezed anyway. The hum went up a note and stayed there. All over the plain the bees were coming home early in long gold threads, and far off to the west the sky had gone the colour of a bruise.

Then the squall hit, and the side came off the hive.

A gust like a shove from a giant's hand got its fingers under the thinnest paper on the tower's south face and peeled it off the way you'd tear the crust off a loaf: a curved slab of paper and comb as tall as a door. It fell slowly, and landed in the violet like a dropped mattress.

The hive screamed. There's no other word. The chord tore straight up through the saw, bees gushed from the wound and the doorway, and the air round the tower went dark.

I ran, and then I stopped running, because fast is cross, and walked.

The slab lay on its back in the crushed flowers. Gold comb, and in the cells under thin wax caps the brood, white and curled like grains of rice the size of my thumb; and at one edge, hanging like long pale acorns, three queen cells. The bees came down over it in a living blanket, layer on layer, shivering to warm it. I could feel the heat off them through the air like a stove, and smell it, wax and warm milk.

The first fat drops of rain burst on the comb.

Brood chills. I'd learned that on a Survey world where we kept pollinators in seed domes: a brood comb lying in the rain for an hour is a dead comb. The bees knew it. It wasn't going to be enough.

"Hask!"

He was already coming, in long grinding strides that left holes in the ground.

The wound in the tower was three metres up, a ragged mouth of torn paper with the raw gold comb behind it. Hold the slab in place and the hive would do the rest; they can mend anything with wax, given time, the way the Undercroft mended with thread. It only had to be held.

I knelt and put my hands into the bees.

They gloved me to the wrist at once, warm and crawling, tasting me with a thousand tiny feet. The rain came harder. I didn't look at the bees. I looked at the slab, and where to lift it, and lifted it the way you'd lift a sleeping child out of a chair, one hand under, one behind, slow. It was heavy with honey at the top and warm with brood in the middle. Bees walked up my arms, into my collar, onto my eyelids and the corners of my mouth. I let them.

Hask's top stone came down to me, level and steady as a step. I stood on it with the slab against my chest and my back against his stack, and Hask rose the way a sunrise rises, all of a piece, until the torn mouth in the wall was in front of my face, hot and loud and smelling of the inside of the world.

The slab had bent in the fall. To get its top edge home I had to lean right into the gap, my face against the inner comb, and the queen cells came in against my neck and one of them burst, a soft cold pop under my collar.

Something ran down inside it. Thick and cool and sour-sweet, like yoghurt and wet paper and lemon rind, and the smell went straight up my nose and filled my head, and all around me the bees went still. Not quiet. Still. As if someone had walked into a crowded room and said a name they all knew.

I held the slab with my palms and my forehead and my whole weight, standing on a stone on a stone in the rain, while the bees came out of the dark and covered the seam in a thick gold rope and began to work it. I felt them chewing. I felt the wax go soft and warm under my hands. My shoulders burned. Water ran into my eyes and out of my sleeves. Once the wind came again and the slab bucked, and Hask leaned his whole stack into the tower and held me against it like a book against a shelf.

"Examiner," he said, very calm. "There are bees in my joint."

"Are they harming the leaves?"

"They are inspecting them," said Hask. "Thoroughly."

The rain eased. The seam set. I took my hands away, one and then the other, and the slab stayed, and through the new thin paper the bees on the brood were fanning, and the sound out of the hive came down through the saw, and down, and under it, the chord.

Hask set me down in the wet violet. I was crawling with bees from hair to belt. They walked over my face, my stung neck, my lips, and one by one lifted off and went home. Not one had stung me the whole time I was up there.

My collar came away on my fingers with a smear of pale cream, and the smell of it rose sour and milky and sweet, and somewhere in the oldest part of my skull it said home.

The guards came out of the doorway. The first walked across the trampled wax and climbed onto my boot, and tasted the leather and the laces and my ankle. Then it lifted its feelers and touched my shin, twice, quite gently, the way you'd tap a friend on the shoulder in a crowd. It turned and went back inside, and the other two followed.

"Examiner," said Hask, after a while. "I am obliged to report that you smell of a hive."

I rubbed at the collar with my thumb. The cream went into the weave and stiffened there, and the smell came up stronger, as if I'd only woken it.

"I know," I said. "I don't think it's coming out."

◆ ◆ ◆

At six, with the storm wall standing over the western hills so dark it was nearly green, the Register chimed its double note on the Hiveplains entry I'd followed.

VARRO-TANDEM BIOREFERENCE, FIELD SERVICES. Notice of works. Hiveplains reserve: tower telemetry units, all, scheduled for replacement. Day 19, 06:00. Superseded units to be recovered for disposal.

Twelve hours. The morning after an Examiner pulls nineteen years of a series at eleven at night. I don't believe in coincidences. I believe in field offices that are awake.

"They're going to take the box," I said.

"Is that enumerated?"

"It's maintenance. Nothing personal."

The first thunder came at seven, like furniture being moved in an attic. I counted from the flash. Twenty-four. Eight kilometres; the storm's heart still over the coast. The air had gone thick and warm and smelled of hot stone, the way a room smells just before the stove catches. The towers stood dark and humming, not glowing tonight. I checked what I had: slate, cracked and dark, but my nails would light it; flask, full and scalding, strapped across my back; forty metres of honeyed rope; knife; a pad of paper forms, because paper doesn't need charging.

"Examiner," said Hask. "Item nine hundred and twelve. Harm by weather of the upper air, fire excepted." A long pause. "Lightning is enumerated. I am obliged to prevent it. I cannot reach it."

"Then don't try. Lightning hunts the tallest thing, and tonight that isn't you, and it isn't me." I pointed west at the black tower with its fishhook mast. "That's why it's black. It's been taking the plain's lightning for years. Never be the tallest thing on a hill, Hask. Be the second tallest, next to the tallest."

"Who told you that?"

"A Survey cook, on a world where we lost a radio mast a week."

The guards met me at the doorway in the rain. The first tasted my boot, my shin, and then lifted its feelers to the warm damp air in front of my collar and stood a long moment, tasting.

It stepped aside, and I went into the hive.

◆ ◆ ◆

It was dark inside, and warm as a kitchen, and so loud the sound was a hand pressed flat on my whole body. Honey so thick it was nearly a taste; hot wax; the inside of a sheepskin; and under it, faint and sour, my collar. I held up my hands and the blue-green crescents under my nails lit a hand's breadth of the world.

The heart of the tower was hollow.

The bees had built round the old iron mast the way a pearl grows round grit, and left a shaft about it as wide as a well, running straight up into the dark. Its walls were comb, the cells as big as my thumb, and every inch of it was bees, a living wall glittering in my fingernail light. Hot air came up past me. They were fanning it. The whole hive breathed up its own chimney, the way a house breathes up its stairwell. And on the mast, every forty centimetres, the company had bolted iron rungs, and the bees had waxed every one, so they shone like a ladder made of honey.

I climbed up the inside of a living hive, in the dark, with a storm coming, by the light of my own fingernails, and the bees let me. They walked over my hands on the rungs and away. They hung before my face, tasting, and moved aside. When I leaned my shoulder on the wall to rest, they shifted under it like a crowd making room on a bench. Through the thin comb on every side I saw into the hive, the brood, the stores, the slow gold glitter of honey, and dancers, even now, running their little straight lines in the dark.

At ten metres the first lightning came through the walls.

The whole chimney went gold. Every cell, every bee, every rung, lit from outside like a lantern with a hand round it, and for one long breath I stood in a tower of gold glass full of living things, a thousand thousand of them, all turned to the light. Then dark. Twelve, before the thunder. Four kilometres.

I'm not ashamed to say I stopped there a while and simply held on.

Higher up the chimney narrowed and the heat thinned, and I felt the wind leaning on the tower and the whole hive swaying with it, slow, with a long creak of paper. Rain hammered on the comb like gravel on a window. The hum had climbed to a storm-key, high and tight.

At twenty-four metres my head came out of the top of the hive into the storm.

◆ ◆ ◆

The tower's crown was a rounded dome of paper with the mast standing out of it, slick and black, and the box on its tip at the height of my face, its green light still blinking in the rain. All round me in the dark lay the whole of the Hiveplains, with the wind coming over it like a sea.

I got an arm round the mast and a turn of rope round it and round my waist, and tied a hitch Rook would have checked twice and passed, and held the slate to the box.

It was an old company box. It wanted contact, and it wanted time. The two of them began to talk at the speed of a protocol that had been old when Teague was eating plums.

Telemetry unit HP-22/14. Local store: 19 yr 4 mo. Transferring.

Four per cent.

Flash. In it, four hundred metres west, a white-violet rope of light stood on the black tower's fishhook mast like a finger on a pin, and the whole plain jumped out of the dark at once, every block, every tower, every silver line of rain. One. Two. Th— The thunder came like a building falling over.

Eleven per cent.

I read the numbers in the gaps, by my fingers, as the slate laid them down. Hive weight: falling, year on year, a long slope with little spring bumps where something still flowered that the bees knew, until the bumps stopped coming. Forage flights: falling, and this spring, near nothing. Hum: climbing, a thin rising line. The kettle in the empty house.

And at the very end, yesterday and the day before: the hum dropping all at once like a fever breaking, and the forage flights going up like a rocket. Somebody had come.

Forty per cent. Flash, thunder on top of it. Sixty. The wind tried to take me off the mast and I let the rope take it and hung with my cheek on wet iron. Eighty.

Transfer complete. Checksum valid.

I let out a breath I'd been holding since the Corvel reach.

Then I opened a Discrepancy, on top of a hive in a storm, with my arm round a lightning mast and my fingernails for a lamp.

In the matter of the MORIEL BIOREFERENCE INDEX, pollinator lines 18–26. The Index holds the lines STEADY, variance 0.0%, from certified tower telemetry. The towers hold otherwise. Attached: the raw store of unit HP-22/14, recovered in person from the unit, showing nineteen years of decline, the hive starving in full bloom. The certified series repeats one day's readings, unchanged, for the same nineteen years. The Examiner has also dug a hole. Class: Forged. Correction: the certified series struck, the raw series entered, the Index to publish what the hives say. Case open. Unit HP-22/14 and the whole Hiveplains reserve entered as evidence.

I hesitated over one more line, then put it in.

Note: the block-14 lines and some others are now recovering. Cause under investigation. The cause appears to be polite.

The Register thought about it for no time at all.

MORIEL BIOREFERENCE INDEX · POLLINATOR LINES 18–26 · STATUS: UNDER DISCREPANCY (FORGED). Certified series withdrawn. Raw telemetry entered. Hiveplains reserve: evidence under open case. Works suspended.

Under it, the replacement order quietly turned grey.

Nine hours away, over the customs queue at Margin Station, the news crawl was sliding past in six scripts, toll rises, soap operas, and the line that had said the same word every day for nineteen years was, about now, very neatly, the way the Register does everything, saying something else. And somebody in the queue would look up.

I buttoned the slate inside my coat.

My hair stood up.

◆ ◆ ◆

It started at the back of my neck, a crawling lift like a hand stroking the wrong way, and then every hair on my head rose off my scalp in the rain. The mast under my arm began, very softly, to sing, a thin buzz like a bee trapped in a glass. On the corners of the box and the tip of the mast, little flames came out: blue, pale, heatless, flickering like a stove jet turned right down.

I looked west.

The black tower was down. Broken off at half its height, lying across its block in a long dark smear, smouldering in the rain. It had been the tallest thing on the hill for years, and it had finally put that down.

Now I was.

I didn't decide anything. I pulled the slip end of the hitch, let go of the iron, and dropped into the chimney. Back against the comb, boots skidding on the rungs, bees going up past my face like sparks up a flue, five metres, eight, my coat burning on the wax. I jammed a boot on a rung and stopped, and folded myself small against the wall.

The bees came over me like a blanket. Thousands. A warm humming quilt of fur and wings over my head and back and hands, the way they'd covered the fallen brood in the rain. I shut my eyes and smelled honey and wax and my own sour-sweet collar, and held on.

The lightning hit the mast.

It was too big to be a sound. The whole hive went white through my eyelids, through the bees on my face, white and then gold, one enormous lantern with a sun inside it. The iron beside me jumped. A smell like a hot iron set down on a damp shirt, scorched wax, and something sharp and clean I've only ever smelled in storms. Then the thunder, up through my boots into my teeth, shaking the honey in the comb.

Then dark, and rain, and the hum: climbing to the saw, holding, holding, and slowly, slowly coming down.

The old company ground cable, still good after all these years, had carried the whole sky down the mast's spine into the earth and left us humming in the dark. The bees lifted off me one by one and went back to the comb. My nails glowed. I was alive, and so was the hive.

I climbed back up. The box was blackened and split, its green light out for good, nineteen years cooked in its shell. The only copy was buttoned inside my coat, warm against my heart.

◆ ◆ ◆

The storm had walked on east on its legs of light, and behind it, to the west, the cloud had begun to tear.

Then a long sheet of lightning, cloud to cloud, held for a full breath, and the whole country lit from end to end like a page held up to a lamp.

The Hiveplains, every block silver with rain. The fallen black tower, smoking. And to the north and west, past the last of the blocks, where I'd thought there was only more dark, the land ran down in long dim folds to a line, and the line was the sea. I had never seen this planet's sea. It was grey and white and enormous, and it was moving.

Along its edge lay a long dark band, low and ragged, between the land and the water. Trees. A forest standing in the sea, its crowns a rough dark shelf along the coast as far as the light went.

And out of the trees, in three places, straight up into the clean air behind the storm, rose three thin grey lines.

Smoke.

The flash died. I hung on to the warm wet iron with my hair still standing up, and with my other hand I took out the slate and asked the Register what was on the coast.

COASTAL MARGIN, north-west (tidal forest, flooded orchard stock, origin lapsed). Settlements: none. Population: none. Status: uninhabited.

I looked at the place in the dark where the smoke had been. Somebody out there, in the trees in the sea, where the Register said there was nobody at all, had lit a fire against the storm, and was sitting by it.

Below me the hive hummed its chord. Far below that, a mountain of carved stone with three leaves in his joint was calling up the chimney, very calmly, to ask whether I had been struck, and if so, whether by weather of the upper air.

"Not today, Hask," I called down.

I unscrewed the flask. Steam came out into the rain, and tar, and burnt raisins.

"Item fourteen," came up the chimney, faintly.

I drank it anyway, at the top of the tower in the tail of the storm, with four days left, looking at the place where the smoke had been. For the first time since a boy climbed out of a cleaning hatch, I thought I knew where he was going.
