— enamel placard, Margin Station

The Register had a new word for the boy, and it used it the way it uses every word: instantly, without doubt, and with no idea what came next.

EXERCISED. It made him sound like a horse.

The boy himself was standing in the hatchway of the hop with his palm flat on the hull and his eyes shut, listening to Margin Station.

"It's arguing," he said. "All of it. Everything at once."

I had lived inside that noise for eleven years. I had slept in it and eaten in it and filed in it, and I could have told you its pitch the way you can tell the time by your own kettle. I put my hand on the hull beside his. Cold metal, a faint fine tremor, the ordinary hum of a ring that keeps eleven thousand people from becoming weather. That was all I could feel. He could feel the rest of it. I took my hand back and didn't say so.

The hatch sighed open, and Margin came in.

Air first. Margin's air has been round the station more times than anyone on it. It comes out of the vents warm and very clean and tasting faintly of tin, and of whatever the arrivals deck has been up to lately, which that evening was oranges from the stall at clamp eight and a wet green breath of fog that had got loose from the farm decks on ring three. I stopped tasting it in my first month. Bram stood in the hatch and breathed it in, and the six gill-slits under his ears fluttered, the way they did now when he was unsure, instead of slamming. He made a face.

"It's been breathed," he said.

"Everything here's been breathed. Several times."

"Who by?"

"Everybody."

He breathed again, slower, the way Fallow used to taste a pasty when he suspected the onion. "It has," he said. "It's been through all of them. There's a man with a cold in it."

Then the ceiling spoke.

It clicked first, high in the vault of the arrivals deck, and hissed, and Bram went still from his plated soles to his crown, the way a hare goes still in a field. Twenty-seven days, a planet, a hearing and a fold, and a voice in a ceiling could still do that to him in one heartbeat.

"Hop from Moriel on clamp nine," said the tannoy. "Welcome back, Inspectorate. Spinward tram's running eleven minutes late. Fog advisory continues on ring three, farm decks ask residents not to water anything until further notice. That's all. Oh, and—" A pause. A large, wet sneeze, very close to the microphone. "Sorry. That's all."

Bram looked up at the ceiling for a long moment.

"Bless you," he told it. And then, in a voice that was plainly someone else's, someone who had been saying it to ceilings for years: "Lovely evening for being looked at."

"It can't hear you," I said. "It's a man called Abel in Operations. He's had that cold since spring."

"It sneezed," said Bram, as if that settled something. I suppose, for him, it did. Nobody had sold the rights to Abel.

Behind us Hask came out of the hatch a stone at a time, with the sound of a quarry being let off early, and Halloran came after him with the tin under his arm and the face of a man who has slept nine hours in two seats and resents every one of them. Biscuit put its head out of Bram's collar, looked at the light, found it was not sunlight, and approved. Its throat went amber.

Over the concourse, the crawl went by in six scripts.

CHANCERY ADOPTS SCHEMA REVISION 59 (MORIEL) · "CORRIDOR OF HEARTS" SEASON 341, EPISODE TWO TONIGHT · TRAM DELAYS, SPINWARD LINE · MORIEL BIOREFERENCE INDEX: UNDER DISCREPANCY

Bram read it aloud, slowly, in the one script of the six he could read. Then he looked at me.

"That's us."

"That's the Index."

"That's us," said Bram.

Twenty-seven days earlier I had stood under the same words with STEADY on the end, in a customs queue on Moriel, and noticed them, because I notice everything, and forgotten them, because the queue moved. This time I read them twice. The queue could wait.

Margin's customs desk is one desk at the end of the concourse, and the officer at it had been there longer than I had: a broad, unhurried woman with a ruff of grey feathers at her collar that lifted when she was interested, which was seldom. She had stamped me in and out perhaps two hundred times. She had never once looked up to do it.

"Examiner. Proxy," she said, to her screen. "Anything to declare?"

"Yes," said Hask.

The feathers lifted half a centimetre. "Is it the seed?"

"It is not," said Hask, "the seed."

He had not looked at it since the fold. He had told Bram he would not look until we arrived, so that it would have finished deciding what it was. Now, at the desk, with the queue piling up behind us, he tipped his top stone forward and down, very slowly, toward the joint between his seventh and eighth stones, counted from the bottom, the way a tall man looks at something small that has got into his coat.

The bud had finished deciding.

Among the four leaves, gone dark and glossy with the journey, there was a flower. It was no bigger than my thumbnail. Five petals, cupped, fine as the skin on cooling milk. Bram said later there were six. Halloran swore there were three. And its colour.

I have been trained, by the Survey and by Fallow and by eleven years of the Register, to put a name to what is in front of me and write it down. I looked at that flower for a long time. It was nearer blue than red, and then it wasn't. It was the colour a stone might be if it had been rained on for three hundred years and had kept something back the whole time. There was a smell off it, very faint, of rain on warm rock, and of nothing else at all. I had no drawer for it.

"Oh," said Bram, very quietly, beside me.

The officer had stopped typing.

"I declare," said Hask, "in the joint between my seventh and eighth stones, counted from the bottom, one flower." His voice filled the end of the concourse without being raised, the way a landslide fills a valley. "Provenance: a seed, of unknown kind, arrived on a wind some ninety years ago and lodged without disturbance. Declared on Moriel. Sprouted in the Spill. Leafed in the Undercroft, in the Hiveplains and in the Pale. Watered by a person on my behalf. Budded in the High Gardens, while I was a roof. Opened in transit." A pause, grinding. "I did not see it open. I had decided not to look."

The officer looked at Hask. Then at the flower. Then she reached under her desk and brought out something I had never seen her use: a fan of enamel cards on a brass pin, a couple of thousand of them, each a numbered colour, worn grey at the edges by twenty years of thumbs.

"Tally Standard Chromatic Reference," she said. "Box says colour."

She fanned it open and held the cards up against the flower, one after another. Bluish greys. Greyish blues. A blue like a vein. A grey like a pigeon. Something called Dusk, Corvel, 1,140, which was nearest, and wrong. The flower sat between every pair she tried, like a cat between two chairs, belonging to neither.

Bram leaned in until his nose was nearly on it.

"It's—" he said. He had named his own bones, I thought. The bones of his ear, the rooms of his lungs, his own face, in a kitchen, with his grandmother's hand on it. "It's—"

He shut his mouth. He looked astonished, and then he looked delighted, as if he'd found a door in his house he'd never noticed.

"Brown," said Halloran.

"It isn't brown, sir."

"Everything's brown if you're far enough off," said Halloran, who was not far enough off.

The officer lowered her fan. "I can't leave the box," she said. "Box says colour."

"Then I ask," said Hask, "that it be left empty. An empty box is honest. It admits that it is empty." His top stone turned, a little, toward me. "I have learned that from the Examiner. She had it from a drawer."

The officer looked at the box on her screen for a long time. Her feathers went all the way up.

Then she stamped it without writing anything in it.

"First empty box I've stamped in twenty years," she said, and for the first time in two hundred crossings, she looked up at me. "Is he always like this?"

"He's getting worse," I said.

Deep in his stack, Hask made a small grinding sound, the one that in an Orrun is roughly equivalent to applause. It went on rather longer than it had on Moriel.

"And the boy," said the officer.

Bram put his papers on the desk: one sheet, in my hand, with a seal on it the size of a dinner plate. Then he put down the rest, one thing at a time, the way he'd seen me do it on an elders' bench. A brown stoneware jar with a cork and a cloth tied over it. "Bread," he said. "It's alive. It's two hundred years old. It came from my gran." A paper twist that rattled. "Tomato seeds. Red ones. They don't sing." A shirt that glowed. "Luggage. The Chief Examiner filed it."

"Evidence," I said.

"Luggage," said Halloran, from behind us, through a seed.

The officer wrote down bread (alive) and tomatoes (silent) and luggage (glowing) with the face of a woman who has seen everything and is being shown more.

"Purpose of visit?"

Bram looked at me.

"Residence," I said.

"Name?"

"Bram Hale."

"Of?"

The pen waited. The queue waited. Bram looked down at the brown cord knotted round his left wrist, and at the long grey fingers that caught on the edge of the desk, tk, and held.

"Firstfield," he said. He thought about it. "And here."

She wrote both. There was room. There's always room on that form; I'd simply never seen anyone use it.

Halloran bought the first round at the Weighbridge. He said so as we went in, so that it would be on record, and that it would be once.

The Weighbridge is a bar in what used to be the cargo weigh-station on ring one, and the scale is still in the floor: a brass plate just inside the door, polished by forty years of boots, with a great dial on the wall above the bar and a bell that rings when the needle passes the end. Everyone who comes in is weighed. That's the rule. Nobody remembers why. The place smells of beer and hot mutton pies and the brass polish the barkeep uses on the plate every morning, and it is where the Inspectorate drinks, because nobody else will drink with us.

Bram stepped on the plate. The needle swung, wobbled, and settled on a number a good deal lower than I'd have guessed, and he looked at it with deep suspicion.

"That's not all of me," he said.

"It's all of you that weighs anything."

Then Hask came in.

The needle went round the dial at the speed of a man falling downstairs, hit the stop, and the bell went, as it has gone every time Hask has come into the Weighbridge for eleven years. The whole bar turned round to watch, as it does every time, and raised its glasses, as it does every time.

"Over," said the barkeep, a woman with arms like hams and a second, smaller pair of eyes above the first.

"I have been over," said Hask, "since the Eleventh Chancery." He did not step off. He was looking up at the dial. "Three hundred and forty kilograms. Less four leaves." A pause. "And the flower weighs nothing. I have checked."

"Nothing weighs nothing," said Bram.

"This does," said Hask, with enormous satisfaction, and went to stand in front of the big screen at the back, where the second episode of the three hundred and forty-first season of Corridor of Hearts was beginning, and where, as far as I know, he has stood on every eighth night of his service. Doctor Vell, it turned out, was not dead. Hask did not say so. He did not need to. His stack ground faintly at the swell of the music.

Halloran sat down at the end of the bar on a stool that has had his shape in it since before I joined, set his tin on the zinc and cracked a seed. Tock. Biscuit's head came out of Bram's collar at the sound like a lamp turned up.

"No," said Halloran, without looking.

Biscuit went back in, and sulked, and Bram's shirt began to smell faintly of damp dishcloth.

The pies came. Bram ate his with both hands and his eyes shut, and then ate half of mine, and then, with the gravy still on his chin, turned on his stool to face the Chief Examiner of Margin Station.

"My sister said I've got to tell it to everyone out here," he said. "And say it's hers."

Halloran looked at him with the long, slow, folded look he gives a form.

"Go on."

"What do you call," said Bram, "a tomato that won't sing?"

Halloran thought about it. He thought about it properly, the way he thinks about everything, with a seed in his teeth. On Margin, the tomatoes come up from Moriel in crates, blue as deep water, and hum all the way to the market on ring three. Everybody on the station has heard one.

"What?" said Halloran.

"A tomato," said Bram.

There was a silence. It went on.

Then a sound came out of Halloran. It started low in his chest, like a hop's engine catching, and came up through him and out, and his heavy grey face folded up round it, and he put his big hand flat on the zinc to hold himself up. The barkeep put a glass down very carefully. Two Examiners at the corner table turned round.

That was the fourth time in eleven years. I have started a column.

"That," said Halloran, wiping one eye with the heel of his hand, "is a very bad joke."

"She knows," said Bram.

Halloran's office is on ring one, past the Inspectorate's old record office, and you go to it through the oak door.

It's the only wooden door on Margin Station. Real oak, iron-bound, brought in a ship's hold from somewhere with forests by people who thought an Inspectorate ought to have one. I sat my examinations behind it at twenty-seven. It smells of beeswax and cold stone. I have walked through it a great many times in my head since.

Bram stopped in front of it the next morning and put his palm flat on the wood.

"It grew outdoors," he said.

"It's very old."

"No, I mean—" He moved his hand, the way he'd moved it on Saul's soil. "It grew under a sun. A real one. With rain on it. And wind." He looked down the corridor, at the bulkheads and the deck plates and the green strip of moss somebody in the record office keeps in a trough by the door. "Everything else here grew in a cupboard. It all tastes of the same water. The fruit at the stall. The moss. The bread on the tram this morning." He sniffed. "Pennies and everybody."

"It's recycled," I said. "The water. Everything on the farm decks is grown in it."

"I know," said Bram. "I can taste it."

I wrote that down. I didn't know why. It felt like the sort of sentence that would matter later.

Halloran's office smelled of sunflower husks and old paper and the burnt coffee from the machine in the corridor, which has been broken for nine years and nobody has filed to fix it, because the Inspectorate does not like to be seen asking for things. His chair has been his for thirty-one years. There is a hollow in its seat like the one in Gran Hale's. The lighthouse tin sat on the blotter with Biscuit's tooth-marks in the lid.

He had a form out. A long one.

"Sit," he said.

We sat. Bram sat on his hands, as if they might wander, which they might.

"Pastern says you read things."

"Yes, sir."

"What things?"

Bram thought. "Things that are alive," he said. "And things that were. What they're made of and where they've been and what they're short of." He considered. "Not books. I'm slow at books."

Halloran reached into his coat and brought out a paper bag of sunflower seeds, the kind he buys every week from the same stall, and put it on the blotter between them. Printed on it, in green: MARGIN RING THREE · STATION-GROWN · PREMIUM.

"Read that," said Halloran.

Bram looked at him, and then at me, and I gave him nothing, because I didn't know what Halloran was doing either, and because I have never once in eleven years known what Halloran was doing until afterwards.

Bram took a seed. He cracked it between his front teeth, the way Halloran does, and held the kernel on his tongue with his eyes half shut. I saw his throat move. The tracery on his left wrist, under the brown cord, went faintly green-gold.

"These didn't grow here," he said.

Halloran didn't move.

"They don't taste of the water." Bram's eyes were shut now. "They taste of rain. Real rain, falling a long way, and wind, and a lot of sun. A big yellow one. And chalk in the ground, lots of it. And they're old. Two summers, maybe. They've been in a sack." He opened his eyes. "They grew in a field. Not in a cupboard. Somebody's selling you field seed and saying it's station-grown."

"Station-grown," said Halloran slowly, "is a third dearer."

"I don't know what dearer is," said Bram. "But they're not from here."

Halloran looked at the bag. He looked at it the way I have seen him look at a ministry, a Chancery seal the size of a dinner plate, and once, a Varro-Tandem complaint thirty pages long.

"Thirty-one years," he said.

"Misfiled, sir," I said. "Possibly forged."

"Thirty-one years, I've been paying station price." He took the bag, folded the top over twice, very precisely, and put it in a drawer, and shut the drawer. "Open a case."

"Sir?"

"Not you." He was looking at Bram. "Him. When he's learned which end of a form is up." He picked up his pen. "Field of expertise."

There was a long box for it on the form, and he did not ask either of us what to put in it. He wrote it himself, slowly, in his heavy upright hand, and turned the form round so we could read it.

Where the paper and the life disagree.

Bram read it twice. His gills shut and opened again.

"He's a minor," said Halloran, to me. "On any world in the Tally. He'll want a care of."

"Yes, sir."

"Whose?"

I had written my name on perhaps eleven thousand forms in eleven years. I took the pen. My hand was not steady. I found I didn't mind.

Care of: O. Pastern, Examiner.

"That's what care of means," said Halloran, and tock. "Don't make me write it down." He signed underneath, and pressed his seal on it, and it went into the Register with a small double chime that I felt more than heard. "Consultant. Inspectorate of Discrepancies, Margin Station. Unpaid, until you can do arithmetic." He looked at the boy. "Don't make me regret it."

"You will, sir," said Bram. "Sometimes."

"I know," said Halloran. "Get out."

We got up. He held up a hand. There was an envelope on the corner of the blotter, company grey, the colour of a filing cabinet, addressed in a small, neat, sloping hand: Examiner Pastern, care of the Chief Examiner.

"Came on the company packet this morning," said Halloran. "I didn't open it. I was tempted."

Inside was an Inspectorate Form 9, Assistance Rendered, the paper version, the one I carry in pads in my coat because paper doesn't need charging. It had been filled in, very neatly, in the same sloping hand.

Assistance rendered: none. By me.

Grievance lodged: Pell-7 against Pell-7. For the record, I said I was still going to recover him. I have not. I have been asked by nineteen of myself to account for this and have been unable to give a satisfactory answer. I do not expect to forgive myself. I note that I do not appear to mind.

P.S. The shoes still do not fit.

I read it twice. Bram read it over my shoulder, slowly, his lips moving on recover.

"Do we reply?" said Halloran.

"We file it," I said, and put it in my ledger case, with the one that came back from the Hiveplains with my rope (Condition: honeyed) and the one I'd picked up off a mud bank at Highwater with a grievance against the otter on it, and kept.

On the blotter, between us, I'd set my flask down while I read. Halloran was looking at it.

"Stores have a new flask for you," he said. "Insulated. Issue. No dents."

"No, thank you, sir."

"That one's got four dents in it."

"Yes, sir."

"And holes."

"Two, sir. Tooth."

He looked at me. Then at Bram.

"The fourth one's mine," said Bram.

Halloran looked at him for a while longer.

"Is it," he said.

That was all. He didn't ask. I've wondered since whether he already knew, from the report, or whether he just decided it was the kind of thing a Chief Examiner doesn't need to have explained to him. He picked up the tin. We were at the door when he said, to the tin:

"Pastern."

"Sir."

"Ring two. Same door." Tock. "His knees are worse."

I stood with my hand on the door frame for a moment. Then I said, "Thank you, sir," and we went out through the oak door, and it was cool under my hand, the way it always is in the world, and smelled of beeswax.

I had lived on ring two for eleven years and knew the names of three people on it. One of them was Hask.

The spare room had eleven years of case copies in it, in boxes, floor to ceiling, and nothing else. We moved the boxes into the corridor, and Hask stood guard over them for two days, which drew a complaint from the residents' committee about the boxes and a second complaint about Hask. I answered both, on paper. Then Bram slept in the spare room on a mattress from stores, under a blanket from stores, which smelled of wool and nothing else and so, he said, couldn't keep him awake. I don't know that he slept much. I would hear him get up in the station's night and stand in the corridor with his hand on the wall, listening to the ring argue with itself. Hask lives in the corridor outside my door, because he doesn't fit through it. In the mornings I would come out and find the two of them there, one with his palm on the bulkhead and one with his top stone tipped toward a flower, both perfectly still, like a pair of monuments to two different kinds of listening.

He fed the crock every morning. Flour and water and a pencil to stir it, in the jar on my kitchen shelf, and it muttered all day and smelled of a cellar that was pleased with itself. On the fourth day he baked.

There is a galley at the end of every residential corridor on Margin, with an oven in it the size of a wardrobe that nobody uses, because nobody on Margin bakes; you buy bread on ring three, in paper, tasting of pennies and everybody. Bram cleaned that oven for two hours with a brush and a bucket and a great deal of village language. Then he put in two loaves shaped by hand on a tin tray, and shut the door, and counted to five hundred under his breath before he'd let either of us look.

The smell went down ring two like a tide coming in.

I don't know how else to say it. Crust and steam and the scorched-sugar edge of a bakehouse at six in the morning, in a corridor that had never smelled of anything but tin and other people's breath. And doors opened. Doors I had walked past for eleven years. Merrit, the four-armed night nurse from three along, in her dressing gown. Old Sarno with his second pair of eyes, who had nodded to me in the corridor every day since I came and never once said a word. The Kasper-Tul family from the end, all four of them, folded into the galley doorway like laundry, the smallest one sneezing, the air round them going from burnt sugar to something like warm oranges as the bread came out. A child I'd never seen, with skin that was going, as I watched, the brown of crust.

Bram tore the first loaf and handed it round, hot, and burned his fingers, and laughed his laugh, the one like a donkey being surprised. And to every one of them, before they were allowed to eat, he explained what a tomato was, and then told them his sister's joke, and said it was hers.

By the end of the week, ring two had a joke. Sarno told it to me in the corridor, unprompted, his first words to me in eleven years. It was very bad. He was enormously pleased with it.

On the fifth day Halloran started coming by at the seventh bell, on no pretext at all, with the lighthouse tin under one arm and a second tin under the other, smaller and newer and already dented, with a paper label on its lid in his own hand: LUGGAGE. Biscuit, who had been living in the heating ducts since we arrived and coming out only for meals and outrage, learned the sound of that lid in a single evening.

Halloran gave Bram a box of dead files from the bottom drawer of the record office, cases nobody had wanted for forty years. On the seventh day Bram gave two of them back with a note on each, in his big careful hand with the too-big B. Halloran read them standing up in the corridor, with his coat still on. Then he took the box away and gave him a heavier one.

In the back of Master Aldous's green book, on the blank pages after the poem, Bram had started a list. I saw it once, open on the galley table, and didn't read it, but the heading was at the top in capitals and I couldn't help that. THINGS TO TELL THIRTEEN. The first one was the joke.

The relay from Moriel came on the sixth evening. Three flat notes, like bacon frying. Bram held my slate in both hands as if it might spill.

"Did you tell it?" said Tilly Hale, from a whole fold away, before he could speak. "Did you say it's mine?"

"I told the Chief Examiner. He laughed."

A pause the length of a planet.

"Properly?"

"He said it was very bad."

"It is very bad," said Tilly, in a voice of pure triumph, and then, behind her, faint, crackling like paper in a stove, another voice said, Tell him to take a coat, and Bram laughed so hard he had to sit down on the galley floor.

When the relay closed he sat on for a while with the slate dark in his lap, turning the brown cord on his wrist round and round with one finger. He didn't say anything about it. I didn't ask.

And on the second day, before any of that, he'd gone up to ring three through the fog, and come back wet to the knees and smelling of cress, with a fistful of grit from the farm-deck beds. He took the cup I don't use. I have two. I'd never had a reason for the second one. He put the grit in it, and pressed one of Saul's seeds into it, first knuckle deep and not a hair more, and watered it, against the fog advisory, and set it on the ledge of the viewport at the end of our corridor, under the day-strip, where the light was best.

Then he stood back and looked at it.

"Somewhere that's never heard of a tomato," he said. "Then look twice."

"Who says that?"

"Saul." He looked out past the cup, at the dark. "And me, now, I suppose."

On the eighth evening I walked home from ring one the short way.

You'll want to know why that matters, and there isn't much to tell. Ring two is a circle. My door is a little spinward of the viewport. For seven years I had gone home the long way round, anti-spinward, past the laundries and the school and a chapel nobody uses, which adds twenty minutes to the walk and is very good for the legs. I'd stopped noticing I did it. That's how a path wears in.

That evening I turned left out of the tram instead of right.

Hask came with me, a stone's length behind, as he always is. He did not remark on it. He is very good at not remarking on things, for a mind that has never in three hundred and eleven years been able to stop itself enumerating them.

The door was where it had always been. A residential door like all the others, grey laminate, a number, and a card in a little brass holder at head height, written in a square, upright hand I'd have known anywhere, because I'd learned to sit down first by watching it fill in forms.

FALLOW.

There was a smell coming from under it. Onion, fried slow in butter, and pastry browning. And a sound: some programme with a great deal of music in it, which I would bet my flask was the second episode of the three hundred and forty-first season, and over it, close to the door, the creak of a chair taking a man's weight, and the slow, careful, two-handed shove of someone getting up from a table with bad knees.

I stopped.

I put my hand flat on the door. Not oak. It wasn't warm and it wasn't cold. It was a door.

There's a gully in my head that everything drains toward, and I felt the water start to run, the way it does. I let it. And this time other things came down it too. A woman with a chest like gravel in a pail, standing up in a meeting house and taking a doctor's hand off a stack of notebooks: It'll want doing again tomorrow. I'll do it again tomorrow. A small brown woman, gone the colour of a shawl: you great fool. A boy on the bank of a pond in the Pale, before any of it, his voice still his own: It doesn't go. But it gets to be yours. And the man behind this door, years before he was the man behind this door, on his bad knees on a receiving-office floor, holding up a finger grey with dust.

Chair first, Pastern.

Inside, the chair creaked again. The music swelled. Somebody, on screen, was being told something very important in a corridor.

I didn't knock.

I stood there long enough for the onions to catch a little, and somebody inside to say a word I wasn't meant to hear and get up again to see to them. Then I took my hand off the door.

"Shall I file?" said Hask, behind me, very quietly.

I thought of a kitchen under a dome, the first morning, and a bowl set out clean and empty in front of a chair, and the same two words.

"Not yet," I said.

I took out my ledger and wrote it down, under the line from the High Gardens. Day 35. The short way round. O.P. Then I put the ledger back in my coat, and walked on, spinward, the way I'd come, toward the viewport and the boy and the noise.

He was at the glass.

Viewport 9 is the big one at the end of our corridor: a long, curved panel taller than Hask, with a bench in front of it that nobody sits on and the enamel placard on the frame that nobody reads. Through it, the whole of the dark. Not black. I had thought it was black for eleven years. It's every colour of black there is, and in it the stars, hard and steady and unblinking, and the long pale smear of the rest of them tipped across the middle like flour on a table. The ships came and went at the docking ring below us, small and lit, the way bees come and go at a hive mouth.

Bram had his nose about a finger's width from the glass, and his breath held, and one hand raised. He was waving.

Outside, on the far side of the panel, a hull drone was coming round the ring. A fat silver thing on suction feet, the size of a cart wheel, cleaning the dust of a thousand years of nothing off the viewports one by one, with a little lamp on its front.

"It's the same make," Bram said, without turning round. "Near enough. Different feet."

"As what?"

"As the one in the dome." He waved again. The drone, which had no idea it was being waved at, went on cleaning. "Two hours and six minutes round the ring. I timed it. It docks at a hatch by the farm-deck lock for three minutes and empties its bag."

"Are you planning to leave through it?"

"No," said Bram. "I just like to know where the doors are."

On the ledge by his elbow, under the day-strip, the cup. I'd been watching it for a week, the way you watch a pot. That morning it had nothing in it. Now, out of the grit, a red hook of stem had come up, bent double like an old man tying his boot, with the two seed-leaves still folded together at the end of it, pale, in a scrap of husk.

Small. Red-stemmed. Just sitting there.

I thought of seven of them by a pond on another world, with a notched stick beside them, the first evidence I ever had that he was the kind of boy who stops.

Behind him, on the bench nobody sits on, the dinner argument had been going for some time.

"The Weighbridge," Halloran was saying, with the lighthouse tin on one knee and LUGGAGE on the other, and Biscuit's whole front end inside LUGGAGE. "Pie. It's the eighth night. I've had the pie on the eighth night for thirty-one years."

"I concur," said Hask, "with the Weighbridge."

"You don't eat," I said.

"I do not eat. The Weighbridge has the large screen. Doctor Vell has been accused." He considered. "I object only to the plate. It rings every time. I find I am tired of being a discrepancy in front of people who are eating pies."

"There's bread," said Bram, to the glass. "There's two loaves proving in the galley. They'll be ready at the eighth bell."

"Two?"

"The first one was Biscuit's," said Bram. "It didn't ask."

From inside LUGGAGE, Biscuit's throat glowed amber, unrepentant.

"The noodle stall on the spinward concourse," I said, and sat down at the other end of the bench, and unscrewed the flask. "The Lisk one. The broth."

"Item fourteen," said Hask at once. "The broth at the Lisk stall is served at a temperature I have been unable to find enumerated anywhere but in my own Schedule."

"Then it's enumerated."

"Under scalding. I have stood beside you while you drank it on three hundred and twelve occasions."

"And I've lived."

"You have lived," said Hask, "despite."

The tea was very hot. It always is. The steam smelled of tar and smoke and burnt raisins and the inside of a steel flask with four dents in it, and it took the skin off the roof of my mouth, and Hask said "Item fourteen" again, for the record, and nobody moved toward any dinner at all.

That was when the slate chimed.

The Register's double note, the one for a followed record. I knew before I looked. Halloran's face, over the tin, did nothing at all, very carefully.

"Came in this afternoon," he said. "Nobody else wanted it."

I opened it. And Bram, who had been standing at the glass with his back to me the whole time, was suddenly standing at my shoulder, reading it, his chin nearly on my collar, his breath smelling of crust.

"Nobody reads over my shoulder," I said.

"I'm reading over your shoulder."

"I know. It's very annoying."

INSPECTORATE OF DISCREPANCIES, MARGIN STATION. Assigned: Examiner O. Pastern. Consultant: B. Hale (living records). TARROW, the Vessany lanes. Harbour Board reports that the Old Customs House on Tarrow Quay (stone; three storeys; Listed; built 212 years ago) has moved forty-one metres down the quay since spring and now stands partly in the harbour. Register shows the Customs House as a building. Status: stationary.

And under it, in a harbourmaster's furious capitals, a note somebody had seen fit to forward:

IT HAS STARTED TAKING IN THE TIDE. ON TUESDAY IT SNEEZED.

Two true things that could not both be true. In my experience, that is where somebody is hiding something. Or where something is hiding.

"Buildings don't sneeze," said Bram.

"This one's filed as one."

"Is there a picture?"

There was a picture. A long grey building on a stone quay, slate-roofed, three rows of tall windows, with green water slapping round the foot of its seaward wall and a crowd of small dark birds sitting along its ridge as if they'd been told to wait. An ordinary building. A customs house. I've been through a hundred like it.

Bram looked at it for a long time. Then he put one long grey finger on the picture, very gently, the way he'd touch a seed.

"Look twice," he said.

I looked twice. Every window on the seaward side of the building was open. Every window on the landward side was shut.

"Nobody opens all the windows on one side of a building," said Bram.

I scrolled down. The harbourmaster again, a line further on, in calmer capitals, as if he'd had a cup of tea first: NOBODY HAS OPENED ANY WINDOWS.

"Then it's breathing," said Bram.

Behind us, Hask's stack ground, long and low, like a landslide considering a hillside. His top stone had tipped toward the slate.

"Stone hears stone," he said, slowly. "I should like to be introduced."

Halloran cracked a seed. Tock.

"Tomorrow," he said. "The Tarrow packet's at noon. Nine hours."

"Is there a fold?" said Bram.

"There's always a fold."

Bram's face did something complicated. Inside LUGGAGE, Biscuit went abruptly dark.

"Can we read it?" said Bram, to me. "All of it? The whole building?"

"One at a time," I said.

He looked at me very oddly then, and laughed, and I have no idea why, and he wouldn't tell me. I've written it down. It's an open question.

"Tomorrow," Halloran said again. "Tonight is dinner. The Weighbridge."

"Bread," said Bram.

"Noodles," I said.

"Item fourteen," said Hask.

And it started all over again, the way it had on Moriel and in the hop and on the bench every evening for a week, pie and bread and broth and the plate that rings, round and round, and none of us minded, and nobody got up.

Until Hask said, in a different voice:

"Item four thousand one hundred and twelve."

We stopped.

I had never heard him cite it. In eleven years I had heard him cite perhaps nine hundred items, on stairs and in queues and under falling livestock. I had never heard that number, and I knew why. It's the last one.

"Solitude of the bearer," said Hask, "where company is to be had."

Nobody said anything. On the other side of the glass, the drone went on cleaning.

"It is the last item in the Schedule," said Hask. "The notary added it in a different ink, in the year he died. I had always taken it for a flourish. He was alone a great deal, at the end." His top stone turned, slowly, the way it does when he is looking at you, though he has no eyes. "I have carried it for three hundred and eleven years. I have never been able to act under it. No bearer of mine has ever given me grounds." A pause like weather. "Until lately."

The tea had stopped being hot in my hands. I don't know when.

"It does not specify a place," said Hask. "Only that the company be had. I find I am content with any of them. I shall stand on the plate."

Halloran looked at the tin on his knee for a long moment. Then he put a seed in his mouth and didn't crack it.

"Well," he said. "Well."

Bram had gone back to the glass.

He stood with his toes against the bottom of the frame, in his da's coat, with the cuffs turned back twice, and the seedling at his elbow, and the brown cord on his wrist, and an otter's tail hanging out of a tin on a bench behind him, and he looked out at all of it: the black that wasn't black, and the stars like nail heads, and the ships going in and out of the ring like bees. And then, in a voice I knew from a skywalk, warm and pleasant and not dead at all, he began to narrate.

"Here we see the Baseline Human," said Bram, to the glass, "at the window."

Halloran snorted.

"Note," said Bram, "the single set of lungs."

"And the gills," said Halloran, not looking up.

"Note the gills." Bram turned his head a little, so that the slits under his ears caught the day-strip, and fluttered them, all six, on purpose. "Note the traditional wooden implements." He held up the pencil he stirs the crock with.

"Note the flower," said Hask. "Colour not enumerated."

"Note the otter," I said. "It's luggage."

From inside LUGGAGE, Biscuit sneezed.

"Note," said Bram, and stopped, and I saw him look at his own reflection in the glass, laid over the stars, and over our three reflections behind it, on the bench, a big grey man and a cairn with a flower in it and a woman with a flask. He looked at us in the glass for a long time.

"Here we see the Baseline Human," he said again, quieter, "at — at —"

He looked back over his shoulder at us.

"Dinner," said Hask, who would not have a thing go into the record unfinished.

"Home," said Bram, and turned back to the stars.

Then he leaned in and breathed on the glass. A big, foggy breath, all the way from the bottom of the one good set of lungs he was born with, onto the inside of a panel with nothing on the other side of it. The fog bloomed on the cold pane, silver against the dark. And in it, with one long grey finger, he drew, the way they draw at the Window on Tuesdays. A sun. A tomato. A face.

And a door, standing open.

Outside, the drone came round the ring on its suction feet and reached his panel, and stopped. Its little lamp came up and shone on the fog and the drawing and the boy behind them, for a long moment, the way you look at something you have never seen before and have no category for.

Bram waved.

Behind him, we went on arguing about dinner.
