What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 3 of 24

Chapter Two: A Discrepancy at Breakfast

← Chapter One: The Twelfth (Do Not Tap the Glass)ContentsChapter Three: Nothing Floats for Free →

A young clerk asked the old cairn, "What is the weight of an empty chair?" The cairn said, "Ask the Register." The clerk asked the Register. The Register said, "Present." The clerk wept. The cairn, for the first time in four hundred years, laughed.

— Stone Sayings of the Weathering School, no. 112

The Register said the boy was safe.

The Register is the memory of the Tally: every birth, debt, deed, oath and species, entered once and never struck. It is very seldom wrong, and it is never uncertain. Where it disagrees with the world, the Inspectorate of Discrepancies sends someone to find out which of them is lying. For the past eleven years, that someone has usually been me.

My file read: Moriel Heritage Preserve. Field sensor 40-K reports one (1) baseline human genome, unregistered, moving. Register shows every baseline human on Moriel accounted for: 211 resident in the Heritage Village; one (1), Reference 12, transferred to the Upper House per procedure. Two true things that could not both be true. In my experience, that is where people are hiding something.

I read the file four times on the hop out from Margin Station: for the facts, for the gaps, for the tone (the flat, slightly embarrassed tone the Register uses when it has been contradicted), and once more because the hop is nine hours and there was nothing else to do. You spend most of it in a padded seat that smells of other people's journeys, and the last half hour dreading the moment when the ship folds space and your stomach decides it would rather stay behind.

"I have read the file," said Hask, from the cargo-rated seat behind me, in a voice like a landslide being polite. "It is a strange file."

"They're all strange files. That's why they send them to us."

"This one is stranger," said Hask, and then said nothing else for two hours, which for Hask is a sort of chattiness.

Hask is my Proxy. Picture a cairn of dark stones, nearly as tall as a door, each stone carved with tiny script, moving with the patience of a glacier that has somewhere to be. The Orrun built minds like Hask to keep oaths, and the Tally hired them to keep people alive. His mandate is cut into a paler stone in the middle of the stack. It was drafted by a notary dead these three hundred years, and it requires Hask to protect the bearer of this seal from harm of any kind enumerated in the Third Schedule.

I am the bearer of the seal. It hangs on a cord around my neck and weighs about as much as a guilty conscience.

The Third Schedule lists four thousand one hundred and twelve harms. It includes drafts, insult to lineage, scalding by beverage, and falling livestock. It does not include being shot. Being shot at is listed. Hask once explained the difference to me, at length, at a moment when it was relevant.

Somewhere around hour seven I unscrewed my flask. It is steel, dented in three places and scorched black along one side. The tea in it was black and stewed and strong enough to tan a boot, and the steam off the lip smelled of tar and woodsmoke and burnt raisins and made my eyes water. The first mouthful took the skin off the roof of my mouth, as it always does. Tea that doesn't hurt a little is just warm water with ambitions.

Behind me, a stone shifted.

"Item fourteen," said Hask.

"I know."

"Scalding by beverage. I mention it so that the record will show it was mentioned."

"The record is very full of it, Hask."

"The record," said Hask, "is where such things belong."

The ship folded. My stomach stayed behind for a while and then caught up, sulking. The tea, being sensible, stayed in the flask.

◆ ◆ ◆

Moriel spaceport smelled of rain and something sweet, like fruit just before it turns.

The arrivals hall was a long glass vault with a garden down the middle, and on Moriel the garden was a riot. Ferns with leaves like stained glass. A vine that had climbed the far wall and was, slowly but unmistakably, reaching for the departures board. A row of trees whose bark was covered in soft grey fur that rippled when the air vents came on, and gave off, as it rippled, a faint warm smell like a dog that has been sleeping in the sun. A small sign said PLEASE DO NOT PET THE TREES. Somebody had petted the trees. You could tell. They looked smug.

The customs queue snaked between the planters. Ahead of us, a family of Kasper-Tul were holding a quiet argument entirely in scent, citrus and then burnt sugar, and their youngest kept sneezing. Behind us, a Lumen-choir courier hung in its suspension tank like a slow pink sunrise, humming in a register I felt in my back teeth rather than heard. Above the queue, a news crawl slid past in six scripts: CHANCERY DENIES FORM SHORTAGE · TRANSIT TOLLS RISE AGAIN ON THE VESSANY LANES · "CORRIDOR OF HEARTS" RENEWED FOR 341st SEASON · MORIEL BIOREFERENCE INDEX STEADY.

I noticed that last one, because I notice everything, and then I forgot about it, because the queue moved.

Moriel took its own blueprint in hand eight generations ago and started editing, and it shows. The customs officer at the next desk had a delicate fan of gills along each side of her neck, which fluttered when she talked. The man beside her had a second pair of eyes set just above the first, smaller and darker, which made him look not frightening, only attentive. A child in the residents' queue had skin that shifted colour with her mood. At that moment she was a bored, mottled beige, which I sympathised with.

The officer who got us had none of these things, or none I could see, until she looked down at her screen and a thin membrane slid sideways across her eyes, like a blink that had been thought about.

"Purpose of visit?"

"Inspectorate business." I put my warrant on the counter. "Discrepancy audit, the Heritage Preserve."

She read it, membrane sliding back and forth. "And your companion?"

"Proxy. Registered to the warrant."

She looked up at Hask. Hask looked down at her. It is impossible to tell where an Orrun cairn is looking, because it does not have eyes, but you always know when it is looking at you.

"Any food, plants, seeds, soil, live organisms, or genetic material?" she asked him, in the voice of someone who asks this four hundred times a day.

Hask considered the question for long enough that the Kasper-Tul family behind us began to smell faintly of impatience.

"Yes," said Hask.

The officer's gills went flat. "Please declare them."

"Lichen," said Hask. "On my fourth stone. Since the Eleventh Chancery. Also a colony of mites, which I believe are content. Also, in the joint between my seventh and eighth stones, a seed. I do not know what kind of seed. It arrived on a wind some ninety years ago. I have not wished to disturb it."

There was a pause.

"Sir," said the officer, "I'm going to need you to remove the seed."

"I cannot," said Hask. "My mandate requires me to remain within arm's reach of the bearer of the seal at all times while on assignment. To remove the seed, I must be disassembled. If I am disassembled, I will not be within arm's reach of anything, as I will not, strictly, have arms."

"You don't have arms now."

"That," said Hask, with great dignity, "is a matter of interpretation."

The officer looked at me. I looked at the ceiling. In these situations it is better to let the process happen to you.

In the end she filled in a long form, with a box for Seed, provenance unknown, lodged in Proxy (see attached), and an attached form, which was longer. Hask watched her do it with what I can only describe as enormous respect. When she stamped it, he made a small grinding sound deep in his stack, which in an Orrun is roughly equivalent to applause.

"Welcome to Moriel," she said, sounding tired. "Please don't pet the trees."

◆ ◆ ◆

We took a public tram out of the city, because I like to see a place before I am told what to think of it.

The tram was hot and smelled of warm dust and somebody's lunch. Two seats ahead, a man with feathered eyebrows was eating a pastry out of a paper bag, onion and vinegar, and the smell of it put me, for a moment, somewhere else entirely.

◆ ◆ ◆

A grain depot at the end of a branch lane, on a wet little world I have never been back to. My first year. Rain on a tin roof like gravel poured into a bucket.

The ledger said a clerk named Prew had sat at the receiving desk every working day for nineteen years, signing for grain, and the grain had been going missing. I had spent three nights in a rented room with the ledgers and built a theory: thirty-eight pages, cross-referenced, in which Prew and the depot master had been skimming one sack in thirty. It was beautiful. I had used coloured inks.

I walked into the receiving office with my pages under my arm and the depot master behind me, white and sweating, which I took for guilt. The office smelled of mouse and wet sacking. Old Fallow was already there, leaning in the doorway, eating an onion pasty out of a paper, and he said nothing at all, which I took for approval.

I sat down in the clerk's chair to read my theory aloud.

The chair had three legs. The fourth had snapped long ago and been stood back up in its place, not mended, just propped, the way you lean a broom against a wall. The chair held me for about as long as it took to say "In the matter of." Then it went one way and I went the other, and thirty-eight pages went everywhere, and I lay on the floor of the receiving office looking up at a damp stain on the ceiling shaped like a duck.

Fallow finished his mouthful. He crouched, slowly, because his knees were bad, and ran one finger across the seat of the fallen chair beside my head, and held the finger up to the window. It was grey to the second knuckle. Dust like felt.

"Nobody's sat at this desk in years," he said. "Have they."

The depot master sat down on a sack and cried. Prew had died nine years before, and the master had signed in as him every morning since, because a clerk's wage bought his daughter's medicine and the Register had never asked to see a body. No grain was missing at all; that was a decimal point in somebody else's ledger, two hundred kilometres away. Thirty-eight pages about the wrong crime.

Fallow gathered up my pages, squared them against his knee, folded them twice, and wedged them under the short leg of the desk, which had been wobbling the whole time. He leaned on it. It didn't wobble.

"Chair first, Pastern," he said. "Then the paper." And he went back to his pasty.

My hip was purple for a fortnight. I still can't see a water stain on a ceiling without checking what I'm sitting on.

◆ ◆ ◆

The tram lurched. I looked out of the window.

Moriel's farmland ran in strips, every one a different colour. Fields of a grain so tall and gold it seemed to give off its own light. Orchards of short, wide trees hung with fruit in clusters like bunches of glass bells. At a market stop, a woman was selling tomatoes the size of a man's head, blue as deep water, from a cart, and as the tram doors opened I heard them. They hummed: a low, contented, three-note hum, the ripe ones slightly sharp. The air that came in with the hum smelled green and peppery, like a vine stem snapped in the hand. A boy in front of me bought one and carried it off in both arms like a baby, and it hummed all the way down the street.

"They sing when ripe," Hask said. "A marketing decision, I am told. Some generations ago."

"Do you think they mind?"

"I think," said Hask, "that no one has asked them."

I wrote that down. I don't know why. It felt like the sort of sentence that would matter later.

◆ ◆ ◆

The director of the Preserve met us at the dome with a smile that had been engineered, I suspect, at some expense.

She was tall, silver-haired, in the deep green of the Heritage Service, and the smile went all the way up to her eyes and stopped there, like a very good receptionist. She smelled of something floral and expensive. Behind her the dome rose out of the hills, a great soap bubble of glass so big its far side was hazy, and under it, like a pebble in a jewel box, a village: brown thatch, a bell tower, smoke going straight up from forty-one chimneys, small square fields of ordinary wheat and long rows of something leafy studded with red. A cart drawn by an animal I eventually identified, from very old pictures, as a horse.

"Examiner Pastern," the director said. "What an honour. We so rarely have visitors from the Inspectorate."

"That's usually a good sign," I said. "Until it isn't."

The smile did not falter. "I'm sure this is a simple sensor fault. Pollen, mostly. Every one of our residents is accounted for. Reference Twelve was called up to the Upper House on schedule, nine days ago. It's a great honour, in the village. There was a feast." She said feast the way you'd point out an exhibit. "Do come and see."

You don't walk into the Heritage Village. You walk over it, along glass skywalks high under the curve of the dome. From below, the director said, they catch the light in a glittering band the villagers call the Shining, and the visitors behind it are the Shining folk. The villagers know they are watched. They are not told what lies past the far door at the end of the skywalks. By day the glass shows weather; at night, a soft dark blue, and nothing more.

"Market day is Thursday," she said. "Our most popular slot."

From a speaker in the skywalk rail, a warm, dead woman's voice said: "Here we see the Baseline Humans at their daily tasks. Note the absence of any modification. Note the single set of lungs."

I looked down. I wasn't there to be told; I was there to look.

What I saw, God help me, was not pitiable. It was a village, and it worked. A woman beating a rug stopped to laugh with a neighbour over the fence. A small boy lost a hoop race and was furious until an older girl swung him round. A man mended a roof while another held the ladder and criticised. Even through the glass there was noise to it, dogs and a handbell and somebody singing, and a faint thread of woodsmoke from some vent the designers had forgotten. They were a family two hundred and eleven people wide, built out of what they'd been given, which was a dome and each other.

They knew they were watched. They just didn't know how big the room was.

"I'd like to see Reference Twelve's home," I said.

The director's smile flickered, just once, like a lamp on a bad circuit. "His family's home. Of course. Though he isn't there, naturally. He's in the Upper House."

"Naturally," I said. "I'd still like to see it."

◆ ◆ ◆

The staff door into the village is hidden in the side of a hay barn. That should have told me something, and it did, but I let it sit. The barn smelled of dry grass and horse, warm and sweetish, nicer than the books let on.

The house was low and whitewashed, with a blue door and a crooked apple tree propped up with a forked stick. We went in through the back, because the director said the front was "in frame" for the skywalk at this hour.

The kitchen was small and warm, with a fire going under an iron pot and a smell of oats and woodsmoke and, under them, honey. A table, scrubbed pale. Four chairs. Three of them were occupied.

A woman of perhaps forty sat at the head of the table, very straight, her hands folded around a cup she wasn't drinking from, dark hair going grey at the temples. She looked at me the way you'd look at weather. Beside her, a girl of about eight was eating porridge with the grim concentration of a child who has been told to finish it. In a chair by the stove, wrapped in a shawl, an old woman, the grandmother I guessed, was carving something small out of a block of pale wood. She didn't look up. Once, while I stood there, she coughed, a deep wet sound like gravel turning in a pail, and went on carving.

The fourth chair, by the window, was pushed back a little, the way a chair is when someone has just got up from it. In front of it was a bowl with a spoon in it. Clean. Empty.

I put two fingers on the back of the empty chair as I passed. No dust. The bowl was cold, the spoon dry, no ring of porridge in the bottom. Somebody had wiped that chair this morning, and set that bowl, and not filled it.

From somewhere in the ceiling, the warm, dead woman's voice said: "Here we see the Baseline family at breakfast."

I have audited a great many empty places. I had never before been told what was in one.

The mother's eyes moved, very slightly, toward the ceiling, and then back to her cup. Her face did not change. And I understood, all at once, why she was sitting so straight. The window by the empty chair looked up at the glitter of the Shining, and behind the Shining, at this hour, were tourists. You do not weep in front of the Shining folk. Not in this village. She'd been taught that, and her mother before her, for eight generations.

"Mistress Hale," said the director, in a bright, visiting voice. "This is an Examiner from… far away. She's here to see that everything is in order after Bram's calling-up."

"Everything is in order," said Mistress Hale. Her voice was quite steady.

"The narration is in error," said Hask, very quietly, behind me.

"Yes."

"Shall I file?"

"Not yet."

The girl had stopped eating. She was staring at Hask with her mouth open. To her, I realised, he must have looked like something out of the oldest stories in the schoolhouse: a walking cairn, a standing stone come to life. To her credit, she didn't scream. She looked at him for a long, long moment, spoon halfway to her mouth, and then she said, in a small, perfectly practical voice, "Are you from beyond the Shining?"

The director drew a breath. The mother's hand closed on the girl's wrist. And Hask, who has never in three hundred and eleven years understood when a question is dangerous, said, "Yes."

The girl put her spoon down. "Then is Bram there?"

Silence. The fire popped. The grandmother's knife stopped moving.

"That's what I'm here to find out," I said.

I sat down, uninvited, in the chair across from her. Not Bram's chair. I'm not a monster. I took out my slate and then put it away again, because she was eight and you don't take notes on an eight-year-old, you just remember.

"What's your name?"

"Tilly." She was still looking at Hask. "Tilda Hale. He's my brother. He went to the Upper House. Everyone said it was the best thing. They had the feast and the bell and everything. Ma made the honey cake." Her face did something complicated. "He didn't eat any. He said he wasn't hungry."

"Tilda," said her mother.

"He always asked," said Tilly, faster now, as if a door had come open and she had to get through before it closed. "About the Shining. About what was past it. Master Aldous said past the Shining there's just more Moriel, and then the edge, and Bram said how do you know, and he got sent to the woodpile. He went to the woodpile loads. He found a word in an old book, stars, and nobody would tell him what it meant. He said the night was wrong. He said it was just blue, all night, the same blue, like a painted lid." She looked at her mother, then back at me, fierce. "He wasn't bad. He just wanted to know."

Mistress Hale had closed her eyes. When she opened them she looked straight at me, and for one moment, below the level of the window, below the eyes of the sky, her face was entirely naked.

"He was always the one at the edge of the field," she said, very low, "looking at where it stopped. Since he could walk. His father was the same, before—" She stopped. "We love him. You understand? This is his home. We are his people. He loved us too. He loved this." The smallest movement of her hand: the kitchen, the fire, the old woman, the girl, the blue door. "But it was never going to be enough for him. I knew that. I think I always knew."

"Did he tell you he was going?"

"He didn't have to," she said. "He was called up. Nobody comes back from the Upper House. It's an honour." She said honour the way you'd say the name of an illness. "But the night before, he hugged me so hard I couldn't breathe. And he said, Ma, what if there's more? And I said, More what? And he said, Just more." Her mouth trembled and was still. "And I let him go to bed."

In her chair by the stove, the grandmother turned the thing she was carving over in her hands, and I saw that it was a little wooden bird, its wings half out of the wood, as if it were still deciding.

"Is there?" said Mistress Hale. "More?"

The ceiling said: "Note the traditional wooden implements."

I thought of the furred trees and the Kasper-Tul arguing in smells, the Lumen courier humming like a sunrise. I thought of the forty thousand worlds of the Tally and the things past it that even the Register doesn't have a word for.

"Yes," I said. "There's more."

I heard the director inhale, sharply, behind me. She'd have my warrant for that, if she could. She couldn't. It wasn't enumerated.

Mistress Hale nodded once, slowly, like someone who has been carrying a heavy thing for a long way and has finally been told how far there is left to go. Then she straightened her back and turned her face to the window and was, once again, a Baseline woman at breakfast.

◆ ◆ ◆

Outside the dome the Preserve was an ordinary museum, cool and dry and smelling of floor polish. A sign cut into stone: WE KEEP WHAT WE WERE SO WE KNOW WHAT WE ARE. Somebody, once, had meant every word.

Halfway down the main hall there was a wall of plaques.

Eleven of them, bronze, each with a name and a pair of dates and a little relief portrait. A boy with heavy eyebrows. A girl with a gap in her teeth. Another girl, laughing at something outside the frame. All of them young. Under each name: CALLED UP TO THE UPPER HOUSE, and a year. The years were spread out, one every decade or so, back across the better part of a century. REFERENCE 1. REFERENCE 2. All the way to 11.

After the eleventh, there was a space of bare wall the size of a plaque, and in it, four small holes, already drilled. I put my fingertip to one. The edges were clean and sharp, and there was still a little fine stone dust in the bottom. Drilled recently, then. Drilled before anyone had needed them.

"The Wall of the Eleven," said the director, as if she were introducing a fountain. "The village's highest honour. Each generation, one young person of exceptional health is chosen as the Reference, and called up to serve."

"Serve how?"

"As the Reference," said the director, as if that explained it.

I took out my slate and called up the Register. You always can, anywhere in the Tally: it's the one thing that never goes down. I entered Reference 1. The Register answered, as it always does, instantly and without doubt. HOMO SAPIENS (BASELINE). REFERENCE No. 1. TRANSFERRED: UPPER HOUSE. STATUS: RETIRED. I entered 2. RETIRED. 3. RETIRED. All eleven. Retired, retired, retired.

I looked for the next line, the one that tells you where a retired thing goes: a pension, a grave, an incinerator, anything. The Register always has a next line. It is the whole point of the Register.

There was no next line.

"Where did they go?" I said. "After they retired."

"Into the care of the Service."

"And where is that?"

"In the care of the Service."

"That's not a place."

"In the Register," said the director, gently, as if explaining something to a child, "it is."

I wrote it down. I write everything down. It is the one thing about me the Register and I agree on.

On the way back toward the lobby, past the gift shop, which sold little rag-doll Baseline families with embroidered single lungs and a mug that said I SAW MARKET DAY AT MORIEL, I passed a door marked AUTHORISED. It had a little window in it, at about head height, and the light behind the window was amber.

I am, technically, authorised everywhere, and the director had stopped to take a call and turned her back.

The glass was cold against my forehead. Through it I saw a tank. It was taller than me, filled with amber fluid, lit from below, and in the fluid, curled on itself like a question, was something that had a long way to go before it was a boy. Its fingers were still webbed. Its eyes were closed. A thin cable ran from its middle to a socket in the tank's base. A label on the glass, handwritten on a strip of tape, read: 13. Even through the door I could hear the pump, a slow wet sigh every few seconds, like something breathing for it.

Next to it was a second tank, empty, and a third. The room went back further than the light reached.

I stood there and did the arithmetic, because that's what I'm for. A village of two hundred and eleven people, kept at great expense under a glass sky, so that once a decade it could send one healthy young person up to a House nobody came back from. And here, in a room behind the gift shop, a way to make Reference Thirteen without the village at all. Without the mothers, the honey cake, the bell. Without anyone at the edge of the field asking what was past it.

If that tank worked, the Heritage Village was just an expensive way of doing something you could do in a jar.

A hand closed on my shoulder.

It belonged to a large young man in Service green with peppermint on his breath, and he was saying, "Ma'am, you can't be—" in the voice of somebody who has been told to say exactly that to anyone who stops at this door, and has never once had to.

Six years on Survey crews, on worlds where the things that put a hand on you generally meant to eat it, leaves habits that a decade at a desk does not quite file away. I didn't decide anything. I turned in under the hand, took the wrist along with me, set my hip where his balance had been a moment before, and let the floor do the rest.

"Inspectorate warrant, general access, every door on these premises, it's on the back of the card," I said, on the way down with him, "and I'll let you read it, but please don't do that again, it's very hard on the knees."

"—in here," he finished, from the floor.

He slid a little way along the polished green stone on his back. His cap rolled in a slow, embarrassed circle and came to rest against Hask.

Hask regarded the cap. Then he regarded the guard. Then, with the deliberation of continental drift, he regarded me.

"I was preparing," he said, "to object under item eighty-eight."

"Thank you, Hask."

"Unwelcome hands. I had located the relevant subsection. I was about to begin."

"It was very nearly in time."

I held out my hand. He looked at it as if it might be a second trick, which was fair, then took it, and I pulled him up. He was perhaps twenty, freckled, far more mortified than hurt, which was how I'd meant it. I gave him his cap. He put it on backwards and then, with enormous dignity, turned it round.

"The director said nobody looks in there," he said.

"The director," I said, "is on a call."

She had turned at the noise, too late to see anything but a guard straightening his tunic and an Examiner brushing off her sleeve. "Is everything all right?"

"Your floors are beautifully kept," I said. "He was just showing me."

We walked on. Behind me, Hask said quietly, "Examiner."

"I know."

"The Third Schedule enumerates distress of the heart, acute."

"I know, Hask."

"I mention it only because I am uncertain whether it applies to you at this moment, and I should not like to fail in my duty."

"It doesn't," I said. "Not officially."

I wrote down 13, and then, under it, and more tanks. My hand was steady. I was pleased about that, in a small, mean way.

◆ ◆ ◆

The lobby of the Preserve has a vaulted ceiling and a floor of green stone, and in the very centre, in a case of old, slightly wavy glass, lies the Founders' Charter.

A great sheet of real paper, gone brown at the edges, dense with careful script and signed by forty or fifty people whom only the Register remembers. I always read the founding documents. You learn more about a place from what it promised than from what it does.

I read the preamble. That we shall go forward, and change, and become what we choose; and that we shall keep, in all honour, a living memory of what we were, a people and not a specimen, so that we never mistake what we have made for what we are. I read the provision for the villagers' education "in all subjects, including, at the proper season, the truth of their own situation." I read that one twice.

And then, about two thirds of the way down, I came to a clause I couldn't read at all.

It had been painted over. Not scratched out, not torn: painted. Someone had taken a fine brush and brown paint the exact colour of the aged paper and filled in nine or ten lines so carefully that you would never see it unless you stood with the light behind you. I moved until it was. The painted block had a faint sheen the old paper didn't, like a skin on cooling milk. Only the heading had escaped, because it was in the margin in a different hand: a single word. Choosing. And a numeral at the start of the block: XIV.

"What's Article Fourteen?" I said.

The director had finished her call. She glanced at the case, and for the first time the smile did something I wasn't expecting. It didn't falter. It tightened, just slightly, at the corners, like a knot someone has pulled.

"Conservation work," she said. "The original ink was degrading. We took steps."

"You painted over it."

"We preserved it. Under a protective layer."

"Has the text been entered in the Register?"

"The Charter is a historical document, Examiner. Not a live instrument."

"Note what you like," she said, and walked away across the green stone floor, heels very precise.

I wrote down Charter, Art. XIV, "Choosing," painted over, "conservation." I didn't know what it meant. I wrote it down anyway. Somewhere at the back of my head, Fallow said, with his mouth full, that the facts you don't understand yet are the only ones worth carrying; the ones you already understand, you'll remember.

◆ ◆ ◆

The keeper caught me at the outer gate.

Grey, tired, in a cardigan washed a great many times, holding a battered notebook against her chest like a shield. She waited until the director's car had gone, then came across the gravel quickly, the way people do when they're afraid they'll lose their nerve. She smelled of tea and of the sharp clean stuff doctors put on cuts.

"Examiner. I'm Dr. Ommery. I'm the village keeper. Their doctor. I teach medicine in the schoolhouse, and I'm the one who goes up and down the long stair." A crooked smile. "I've been going in under that dome for twenty-two years."

She held out the notebook.

"Bram's," she said. "He left it in the back of the schoolhouse reading cupboard, where he knew I'd find it. He timed the cleaning drone. For three years. The machine that does the inside of the dome at night. Nobody in the village is supposed to know it exists. He knew. I thought…" She shook her head. "I don't know what I thought. I thought he was learning about time."

Columns of numbers in a careful hand, with the fussy serifs of someone who'd learned to write from very old books. Drone: 4 hr 11 min. Hatch open at base: 90 sec. Then, further down, in a different ink, as if added much later: Shoulders = 41 cm. Hatch = 42? And on the last page, in pencil, very small, a drawing of the dome from the outside, the way he couldn't possibly have seen it, with an arrow pointing out into empty paper, and beside the arrow, one word: more?

"Will you report me?" she said.

"For what, exactly?"

"For loving them," she said. "For teaching their children and eating their honey cake at the feasts and not telling them. For telling myself that was kindness." She looked back at the dome. "For letting him be called up. I knew what the calling-up was. Not all of it. Enough."

Over by the entrance a school group was pouring out of a tram, gills and extra eyes and mood-coloured skin, their teacher already doing the count. In a few minutes they'd be up on the skywalks, looking down at Tilly Hale's schoolhouse yard.

I looked at Dr. Ommery for a while. I thought about a person I have not forgiven, and the particular way they said my name the last time, and how much that has cost me in the years since, and how I have kept the cost carefully entered, every year, like a debt that accrues interest. And I thought about how the Register has a box for every debt there has ever been, and none at all for letting one go.

"There isn't a form for that," I said.

She nodded, as if I'd said something kinder than I had. Her eyes were wet, and she let them be. I liked her for that.

"He's not stupid," she said. "Please don't let anyone tell you he's simple because he's… plain. He's the cleverest child I've ever taught. He just doesn't know anything. Nobody ever let him. I gave him books I shouldn't have. Old adventure stories. Children who went out past the edge of the map and came back changed." She laughed, a small cracked sound. "I gave him the idea."

"Ideas aren't usually where the problem starts," I said. "Usually it's the hatch."

That got something like a smile out of her. Then it went.

"Please find him," she said. "Before they do."

"Who's they?"

But she was already walking back toward the dome, toward the voice in the ceiling, still describing a family at breakfast to a sky full of strangers.

◆ ◆ ◆

Field sensor 40-K stands forty kilometres north of the Preserve, at the edge of what the maps call the Spill.

We went at dusk, because the sensor's last ping had been logged at dusk three days running. The skiff was an open flatbed on a cushion of air that whined like a kettle and blew grain dust round our ankles; its driver was a broad, sunburned woman with chaff in her knuckles who talked the whole way. Hask rode on the back like freight. Every time we hit a rut, he said, "Ah."

Halfway out she pointed with her chin at a farmhouse off to the left. There was a blue tarpaulin roped over a hole in its roof, and a heap of broken slates in the yard.

"Sky-grazer," she said. "Came down through the roof in the night, landed in the Tobins' bathtub. It was fine. The tub wasn't." She shook her head. "Three fell this month. Used to be one a season."

"Why are they falling?"

"Ask the Preserve. They say it's the Spill. Something in it getting into them." She shrugged a big shoulder. "Every farm along the cut's filing roof claims, and the Service is sick of paying out, so now there's talk of burning back the Spill along the farm cut." She glanced at me sideways. "They're gentle as anything, mind. Weigh about as much as a goat, but they float. Gas bladders. Just don't startle them."

I wrote down three this month / one a season, and burn the cut.

The skiff left us at the end of a farm track where the gold grain stopped, abruptly, as if it had been cut with a knife, and beyond the cut began the wild.

Up close the Spill was everything Moriel had ever made and then stopped bothering to keep: the escaped, the discarded, the outbred, the unlicensed, all tangled together over eight generations into a forest that didn't know what kind of forest it was supposed to be. Trees with bark like wet leather. Trees with bark like fur. A stand of something like bamboo that swayed against the wind instead of with it, creaking. The air changed the moment we crossed the cut: thick, wet, sweet and rotten and resinous at once, like a greenhouse left shut all summer. It tasted green on the back of the tongue. Things clicked and dripped and whirred in the undergrowth, and I knew none of the sounds. The ground cover was grass, mostly, a soft, warm-looking grass that I swear leaned toward my boots when I stepped onto it, the way a cat leans into a hand.

"Do not pet the grass," Hask said.

"I wasn't going to pet the grass."

"Your hand was moving."

"I was looking at the grass, Hask."

The sensor was a grey post with a cracked solar cap, stuck into a low rise about two hundred metres in from the farm cut. Beyond it, the land dropped away into a shallow valley with a stream at the bottom, dark and quick, loud over stones, and beyond the stream the Spill rose again, thicker, up toward a line of ridges that were turning purple in the last light. Overhead, the sky was full of birds, or what passed for birds: a great loose flock of something long and pale, drifting on the evening air with their wings barely moving, grazing on clouds of floating seed that had lifted off the grain fields with the heat of the day. The sound of the whole flock grazing came down to us faint and soft, like rain on a canvas roof.

I didn't plug in my slate. I put my hand on the post first.

The housing was warm from the day and solid. The cracks in the cap were old, furred with green. Nothing had chewed the cable. Then I plugged in. The logs were clean: three pings, each at dusk across the last three days, each reporting one (1) baseline human genome, unregistered, moving, and each a little further up the slope than the last. Not a fault. The machine was doing exactly what it had been built to do, faithfully, with no idea that what it was reporting was impossible.

I walked a slow spiral out from the post, eyes down, letting the ground tell me what it had. The warm grass sprang back too fast. But at the bottom of the slope, where the ground turned soft and black near the stream and smelled of iron and leaf-rot, it had kept a record.

Footprints.

Bare human feet, smaller than mine. A long, uneven stride: somebody walking fast on feet that hurt. In two of them there was a darker stain pressed into the mud that I didn't need to touch to know. I crouched and put my hand beside one. The edges were blurred by days of dew, but they were there, a line of them along the stream bank, heading north.

And then, about twenty metres further on, they changed.

Not suddenly. That was what made the back of my neck go cold. Gradually. Print by print, over the space of maybe a dozen steps, the dark stains stopped. The toe-marks grew shallower and blunter, and the whole sole pressed broader and flatter and deeper, as if the foot had got heavier, or tougher, and the mud in the bottom of the prints took on a fine ridged texture, like the tread of a good boot. By the last print, before the bank turned to stones, there was no sign that the thing that had made it could be hurt by walking at all.

"Hask," I said.

"I see it."

"What does a baseline human foot do, over twelve steps, to end up like that?"

Hask considered this for a long time. "It is not in the Third Schedule," he said finally.

Snagged on a thorn at the water's edge was a scrap of fabric, pale grey, very soft. I worked it free. It was a corner of a shirt, the kind of plain knit you'd give a child you didn't want to look at too closely, and stitched into the hem, very small, in green thread, were the words PROPERTY OF THE MORIEL HERITAGE SERVICE.

I folded it carefully into my evidence pouch, because that's what you do, and because I didn't want to look at it anymore.

The paper would have to wait. Because that was when the sky-grazers started to fall.

◆ ◆ ◆

I don't know what startled them. Later, I thought it might have been us: a stone-mind and a woman crouched by the water, in a place where nothing that size had walked in years. Or something in the trees across the stream that we never saw. At the time, all I knew was that a sound went through the flock like wind through a wheatfield, a high, thin piping, and then the whole slow pale drift of them broke.

They scattered upward, most of them, bladders swelling, wings beating at last, a great startled cloud of them going away north toward the ridges. One shape among them hung lower than the rest, and heavier, labouring after the others as if it were carrying something. But the evening air had cooled, and the ones at the bottom of the flock were heavy with seed, and when they panicked and dumped gas to turn, they didn't rise. They dropped.

The first one hit the far bank of the stream like a wet sack of grain. The ground shook. The second hit the water and sent up a sheet of spray. The third was coming straight at me.

I saw it the way you see things in accidents, very clearly and much too slowly: a long pale body, wings folded wrong, a soft blunt face with surprised dark eyes, a body the size of a goat and getting larger, falling out of a violet sky directly toward the spot where I was crouched with a scrap of shirt in my hand. I remember thinking, with total calm, this is a very stupid way to go, and also I never filed my travel claim.

Then the world went dark and loud.

Hask had moved. I have never, before or since, seen an Orrun cairn move like that. One moment he was three metres away, standing by the water with the infinite patience of geology. The next, he was over me: stones grinding apart and re-stacking in a sound like a landslide changing its mind, the whole column of him tipping and spreading into a roof, a lean-to of dark carved rock, and I was underneath it in the warm wet dark with my face in the mud.

The sky-grazer hit him.

I felt it through the ground more than heard it, a deep, flat thud that went into my ribs. A stone shifted above my head. Something warm and soft slid down the outside of the shelter, a long scraping sigh of feathers on rock, and then there was the sound of a large, confused animal thrashing in the shallows.

"Hask?"

"Please remain where you are," said Hask, from all around me. His voice was very calm and very slightly muffled. "There are two more."

There were. One hit the bank behind us, close enough that the shelter rocked and I heard one of his stones crack, a sharp, clean sound like ice. The other went into the stream and floundered there, bladder half-inflated, piping in distress. Then nothing. Then the soft rush of wings far overhead as the rest of the flock found its height and went away.

After a while, the stones above me lifted, one by one, and settled back into their column. The evening came back. The air smelled of crushed grass and stream water and something rich and musky that I guessed was frightened sky-grazer. On the bank beside us, the one that had hit Hask was sitting up, shaking out its crumpled wings, looking about with the dazed indignation of someone who has fallen down a flight of stairs in public. In the water, the other two were slowly paddling to the far side, their bladders refilling with a noise like a deflating cushion played backwards. They were, as far as I could tell, fine.

Hask was not entirely fine. There was a crack across his third stone, running right through a line of the tiny carved script, and a smear of pale down stuck to the crack.

"You're hurt," I said.

"I am adequately whole." He paused. "The script that is cracked is from the codicil on wills. I have never needed it."

I got up. I was soaked to the elbows and covered in mud and I had a scrap of a boy's shirt in a pouch at my belt and I was alive. I put my hand flat on Hask's cold stone side, which is not something you're supposed to do to a Proxy, and he allowed it.

"Thank you," I said.

"It was enumerated," said Hask. And then, after a long moment, in what for an Orrun is almost a whisper: "Third Schedule, item two thousand and six. Harm by falling livestock. I have carried that item for three hundred and eleven years. I confess, Examiner, I had begun to think it was a joke."

I started laughing. I couldn't help it. I sat down in the mud and laughed until my ribs hurt, and the sky-grazer on the bank turned its soft surprised face toward me and blinked, and Hask stood over both of us like a monument to a very specific kind of foresight.

◆ ◆ ◆

That evening I stood at the edge of the Spill and looked out at the wild.

The skiff was coming back for us; I could see its lamp bobbing along the farm track, far off. The grass at my feet was moving against the wind. The Spill was getting louder as the light went, not quieter: a wet ticking from the trees, something in the stream like small bells rung underwater, the creak of the bamboo that swayed the wrong way. Down by the stream, where the footprints went from a boy's to something else, the mud was turning silver in the starlight. Somewhere up on the ridge, a flower the size of a dinner plate was slowly turning its face toward me.

I unscrewed the flask. The tea had stewed all day into something nearly solid, and it was still hot enough to steam in the cooling air, and it tasted of smoke and tar and the inside of the dented steel, and it was the best thing I had ever put in my mouth.

"Item fourteen," said Hask.

"I've been nearly flattened by a goat, Hask."

He considered this. "Item two thousand and six does not cancel item fourteen."

"Noted," I said, and drank anyway, and burned my tongue, and was glad of it.

I took out my slate and opened a new line in my report, and I wrote down what I knew. Nine days gone. A bowl set out fresh every morning. Eleven plaques and no next line. A tank with a number on it. A charter with a Choosing painted out. A mother who wasn't allowed to cry in front of the sky. Footprints that stopped being the same person halfway along a riverbank.

Then, on a line of its own, because I didn't know what it meant: One grazer in the departing flock, flying low and heavy, as if carrying something. I left it there.

Somewhere out there, a boy with one set of lungs, who had spent his whole life asking what was past the sky, was finding out, and learning how much of it wanted to eat him.

And the Register, serenely, had him down as safely transferred.
