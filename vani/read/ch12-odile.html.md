The lights came in on the last of the flood, two by two, and I counted them the way Survey taught me: first the number, then the spacing, then what the spacing meant.

Six. White running lights, low on the water, a blue at each bow like a nose, a boat's length apart in a line that bent only where the channel bent. Nobody keeps a line that neat through a drowned forest at night without a chart, and the Register said there was nothing here to chart.

Six tenders. Each, if the plate I'd read that morning was honest, rated for two Associates or four hundred kilograms of recovered materials. Twelve, then. Or about two and a half tonnes of us.

Beside me in the crown of the tall tree, a man who had not known his own name at sunset said, "Marrow's," to nobody, and Lenna Brook, on the branch below, made no sound at all.

The tide had been nearly in at sunset. High water within the hour, then, and after it a little over six hours of ebb, the sea draining out of the orchard down a hundred channels and the mud coming up like a back out of a bath.

"Lenna. When does it turn?"

"Ask Teasel. He'll tell you to the minute and be rude about it." She was already going down the rope ladder at a speed no woman of seventy-seven has any business owning. Over her shoulder: "Sedge. Fire."

Sedge dropped off the branch before the word was finished, bough to bough through the leaves, and a whistle went out from her as she fell, long, short, long, short. It came back from three directions and ran on into the village like a crack across ice.

Bram was trying to stand. He'd been inside a man for an hour and come out shaking, and his legs had been filed under string. All six slits on his neck were shut.

"Stay," I said.

"They're here for me."

"They're here for anything they can lift." I turned his face to the water, not gently, so he could see what I'd seen: twelve small points of colour catching the running lights, one on each grey lapel. "Those pins are numbers. That's not a search party. That's an inventory."

* * *

Highwater put itself out.

It did it in whistles. The iron basket on the top platform, the beacon for the fishing punts, went out with a hiss I heard two trees away. The glow-fungus lamps went into sacks one after another, pinch, pinch, pinch, like someone walking down a row of candles. Babies' nets went up into the high branches on pulleys that creaked once and stopped. Three hundred people in the trees above the sea, and not one of them moving.

When my boots hit the low platform it was the darkest place I had ever stood that was full of people. The flood was at its height, lapping the boards. It smelled of doused smoke and fish and the aniseed in the barrel by the rail.

And the sea was alight.

Everywhere the water moved, it burned green. The tide pushing round the roots drew bright collars on them. A fish turned and left a comet. Riding in on the flood like a scatter of pale seed came the stitchers' young, the snow, each speck lit from inside, pooling in the slack corners in drifts of soft cold light.

And down the main channel came six wakes of green fire, as plain as six lines ruled on a page.

Teasel was at the rail, knife in hand, a coil of rope over his shoulder.

"When does it turn?" I said.

"When the water touches the heron's foot on that root," he said, pointing with the knife at a carved bird I could not see. "Then it stands. Then it goes. You'll have the time between a pot boiling and a pot boiling dry."

"Twenty minutes."

"You'd count a sneeze," he said, with contempt, and I took it as agreement.

"When it goes, which channels go first?"

He showed me with the knife. The wide ones, the pretty ones, where the roots went down bare. I'd learned that by daylight; he knew it in the dark by smell.

"Then for twenty minutes we keep them busy," I said, "and after that we make them hurry. Into those. Anything that moves fast out there lights up. Anything that lights up gets bitten." I looked at the green wakes. "And they don't know that yet."

Teasel put two fingers in his mouth and whistled something long and complicated, and somewhere above us a child giggled and was shushed.

* * *

A voice came across the water, amplified, pleasant, a baritone that smelled of cedar even at forty metres.

"Good evening. This is a recovery operation of Varro-Tandem Bioreference, under claim, coastal margin north-west, materials various. The coast is listed uninhabited. Any materials recovered tonight will be logged, handled with care, and held pending disposal. Please remain where you are." A pause. "Nothing personal."

A second voice, not amplified, carried perfectly over the flat water. "I was not consulted on the formation."

"Seven, you are on an open channel."

"I am aware. I wish it noted."

The lead tender swung its nose-light up into the trees. The beam crossed leaves, a rope bridge, an empty doorway, and the wicker wall of a house where an old woman with one clouded eye was leaning in the full glare: her face, her cropped hair, her arm along the wall, all gone the brown-grey of the weave.

"Nothing," reported the hailer, to itself.

Lenna stepped off the wall when the light had gone, her colour seeping back, and winked her good eye at me across the platform.

* * *

From high above, where Pim Ashby lived behind his blanket and hated noon, came a falling hoot, then another. Mae Cotter, dripping beside me, her scaled forearms dull silver in the sea-glow, translated under her breath.

"Pim's got their eyes. He sees in this like it's afternoon. Two boats at the fig channel. One at the school roots. Three coming here." A pause. "Some are getting out. Up the ladders."

The first came over the end of our platform: tall, thin, silver hair combed flat, a lamp at his belt and a net-launcher on its strap, and on his lapel, catching the glow from the sea below, a small enamel 19.

"Oh. Examiner. Good evening," said Pell-19, finding me with the lamp. "I'm so sorry, you're not on my list."

"I'm not on anybody's."

"We have a form for that." He lifted the launcher. Behind him a 22 came over the ladder top, wand in hand, its brass cap fizzing blue.

I took one step back, which put my heels in the water on the boards, and said conversationally, "Associate. Look down."

Pell-22 looked down. The flood lay ankle-deep across the low platform, as it had every high tide since the reef went soft. His good company shoes were in the sea. So, very nearly, was his wand.

"Salt water," I said. "Ask Seven where it conducts to."

He held the fizzing wand very still at arm's length, like a man who has picked up the wrong end of a snake, and while he was holding it I took it off him. You come at the wrist from below and turn it outward, and the hand opens because hands are built to; a Survey cook who had once been something else taught me that. The wand went over the rail and hit the water with a short angry flash of blue and a smell of hot pennies.

Pell-19 fired. I was already moving, low and sideways, the way you move when a kettle-dog breaks cover, and the net opened like a cobweb over the place I'd been and settled with great efficiency on a stack of crab pots. The launcher chimed.

"Crab pots," I said, from behind him. "Ownership not established."

I put my shoulder into his middle and he went backwards over the rail with a long surprised oh and a splash that went up green as a firework. He came up flailing, which was a mistake. Something long and dark rose out of the roots towards the commotion, quick as shears.

"Float!" I shouted down. "Like a man in a bath. Don't move fast."

He stopped. He lay on his back in the glowing water with his arms out and his silver hair spread round his head like weed, perfectly still, and the eel nosed the light at his heels, lost interest, and slid back down among the roots.

"Thank you, Examiner," said Pell-19 faintly, to the sky.

Pell-22 was on his knees with both hands up. I took his launcher too, and his lamp. "Sit on that ladder and don't get up."

"I'll file something."

"I'm sure you will."

* * *

The tide stood. I felt it rather than saw it: the push of water against the posts went slack, and the weed on the roots hung in the green light like hair in a bath, undecided.

"Now," I said, to nobody in particular, and the trees heard me.

It began with the children. All over Highwater, from platforms and branches and doorways, small long-fingered hands let go of moonpears.

They fell out of the dark canopy into the channels like lamps let fall, dozens of them, and every one that hit the water made a burst of green fire round its own small light, and bobbed, and drifted. In ten heartbeats the orchard was full of bright slow-moving things, and every Pell in every tender swung round to look.

"Movement. Multiple," said the hailer. "Recover."

Launchers coughed. Nets bloomed grey over the water and came down on fruit. Chimes sounded up and down the channels, pleasant and two-noted, like a dinner bell nobody had asked for.

"Fruit," said a voice across the water, in disgust. "Moon. Ownership not established."

"Mine says two."

After the moonpears came the salt-plums, which do not glow and have stones in them the size of a thumb-end, and which arrived from a great height onto grey suits with a noise like hail on a tin roof. Somebody's lamp went into the sea. In the dark between the trees there was a good deal of courteous swearing.

"All tenders," said the hailer, the baritone gone thin at the edges. "Proceed to the central structure. At speed."

Motors rose to their wasp whine. Six green wakes went from lines to plumes, and the sea blazed round them as if they were dragging lanterns.

"There," said Teasel softly, and he didn't mean the boats. He meant the weed on the heron root, which had begun to stream the other way.

* * *

A whistle came down from above, high and frightened, one note held. Mae caught my arm.

"A little one's out. On the school bridge."

The school bridge ran from our tree to the next, three metres above the water, one plank wide with a rope each side. In the middle of it, holding a plum in each fist, stood the small girl with the long fingers who had first asked Hask what the Shining was. Below her a tender had cut its motor and drifted to a stop, and the Pell in its bow was raising a launcher.

I was already running.

The launcher coughed before I'd reached the bridgehead. The net caught the child round the shoulders and swept her onto the planks, and the line went taut and began to reel. She didn't scream. She clung to the plank and the plums rolled away into the dark, and the launcher chimed, and the Pell read its screen aloud in the pleasant voice they all have, as if he were telling her the time.

"Recovered materials. One. Juvenile. Unclassified."

The line drew her, plank by plank, towards the edge.

I was on the bridge. It swayed like a hammock. I had a knife and about four seconds.

From the far end, over my head, a voice came up through the trunk of the tree and into the soles of my feet.

"Sedge. What is in the basket?"

The basket hung from a branch above the channel: a crab-well of woven withy the size of a wine barrel, that Highwater lowered into the sea at night to keep its catch alive. Sedge crouched beside it with her knife at its rope. Hask stood on the mud below with the ebbing water at his fifth stone, his top stone reaching up the trunk like an arm, close enough to take the weight.

"Crabs," said Sedge, very fast. "Drop it on him, Hask."

"Crabs are livestock," said Hask. "They are kept. They are fed."

"Hask—"

"Item two thousand and six," said Hask, "protects the bearer from falling livestock." A pause, stone on stone, in which I reached the child and got my knife under the net. "It is silent on the subject of the Proxy dropping livestock on others. I have looked."

Sedge cut the rope.

Hask's top stone took the crab-well, tilted it out over the channel with the delicacy of a man pouring tea for a duchess, and upended it.

About a hundredweight of live crabs came down out of the dark into the tender. They came down clicking. They came down furious, claws up, the size of saucers, and found, as crabs will, every soft place on two Associates who had dressed that morning for an office. The launcher went one way and the Pell the other. He stood up. He stood up fast, which in that orchard, that night, was the worst thing he could have done; the green fire round the tender blazed as it rocked, and something long and dark rose out of the roots at it, and then another.

The line went slack. I cut the last strands and the child came out of the net into my arms, all elbows and plum juice, and put her face in my neck and said, muffled, "I knew he'd do that."

"So did I," I said. I hadn't.

Below us two Associates stood stock-still in a boat full of crabs, with the eels at the propeller and their arms held up out of reach, and one of them said, with the dignity of the truly wronged, "I wish it noted that I was pinched."

On the mud, Hask's top stone came slowly back down to its place.

"I shall need to check," he said, to nobody, "whether that was a harm."

* * *

Things went quickly after that, and I remember them the way you remember a fight: in pieces, very bright.

A bridge between the fig tree and the school roots, a Pell at each end, me in the middle with the child under one arm. Sedge at the far bridgehead: "Down!" I went flat, and she cut both right-hand ropes, and the bridge rolled over like a dog showing its belly and tipped the Pells into the sea, and the child and I went round with it and hung underneath by hands and knees, upside down, like sloths. Three more children were waiting on the far platform, and the water under them was full of Associates, so I hung there by my knees and caught the rope end Sedge threw me, and tied it.

One movement. Three heartbeats. The knot she'd shown me four times that afternoon, that had come out every time like a wet dog.

It sat on the rope as neat as a bird on a twig.

Sedge looked at it, and at me, upside down with a child clinging to my coat.

"Still a wet dog," she said.

"It's holding."

"Wet dogs hold." The corner of her mouth went up and stayed there, and she ran the three children across one after another. When I came up the right way round my hands were shaking. The knot was not.

A Pell in the fig channel, aiming his launcher up at the place Pim's hoots came from; and down a hanging fig-rope, head first and silent as a shadow crossing a wall, came something long and leaf-mottled that took the launcher off his shoulder by the strap and went back up. The Pell looked at his empty shoulder.

"I've been robbed," he told the next boat, "by a tree."

"File it."

High in the fork, the man who had been Reference Nine for twenty-nine years sniffed the launcher and dropped it, with great care, into the sea.

And Biscuit, which I saw only as a light: a small amber glow, throat-high on the water, swimming purposefully along the lead tender while its occupant hailed the dark. A pause. A sound of enthusiastic chewing. Then the glow went away through the roots, faster, with something flat and pale clamped in its jaws.

"My ledger," said the hailer, unamplified, with a crack down the middle of it. "Return that. That is the original."

* * *

I didn't see Bram go into the water. I saw what he did there.

Sedge pointed. Down past the lead tender, something was moving under the water so slowly that the sea barely lit round it: a faint green line, faint as a pencil mark, unfolding towards the boats. It reached the lead tender's hull and stayed there a long time. Then it moved on to the next.

"He's breathing down there," said Sedge, her hand tight on my sleeve. "He's never been under that long."

Three boats. Four. Then the faint line came back towards our tree, and at the foot of the trunk a head broke the surface, and Bram Hale hung on a root with his gills wide and pale in the glowing water and coughed like a man bringing up a lung.

I went down and pulled him out by the back of his shirt. He was cold as a stone and his teeth were going.

"What did you do?"

"Talked to the boats." He held up his hands. The lines at his left wrist were burning green-gold, and his palms smelled of something sharp and wrong that a month ago I'd have had no word for. Fish. Torn. Frightened. Here. "I put it on the bottoms. All of them. The eels can smell it for a mile. It's like hanging a sign on the butcher's back door."

"And when the ebb grounds them—"

"They'll want to sit very still," said Bram, "for about six hours."

I wrapped my coat round him. He weighed nothing. Somewhere above us Sedge was saying something unrepeatable, in a voice like a stove door, about people who stay under longer than they ever have.

* * *

The ebb did the rest. It had always been going to; that was the point of it.

The tenders came hurrying after moonpears and plum-throwers and hoots, down the wide clear channels where every root went bare into the water, and the water went out from under them as if someone had pulled a plug. You could hear it up and down the orchard: a motor going up to a scream as the propeller neared the surface and spat foam, the long dark shapes coming out of the roots at the scream, a thump, a choke, nothing. Then the squelch. Six times, from six directions, like an old man lowering himself into six chairs.

By the second hour of the ebb, six grey tenders sat on six shining mudbanks with their nose-lights bravely on, ringed by pools of eels that had been told, by a smell on the hull, that something here was hurt and ought to be bitten the moment it moved. Twelve Associates sat in them, very still indeed. Two were still wearing a crab.

"Recovery operation suspended pending tide," said the hailer, flat with fatigue. "All copies remain seated." A pause. "Which of you has the spare ledger?"

A silence across six mudbanks.

"I was not issued a spare," said Pell-7, out of the dark, with enormous satisfaction. "I was not consulted on the formation."

* * *

The lamps came out of their sacks one by one, pinch, pinch, pinch, as if somebody were walking back along the row of candles, and Highwater came down out of the high branches to count itself.

Nobody missing. Nobody taken. A boy with a net graze; Pell-22 with a crab on his ear, asking politely whether anyone had a form for that. The girl with the long fingers was asleep in Hask's shoulder ledge where the crab usually rode, plums in her fists, and Hask stood on the mud beneath her, not moving at all, as carefully as a man holding a full jug.

I sat on the wet boards with my back to a post and read, by the light of my own nails, the log of the launcher the crabs had knocked out of a Pell's hands. Mae had fished it off the mud.

RECOVERED MATERIALS: 1 (one) JUVENILE, unclassified. Entered to company stock 10:52 pm.

As far as the launcher and the Register and every office between here and Margin Station were concerned, a little girl had been stock for about a minute and a half, and the only reason was that nothing anywhere said she was anything else.

My tea had gone cold. That has never happened before or since.

Lenna lowered herself down beside me, both knees creaking, and read the screen over my shoulder.

"That's what we are, to them," she said. "Materials, various."

"Not for long."

"You've got three days."

"Two. As of midnight."

"Then tell me what you need, girl," said Lenna, "before I fall asleep on you."

So I told her, and it was like setting down a pack. If the boy stayed Retired, he had no next line: nobody responsible, no protection, and anyone who found him could log him. If I ruled him a protected specimen of the Preserve, the law would hand him back to the white room by the end of the week, and I would sign it. There was no third drawer. I'd looked.

Lenna listened with her chin on her stick, looking out at the six nose-lights burning patiently on their mudbanks.

"My gran used to say a thing on the stair," she said at last, "when the big ones went off to the meeting house for a Calling. Under her breath, like a bit of prayer you half remember." She shut her eyes. "And at the coming of age, each shall choose: to keep, or to go and see. And the choosing shall be theirs."

I sat very still.

"That's all there was of it by her day. My mam said it was out of the old Charter, before they put it under glass." A dry sound. "Under paint, I expect you'd say."

"Article Fourteen."

"Is that what it was?" She opened her eyes. "Sixty-two years I've been saying go and see to children who never heard of it. Fancy it having a number."

I took out my slate. "I need to sit down for a while. Properly. Can somebody keep the children off me?"

"Hask will," said Lenna. "He's been wanting a job that isn't crabs."

* * *

Fallow taught me the Reading in my first winter, in a back office that smelled of wet wool, with his bad knee up on a chair.

"You can't hold the whole Register in your head, Pastern. Nobody can. So build it, out of something you already know, and walk round inside it, and let it hold itself. Sit down first; people who do it standing fall over." He bit his pasty. "And leave by the door you came in. Or you'll leave by a window, and you won't like it."

I half-closed my eyes, let the sound of the ebb go out of me, and built the door. It is always the same one: oak, iron-bound, smelling of beeswax and cold stone, the door of the Inspectorate's old record office at Margin Station, where I sat my examinations at twenty-seven and could not stop shaking. I put my hand on it. It was cold. I went in.

* * *

The Register is a building, enormous, and every part of it exactly as wide as it needs to be.

Every kind of thing it knows is a hall: a long hall of drawers, oak and brass, floor to vaulted ceiling, going away into lamplit distance. One hall for persons, one for places, one for lines, one for claims. Every drawer in a hall is the same shape, because every entry of a kind has the same parts, the way every house in a terrace has its front door in the same place. A drawer is a row. Its key is on the front, a small brass plate with its true name stamped in, and no two keys in a hall are ever the same.

Some drawers hold the key of a drawer in another hall. Then there is a corridor: a narrow lit passage opening beside the drawer and running straight to the one it names. You can walk it. You can feel in the soles of your feet the Register's certainty that there is something at the other end.

I walked to the hall of References.

It was short. A single bay of twelve drawers off the great hall of the Moriel Heritage Preserve, handles polished like coins, and a cold coming off them the way cold comes off a church wall in summer. Seven slots on each. Number. Name. Born. House. Called. Status. Next line.

I opened Twelve. HALE, BRAM. Status: RETIRED. And beside it, for Next line, a corridor.

I walked it. It went a little way, lit and confident. Then it ended in a wall.

New plaster, pale, with the faint damp smell plaster keeps for weeks. I put my palm on it: cold, a little tacky. Eleven days old. On the other side there should have been a custodian, a guardian, a body responsible. The Register had built a corridor to promise there was, and walled it up, and left the promise standing.

I opened Five. BROOK, LENNA. Status: RETIRED. Corridor to a wall, the plaster old and crazed like the glaze on Fallow's teapot. But under Died there was nothing at all.

Not none. Not a date. An empty drawer: clean wood, smelling of nothing, no label. The Register's way of saying I do not know.

I stood there longer than I should have, because it was the only honest drawer in the bay. In sixty-two years nobody had told the Register that Lenna Brook was dead, and it had never pretended. Up the coast was a drawer in the hall of places that said Population: none: a number, nought, a lie with a value in it. This was the other thing. This was a drawer that admitted it was empty.

Six, Eight, Nine, Ten, Eleven: RETIRED, each a corridor into a wall, and each of them, at that moment, in the trees above my head, asleep or coughing or keeping graves. Absent and Unrecorded in the same drawer at once, which the Manual says cannot happen.

* * *

Twelve had a second corridor. It ran from House across the great hall into the hall of Heritage Residents, two hundred and eleven drawers, every soul in Firstfield. At its end, Bram's other drawer. HALE, BRAM. Son of Wenna Hale. Grandson of Ada Hale. Status: HELD (conservation).

From that drawer more corridors went out, short and warm and close: to his mother's drawer, two along; to his sister's; to his grandmother's. A family is a knot of corridors. You can stand in the middle of one and feel it round you like the walls of a small kitchen.

On the front of every resident's drawer, under the key, was a little brass slide with two words stamped on it and a notch for each. HELD. RETIRED.

I put my thumb on Bram's and pushed. It wasn't stiff. It was simply finished: there was no metal past the second notch for it to move into.

So there it was, plain as a floor plan. Leave him RETIRED: materials for anyone with a net. Push the slide back to HELD: a protected specimen, and the corridor to his drawer ran straight back to the white room under the Upper House. Or take him out of this hall altogether and file him as a free person somewhere else, and to do that I would have to empty this drawer, and every corridor that ran to it, from his mother's and his sister's and his gran's, would end in fresh plaster.

I stood with my thumb on a slide that had two notches, colder than I'd been since the ice world in my fourth Survey year, and did not know what time it was.

Somebody decided how many notches it had, I thought. So I went to the plan-room.

* * *

It is at the centre, where the halls meet: a round room under a dome with no drawers in it at all. On a great table under glass lies the plan, every hall, every slot, every kind of corridor the Register permits, drawn in brown ink on paper gone soft with age. The schema, Fallow called it when he was being Inspectorate about it. It holds nothing. It holds what shape things are allowed to be.

Beside it, chained to a lectern by a brass chain worn bright, is the book of revisions. Every change to the plan since the founding, dated and numbered. It never forgets one. It only stops showing you the old plans, unless you ask.

I turned back the pages, heavy and cold, smelling of dust and iron-gall ink. Fifty-eight. Forty-four, the year they added Maker / line to persons. Thirty-five. Thirty-two.

Thirty-one. About a hundred and forty years ago, in a clerk's tidy upright hand, half a page among a great many half-pages:

Revision 31. Chancery consolidation. Column CHOOSING removed from REFERENCE and from HERITAGE RESIDENT. Superseded by conservation provisions. No data migrated.

A small tick in the margin, the kind you put by a thing on a list of things to do before lunch.

Nobody had signed it with a flourish. Nobody had underlined anything. A clerk on a long afternoon had gone down a list of tables, tidying, come to a column that didn't seem to do anything any more, taken it out, ticked it, and gone on to the next.

Show me thirty, I said to the room, the way Fallow taught me.

The glass clouded and cleared, and the plan beneath was a little different. On every drawer in the hall of Heritage Residents, and on every drawer in the hall of References, there was a third brass plate under the key. CHOOSING. And beside it, in fine brown ink, the default every drawer would hold until someone wrote something else.

Open at coming of age.

I walked the hall of References as it had been, and the twelve drawers were there with their twelve CHOOSING plates, polished, waiting. And empty. Not a key on any of them.

The hall had been built with a Choosing on every drawer before anybody was put in it. Then a clerk took the Choosing off. Then, seventy-odd years later, they began to fill the hall.

I went back into the present, down the great hall, into the bay of twelve, and bent to Bram's drawer with my nails held up for light. Under the key, where a plate had once been, were four small holes drilled in the brass. Neat. Evenly spaced. Filled with a little old dust.

Every drawer in the bay had them.

* * *

I couldn't put the plate back. An Examiner can open a case and walk a hall and move a slide as far as it goes; only a ruling under an open case, before a hearing, can change the plan. I had not made one.

But beside the lectern is a tray of black japanned tin, gone grey at the corners, marked PROPOSED. It is nearly always empty.

I wrote on a paper form, because in the Reading paper is the only thing that weighs what it should. Proposed revision: restore column CHOOSING to REFERENCE and HERITAGE RESIDENT, as at Revision 30. Default: open at coming of age. Grounds: Charter of Firstfield, Article XIV, found under paint; Revision 31, no data migrated; open Discrepancy, Reference 12. Examiner O. Pastern. I put it in the tray. It lay there, white on grey, the only thing in it.

Then I went back through the halls to the oak door, because I wanted very much not to leave by a window, and put my hand on it. It was so cold my palm stuck.

* * *

I came out with a headache behind the eyes like a drawer slammed shut.

It was the grey before day, the sea gone pewter, every channel a wet silver ribbon and every root bare to the knee. I had sat down at midnight. I'd have sworn on the Manual it had been twenty minutes.

I was cold from the bones out, as after a long swim. My legs had set in the shape of sitting. Someone had covered my knees with a blanket that smelled of fish and woodsmoke, and on my slate a line of white text was waiting.

Revision proposal 59-P received. Schema revisions require a ruling under an open case. STATUS: PENDING.

And I was hungrier than I had ever been in my life: a hollow under the ribs, as if something had been taken out and not replaced.

In front of me, filling the platform's edge, was Hask. He had climbed out of the channel in the night onto the low platform, which nobody had been sure would take him, and it had. On his far side, at a respectful distance, sat a row of small children, cross-legged, watching me with the total attention of an audience that has been told it may not climb on the stage.

"Examiner. You have been very still."

"I was working."

"I told them that." A grinding pause. "They asked what work was done by sitting. I said, the most difficult kind. One has been sitting very still since three, to see if it is." The top stone turned slightly towards the row. "It is not, she says. She says it is boring."

The girl with the long fingers got up, came round the end of Hask, and gave me a moonpear without a word.

I ate it in four bites, skin and all. Cold water with honey in it, and a smell like rain.

* * *

Halloran called while I was on my third, bacon frying in my ear.

"Pastern." Tock. "Varro-Tandem has filed twelve complaints against the Inspectorate since two this morning. One per Associate. Three are against each other. One alleges you dropped a crab on him."

"That was the Proxy, sir. It was a hundredweight of crabs, and it was within his mandate. He checked."

A silence, with frying in it.

"I've also got a revision proposal in my tray with your seal on it," said Halloran, "which is a thing I have not seen in thirty-one years in this chair. It wants to change the plan, Pastern. Not a drawer. The plan."

"Yes, sir."

"You know what they'll say upstairs."

"That I can't. Not without a ruling and a hearing."

"That's what they'll say first." Tock. "Then they'll say it's a hundred and forty years old and nobody's complained, and then they'll say I've lost control of my Examiners, and then they'll go to lunch." A pause. "Is it sound?"

Out on the mudbanks the tide was coming back round six grey tenders, inch by inch, and twelve Associates sat very still and waited for it.

"It's sound, sir."

"Then bring it to me. At the Preserve. You've got today and one more, and not an hour of the second I'm not counting." Tock. "And Pastern. Whoever wrote that column out. I'd like to know if they had lunch first."

"There was a tick in the margin, sir."

He made a sound I have heard from him twice in eleven years. In a warmer man it might have been a laugh.

"Of course there was," said Halloran, and the line closed like a log settling in a grate.

* * *

I couldn't give Bram a third drawer. But there was something I could do that morning on my own seal, because the Manual has always allowed it and nobody ever asks: I could enter the Unrecorded.

First a place, because a person needs somewhere to be. HIGHWATER. Settlement, coastal margin, north-west. Status: inhabited. The drawer up the coast that said Population: none got a corridor to it and a correction under an open case, class Unrecorded, and I watched the word none go out like a lamp.

Then the people. They made a line without being told, the way people do at a well, and Lenna was at the front of it, leaning on her stick, her skin gone slowly the colour of the post behind her.

"Name," I said.

"Lenna Brook."

I wrote it slowly, so she could see each letter go on. Place: Highwater. Then I read her the last slot.

"Maker, slash, line."

She looked at me the way she had on my first afternoon, as if I were a document and she had got to the signature.

"Who wrote you," she said. "That's what we ask, here. That's what it means."

"That's what it means."

She thought about it. Out on the mud a tender's motor coughed and caught as the tide lifted it, and went away down the channel very slowly.

"Sal Brook wrote the first of me," she said, "and my lodger's had a go, and the Upper House had a try at the rest and I didn't let them finish." She lifted her chin. "Put I did. Sixty-two years. I'd like the credit."

I wrote I did.

The slate gave its small double chime, and the Register took it without a murmur, because no one had ever told it a person couldn't.

BROOK, LENNA. Place: Highwater. Maker / line: I did. Status: PERSON.

Then I gave her drawer a corridor of its own, out of the hall of persons and across the great hall to the bay of twelve, to a drawer marked 5, with an old wall at the end of its own corridor and nothing at all under Died. Now the corridor ran both ways.

Lenna looked at the slate a long time. Then she patted it once, as you'd pat a dog that had finally stopped biting, and went and sat at the platform's edge with her back to everyone and looked at the sea.

* * *

Teasel came next, because he would not have stood for anyone else coming next. He gave his name as if I might misspell it, and when I read him the last slot he gave me his tired pity and said, "We did," and walked off before I'd finished writing.

Mae, dripping: Jen Cotter, and the sea. Hollis Fenn, down from the graves with her bark hands folded: "Ruth Fenn. Put Ruth." Joss Tanner, carried down in his hammock, coughing: "My mam. And Thursdays." Pim sent his down by whistle from behind his blanket, and Sedge translated, rolling her eyes: "He says look twice."

"That's not a maker."

"He says it is."

I wrote it.

Sedge waited till nearly the end, which I wouldn't have guessed, and sat cross-legged in front of me with her freckles already dark from the sun, and gave her name in the voice of someone who had never had to give it to anyone who didn't already know it.

"Who wrote me." She scowled at it like a sum. "A picker line. A gran who got out of a crate. A mam who taught me knots. Mae taught me diving. Him," with her chin at Bram, asleep against Hask's bottom stone with Biscuit on his chest, "says there's only four letters, apparently." She considered. "Put us."

I put us.

Davin Marrow came down from his tree about the time the sun cleared the sea, by himself, slowly, which I gathered was new, and stood in front of me with his mottled skin going pale gold in the light. He didn't answer when I asked his name. He breathed in at his own wrist. Then he said, "Davin," and after a while, "Marrow's," and I wrote both, and the corridor from his new drawer went across the great hall to a drawer marked 9, and for the first time in twenty-nine years there was a name at both ends.

Three hundred and three. It took until the sun was two hands up. My hand cramped and Sedge rubbed it without asking. Somewhere about the hundred and fortieth, the girl with the long fingers stood in front of me and said her name was Wren, and that she was five, and that Hask had written her. I explained that he hadn't. She explained that he had. Hask, behind me, said nothing at all with great care, and I wrote Hask (she says).

RECOVERED MATERIALS: 1 (one) JUVENILE, unclassified, said the company launcher, still lying on the boards beside me.

WREN. Place: Highwater. Status: PERSON, said the Register.

They could not both be true. Only one of them had a key.

* * *

Bram woke near the end and watched the last of them from Hask's bottom stone, and when the line was done he came and sat down an arm's length and a half away, as he had the first afternoon.

"Can I have one?"

"Not yet," I said. "You've got a house."

I watched him think about the hall I'd walked, though he'd never seen it: a drawer with his mother's corridor running to it, and his gran's, and Tilly's.

"I don't want to not have a house."

"I know. That's the trouble." I showed him the slate. Revision proposal 59-P. STATUS: PENDING. "It's in a tray. I have to take it somewhere and argue."

"Is it a good tray?"

"It's the best tray," I said. "Nobody ever uses it."

* * *

We left on the morning ebb.

Highwater doesn't go in for ceremony; it goes in for whistles. Teasel brought the heron punt round under the low platform and stood at the stern with the pole, scowling at the carved bird on the bow as if it were late. Sedge climbed into the bow with a bundle and her knife and a look that dared anyone to say anything about it. Nobody did. Hask waited in the channel with the water at his sixth stone and the crab back on his shoulder ledge, as if it had never been sacked.

The tenders had gone on the dawn flood. On the mudbank where the lead one had sat all night lay a square of white weighed down with a salt-plum stone: my own Form 9, the dart I'd thrown at a stranded tender the morning before. Mae swam out and fetched it. It had been retrieved, dried, and annotated in a very neat hand. Assistance noted. Not required. The eels were adequately explained. P-7. And beneath, in a second neat hand: Ledger not recovered. Grievance lodged against the otter.

Lenna didn't come down to the punt. She stood at the rail letting the post have her colour, with Davin on a branch above her, and Hollis, and Joss in his hammock, and a blanket over Pim's doorway with a gap the width of one gold eye.

"Tell Ada," she said, and stopped, and decided against whatever it had been. "Tell her I'm still saying it."

"Saying what?" said Bram.

"She'll know."

He was the last onto the ladder. He stopped on the top rung with one plated foot on it and Biscuit's head poking out of his shirt, squinting at the coming sun, and he looked past the platform and the trees, south-east, to where the canopy thinned and the light came through gold. The lines at his wrist were lit, very faintly, the way they'd been the first time I saw him, as if something under there were awake and paying attention.

"It talked to me," he said. "Last night. After the boats, when I was cold." He didn't look at me. He said it the way you set down something you've carried in your pocket too long, because it has started to get warm. "I didn't ask it anything. It just said."

"What did it say?"

He watched the gold through the trees for a long moment.

"There are others like me," he said. "They've been asleep a very long time."

Below us the tide drew at the roots, going out. Somewhere up in the leaves a whistle passed from tree to tree and faded.

He lifted the hand with the light in it, and pointed: south-east, across the Pale, the way we were going anyway.

"And one of them's that way."

Then he went down the ladder into the punt, and I went after him, and Teasel pushed off, and nobody said anything after that for a long time.
