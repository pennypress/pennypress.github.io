What Do You Call a Tomato That Won't Sing? · 18 of 24

Chapter Seventeen: Take a Coat

← Chapter Sixteen: The Better SumContentsChapter Eighteen: Exercised →

The world is Moriel, and Moriel is the world. Say it after me.

[pencilled in the margin, small, in a careful hand with fussy serifs: how do you know]

— Heritage Primer, page one, Firstfield schoolhouse copy (this copy has been to the woodpile more than once)

I hear it before I see it.

We come down out of the cloud forest on the afternoon of the twenty-third day, me and Odile and Sedge and Hask, with Biscuit asleep in my shirt. The moss gives way to the short dry grass of the Preserve's hills, smelling of dust and warm hay. And through my soles, through the plating, up my shins and into the hinge of my jaw, there's a note.

Long. Low. Endless. Like the whole world thinking one thought very hard and never finishing it.

I stop walking.

"Bram?" says Sedge.

"It's the dome," I say. "I can hear it humming."

She looks round at the empty hills. "Where?"

We come over the last rise at sunset, and there it is.

A soap bubble on the hill. That's what I thought, the night I left, looking back with dust in my mouth. It's smaller than I remember and much bigger, both. The glass has gone orange with the sun on it, the skywalks running through it like veins in a leaf. Round the bottom, the Preserve: the long white museum, the tram lines, the gift shop with its lights on. And under the glass, so small I have to use my night eyes to be sure, forty-one roofs of thatch, and smoke going straight up from one of them, because inside the dome there is no wind.

The sun goes down. And across all that grass, through the ground, I feel the ribs start.

Tik. Tik-tik. One, then another, then three together, all over the sky, like a clock somebody built too big to wind.

I sit down in the grass.

Hask settles beside me with a sound like a cart being unloaded, and I feel him listen too, stone hearing glass. Sedge sits on my other side, close, not touching.

"You lived in a jar," says Sedge at last. Not unkind. Amazed.

"It's a very good jar."

"I can see that." She's quiet a moment. "Do they know you're coming?"

"They know," says Odile. "I sent word to the keeper by relay this afternoon. The director has had my warrant since noon. An Examiner's evidence goes home under an open case, and the far door is the way in. She's looking for a regulation that says otherwise."

My hand goes to my chin. It does that now, all the time, without asking me. Long and narrow and pointed, a stranger's chin. My fingers, a knuckle too long, catch on it and peel off, tk.

"They won't know me," I say. My voice comes out low and breathy, the gill-hiss on the edges of it. It's been doing that since the towers, and I still flinch.

Odile looks at me for a while, the way she looks at a drawer.

"When I went to your house," she says, "the first day, there were four chairs at the table. Three of them had people in. The fourth was pushed back a little, and there was a bowl in front of it, and a spoon. Clean. Somebody had wiped that chair that morning, and set that bowl, and not filled it."

I don't say anything.

"I've been told since," says Odile, "that she does it every morning."

The ribs tick. Down in the dome, somebody lights a lamp.

I don't sleep much. Around midnight Sedge says, from inside her blanket, "If you keep touching your face it'll come off," and I stop, and then I start again.

◆ ◆ ◆

The far door is round, and enormous, and brass at the rim. I've watched it my whole life from the other side.

From this side it's got a doormat.

There's a little crowd of Preserve people at it in deep green, smelling of fright and floor polish. The director's there, silver hair, the expensive flower smell, and under it something like milk on the turn. She looks at me once and then at a place just past my ear, and keeps looking there.

"Examiner," she says. "This is highly irregular."

"Yes," says Odile, and hands her a form.

The far door opens with a sigh, like the hatch, and I walk in on the Shining.

It's the strangest thing I've ever done, and I've ridden a sky-grazer off a tower. The tube smells of polish and old breath and, faintly, peppermint. The glass underfoot is scuffed grey by two hundred years of shoes. And there below me, so close I could spit on it, is Old Saul's east rows.

I'm a visitor.

The east walk comes in low over the tomato frames, and from up here I can see everything I ever stood in. Saul's stick-marks in the dirt. Ma's hives at the end of the clover strip. The bakehouse chimney, the mill wheel turning slow. Washing on a line behind the Cotters', a blue shirt, flapping—

No. Not flapping. Hanging. There's no wind.

Then the ceiling clears its throat.

Click, high up in the ribs. A hiss. And the warm voice of the woman who died two hundred years ago and sold the rights says, right above my head, "Here we see the Baseline Humans of Firstfield. Note the single set of lungs."

Under my ears, the gill-slits slam shut, flap, all six.

Sedge snorts. Hask says, gravely, "The narration is in error."

"Yes, Hask."

"Shall I file?"

"Later," says Odile. "There's a queue."

And the walk goes down, the way it always does, to the edge of the market square, where it touches the ground. The Window.

There are children at it.

It's Tuesday; out there I only counted knots. They've cleared the visitors off the east walk for us, but nobody told the children of Firstfield, so there they are, lined up along the glass, ready to breathe. Wat from the mill. Jory's cousin Bet with her front teeth out.

And at the end, on her own, with her nose already flat on the glass, Tilly.

She sees Hask first. Her whole face goes open like a shutter.

Then she sees me.

Her face shuts again. Not scared. Careful. A tall grey-green boy with a narrow face and silver-backed eyes, in an Examiner's coat with the cuffs turned back four times. She looks at me the way she looked at Gerald in the jar, before she named him.

I don't know what to do. So I do what you do at the Window.

I breathe on the glass. A big foggy breath. And in the fog, with one long finger, I write a letter. Backwards, so she'll see it the right way round.

A B. With the bottom loop too big.

The way Master Aldous was always on at me for.

Tilly stares at it.

Then she breathes on her side, a great huffing fog, and puts both her hands flat on the glass, and her mouth moves. I can't hear her. The glass is thick; on this side you only ever got them muffled, like people talking underwater.

So I put my palms flat against hers, through the glass, and listen the other way. Through the bones.

"—won't sing?"

Clear as if she's in my skull.

"What do you call," says Tilly, very slowly and suspiciously, "a tomato that won't sing?"

It's a test. She's eight, not stupid.

"A tomato," I say, in the voice that isn't mine, and her eyes go narrow. So before she can decide anything, I give her the other one. The one I've been carrying since the Hiveplains, since I first saw the real ones, blue and big as your head, humming in the sun.

"What do you call a tomato that hums?"

Tilly's eyes go very wide.

"I don't know," she says. "What?"

"One that's forgotten the words."

Then her face crumples up in the middle, and she laughs so hard she has to hold on to the glass with both hands, and if there was porridge anywhere near her it would be coming out of her nose.

"That's terrible," she shrieks through the glass, through my bones. "That's the worst one ever. Bram. Bram."

Behind me, Odile says something to the man in green with the keys.

And the Window opens.

It was a door. All that time. A hinge at the side nobody ever looked for, and a lock the night guard polishes round, and the whole panel swings out into the square, and Tilly comes through the gap before it's halfway, like a sack of flour off a cart, and hits me in the stomach.

Biscuit squeaks.

"You smell like goat," Tilly says into the coat.

"That's the grazers."

"And bread."

"That's also the grazers."

She leans back and looks up at me, at all of it, the face and the eyes and the gill-slits, which are opening again slowly, like something peeking out from under a bed.

"What are those?"

"Gills."

She pokes one. It slams shut.

"Do it again," says Tilly.

◆ ◆ ◆

Then the wind comes in.

The far door's open behind us, and now the Window's open in front, and for the first time in eight generations there's a hole straight through. The air of the outside comes down the tube from the hills and out through the Window into Firstfield, cold, smelling of dry grass and dust and grain and, from a long way off, the green musk of the Spill.

It lifts Tilly's hair. It lifts mine.

Across the square, the Cotters' blue shirt rises off the line and flaps.

Everyone stops. Wat holds his hand up into it, staring at his own fingers. The bakehouse door bangs on its latch and Mistress Thorne the baker, who has never in her life had a door bang, comes out with flour to her elbows to shout at it, and stops on the step with her apron blowing.

And the square fills up. Not all at once. One at a time, out of doors and lanes, the way people come to a Calling. And all of them smell.

Firstfield smells of feelings the way the bakehouse smells of bread at six in the morning: all at once, in a wall. Fright, sharp. Wonder, like a window opened in winter. Somebody's anger, a stove door. And under it the smells I grew up in and never noticed: woodsmoke, clover honey, horse, tomato leaf. And the crock. Through the banging bakehouse door, sour and yeasty and cool, and on my tongue the little lives in it saying what they always said: more, more, warm, more. Old friends. They've been saying it every morning my whole life, and I only just heard.

"Who fed the crock?"

"I did," says Tilly, offended. "Every morning. With the stool."

The crowd looks at Hask. Hask looks at the crowd.

They press in. Not to me, yet. To the stones, and the girl with fingers like twigs, and the woman with lit fingernails, and the six-legged otter that puts its head out of my shirt, looks at the sun, and goes straight back in. Closer, and closer, until Hask, very quietly, sinks his top stone a hand's breadth.

"Item six hundred and nine," he says. "Pressure of persons."

"They're not a harm, Hask."

"I am aware. There are a great many of them."

Tilly's still got hold of my coat. She looks up at Hask, the way she did across her porridge, the day he came.

"You said you were from beyond the Shining," she says. "And then you went and found him."

"Yes," says Hask. And then, because he's been waiting a very long time to get it right: "May I ask you something?"

Tilly nods.

"What is the Shining?"

Tilly points straight up.

Everyone looks.

The skywalks are full. Not the east walk; the north, and the south, and the high one across the middle. Packed with Shining folk pressed to the glass. Gill-necks and four arms and fur like spilled ink and skin going every colour with feeling. Somebody's got a child on their shoulders.

And down in the square, for once, all of Firstfield is standing still, looking up at them.

"That," says Tilly.

Hask considers the skywalks for a long time.

"Then I have been answering correctly," he says, and something goes on in the ground under all of us, slow and grinding and enormous, that I'm fairly sure is the happiest I've ever heard a stone.

Jory's here. I didn't see him come. He's standing at the front by the well with his arms folded, very red round the eyes, glaring up at the north walk. He points.

"Tapper," he says.

I look. A man in a silver hat is tapping the glass right under the sign that says DO NOT TAP THE GLASS.

"Tapper," I agree.

"You timed the drone," says Jory, not looking at me. "For three years. And you didn't tell me."

"You'd have come."

"I'd have come." His voice cracks. He smells of a stove door, and under it, much bigger, of the first warm day after Thaw. "Look at your face."

"I know."

"It's horrible."

"I know."

He wipes his nose on his sleeve. Then he points up at a tall pale double-looker, all four eyes on me, and does the face at them. The full face, eyebrows and tongue.

Four eyes blink at once.

That's when Ma comes.

◆ ◆ ◆

She comes running from the hives with a smoker still in one hand, trailing smoke that the wind takes sideways, and the crowd opens for her.

She stops a step off.

She looks at me. The face, the eyes, the long hands. My gill-slits go flap. I can smell her: burning sacking from the smoker, wax and clover, and under that, all through her, something like the moment a held breath goes out.

She puts the smoker down on the cobbles. Very carefully.

Then she takes my hands.

Not my face. My hands first. She turns them over, the way she turns a frame of comb to the light. The long fingers. The dull grey pads that catch on her palms, tk, tk. She runs her thumbs over every one, frowning, as if she's counting them and checking the count.

"Ten," she says, under her breath.

"Ten."

"Right," says Ma.

And then she worries. She puts both arms round me, coat and otter and all, and holds on the way you hold a rope, much, much too hard, and the Shining folk are right there above us, a sky full of them, watching.

Ma cries anyway. In front of all of them.

◆ ◆ ◆

Hask can't get through our blue door. He tries, politely, and the lintel creaks, and he says, "I shall attend from the garden," and settles under the kitchen window with his top stone at the sill, looking in like a very large cat.

The kitchen smells of oats and woodsmoke and honey. Three chairs and a fourth, pushed back a little.

There's a bowl at it. A spoon. Clean.

Ma goes to the pot without a word and fills it.

I sit down. My chair. The wobble in the back leg is the right wobble. And the porridge is plain. Oats, water, salt, Ma's dark honey stirred in. Plain as a floor.

And it's loud. The oats talking to my tongue in their small dry voices, sun, dry, stored, and the honey saying clover in a thousand bees' accents. In the Spill I couldn't remember the taste of plain porridge. Now I've got both, in the same spoonful, and neither drowns the other.

I eat three bowls. Nobody stops me. Sedge, on the bench by the door, eats two, and says, with her mouth full, "Is this all it is? Oats?" and Ma says, "Yes," and Sedge says, "It's very good," as if she's surprised at herself.

Odile stands by the door with her flask and doesn't sit, the way she didn't sit in this kitchen the first time, until she did.

"Examiner," says Ma.

"Mistress Hale."

"You said there was more."

"There was," says Odile. And then, after a moment, quieter: "He went and saw it."

On the stairs going up to the loft, in its dark corner, the mother crock is breathing to itself under its cloth. I can taste it from the table. And it's the same.

Not like. The same. In the Library, in the hall of shelves that smelled of stone and mint, there was one shelf that was warm: the first crock, the one the founders brought. This is it. Living under the Hale stairs. Fed by Gran, and her gran, and hers.

Oh, says the voice behind my eyes. Very softly. Somebody kept it.

Gran is in her chair by the stove.

She's thinner. Her chest crackles when she breathes, like paper. On the sill beside Hask's top stone is the little wooden bird she was carving the day Odile came, its wings all the way out of the wood now. She hasn't got up. She's been watching me eat with her eyes half shut, the way she watches the stove.

"Come here," she says.

I kneel in front of her chair, the way I did when I was small enough to be washed at the pump. She reaches out and takes me by the chin. Her fingers smell of woodsmoke and old wool. She turns my face to the light from the window, like a jug she's thinking of buying.

For a long time she just looks.

"That's not a Hale chin," says Gran.

"No."

"Where's it gone?"

"I didn't know what it was called," I say, in the stranger's voice. "So when it slipped, nobody could hold it."

Gran's thumb moves on the narrow pointed thing that isn't mine.

I could put it back, says the voice behind my eyes. If you could say it.

"Say it again," I tell Gran. "Like at the pump."

She doesn't ask why. Gran never asks why. She looks at me, and then at Ma, and then she gives my chin a small shake.

"That's a Hale chin," she says. "That's your da's. That's mine." With her other hand she touches her own, long and square and set like a gatepost, a lifetime of saying no to things in it. "My mother's before me. Stubborn as a gatepost. You'll not lose that."

Again, says the voice. Her face. Let me have her face.

I put my long grey fingers on Gran's chin. On the real one. The bone of it, and the papery skin, and the stubborn square line, and the little dent under the lip that my da must have had, that I had, that I never once in my life looked at because it was just there, like a floor.

Yes, says the voice. There. That's a name.

"And the rest," I say. My voice cracks. "I don't know the rest."

Ma comes and kneels down behind me on the flags. She puts her hands on my head. Both of them, round it, fingers in my hair, the way she does every spring with the fine comb, Tilly and me in turn on the bench in the yard, howling.

"I've had my hands on this head every spring since you had hair," says Ma. "I'd know it in the dark." Her fingers move over the back of my skull, behind my ears, over the gill-slits, which flinch and then don't, along the jaw to where Gran's hand is. "It's wrong there. And there. Your nose is mine, God help you. It's got a bump. Here."

Oh, says the voice. Oh, that's very good. She's got the whole shape. In her hands. Where's she been?

"Here," I say. "She's been here."

"And his voice," says Ma. "What's wrong with his voice?"

"I can't do that one." I swallow. "Nobody ever says what a voice is like. It's just—"

"Tilly," says Tilly.

Everyone looks at her. She's sitting on the table, which she's not allowed to do.

"Tilly," she says again, in a flat, tired, put-upon voice, a bit through the nose, dropping at the end like a dropped spoon. "Tilly. No."

It's me at breakfast, four hundred mornings, being told the joke. She's been doing it for years behind my back to make Jory laugh, and I've always said I don't sound like that.

I sound exactly like that.

That's the voice? says the voice behind my eyes, sounding scandalised. It's very rude.

"It's exact," I say.

All right, it says. All right. Hold still. Hold on to them. Hold on to all three.

I hold on. Gran's chin under my fingers, and Ma's hands round my head, and Tilly on the table being me.

It's like the moment the bakehouse dough gets knocked back. Everything in my face goes soft, and gives, and settles, slow, from the inside, the way bread settles in the tin. I feel my cheekbones go down. I feel my nose move, which is horrible, and the bump come back into it. I feel my chin, last, set. Square. A gatepost. And down in my throat, two little curtains I never knew I had loosen and tighten and find the old place, the way your tongue finds a missing tooth.

And the hunger comes up from my boots like the mill race in spring.

When I open my eyes Gran is still holding my chin. She doesn't let go. She turns it to the light again. Left, and right.

"There," says Gran. "That's a Hale chin."

Ma takes down the copper pan from its hook, the one you can see your face in if you hold it right, and gives it to me.

It's me. Bram. My own skin, the colour it was. The bump in the nose. The chin. Six little slits below my ears, three each side, closed tight. When I tip the pan away from the window, into the shadow, my eyes shine at the back, silver, like a knee-deer's at the pond.

"Your eyes," says Ma.

"I'm keeping those." I say it before I've thought. In my own voice. It comes out flat and a bit through the nose. "And the gills. And the feet. And the hands." I look at her. "I'm keeping them."

Ma looks at me for a long moment. Then she does what she always does when she's decided something. She hooks the bread knife off its peg with one finger and starts cutting.

"Keep them, then," she says. "Eat this."

"Say Tilly," says Tilly.

"Tilly."

"No," says Tilly, delighted. "Again."

◆ ◆ ◆

I'm on my fourth slice of bread and my second pot of honey when I remember the twist.

It's soft as cloth now, the paper gone brown with my hands. I put it on Gran's knee, and she opens it with her old fingers. Three seeds. Small and red-brown and hard and quiet.

"I planted the rest everywhere," I say. "By my pond. Under a hole in a cave roof. At the foot of a hive as big as the mill. In a barrel on a tree in the sea. There's tomatoes coming up all over Moriel, Gran. Red ones. Just sitting there."

Gran looks at the three seeds for a long time.

"Then you'll want to put those in Saul's rows," she says. "Where they came from."

"Gran." I take a breath, my own breath, through my own nose. "I met Lenna Brook."

Gran doesn't move. Not her hands. Not her face. Only her chest, which stops crackling, as if it's listening.

"She's old," I say. "One eye clouded, and her skin goes the colour of whatever she leans on. She's alive, Gran. The whole time, in the trees by the sea, pulling the others out of the wild. She said I had your chin. First thing she said."

"She laughs like a gate," says Sedge quietly, from her bench. "One somebody kicked."

Gran looks at Sedge. Then back at me.

"She said to tell you," I say, "she's still saying it."

For a while there's nothing in the kitchen but the stove ticking and, far above, the ceiling narrating someone else's house.

Then Gran closes her eyes.

"The root doesn't see the fruit," she says, "and the fruit forgets the root, and the tree goes on anyway."

She stops. The way she's always stopped. Her mouth closes on the end of it.

And then it opens again.

"And the seed remembers both," says Gran.

Her voice is different on it. Younger. Like something learned off by heart on a stair, a long time ago, and put away in a drawer, and never once taken out.

She folds the paper back round the three seeds and gives them to me, and holds on to my hand, hard, for a moment, the way Ma holds a rope.

"She always did go too far with that one," says Gran. "Old crow."

Then she coughs, a long one, gravel in a pail. And when it's finished she's laughing, very quietly, into her shawl.

Outside the window, Hask says, "Is it a discrepancy?"

"No, Hask," says Odile, who is not quite looking at any of us. "It's been corrected."

◆ ◆ ◆

Biscuit, meanwhile, has found the mother crock.

A wet, delighted, glopping noise from under the stairs, and then the kind of silence every Firstfield mother knows. Gran opens one eye.

Biscuit has its whole head and both front legs in the crock, its back four braced on the rim, its tail straight up like a mast, going round. I pull it out. It comes up with a cork-pop and a beard of starter from its whiskers to its chest, and its throat lit so amber it's nearly white.

"That," says Gran from her chair, very calm, "is two hundred years old."

Biscuit sneezes. Starter goes up the wall.

"It says," I tell her, while the voice behind my eyes translates, sounding faintly ashamed of itself, "you've got the best thing in the house under the stairs, and you should be ashamed, and it would die for you now."

"Tell it," says Gran, "if it does that again, it'll get the chance."

◆ ◆ ◆

The afternoon goes in names.

I go round the village with Tilly holding the back of the coat, and Sedge, and Odile a few steps behind with her slate. The wind goes down the lanes ahead of us, rattling shutters that have never once rattled.

Jen Cotter is in the mill office with the books open and her pen stopped. She was singing; I heard her from the lane. She looks up.

"Mae," I say. "Daughter of Jen. Of the house of Cotter. Number Ten."

Jen Cotter puts the pen down.

"She's a diver," I say. "Silver scales from her wrists to her elbows. She asked if you still sing when you add up."

Jen Cotter makes a sound like the mill wheel when the race is let in.

"She taught me to dive," Sedge says, from the doorway. "She holds her breath longer than anybody. She laughs when she comes up. Like that." She points at Jen, who is laughing, with her face in both hands, and crying, and the books blowing shut in the wind.

Mistress Tanner is on the bakehouse step with her basket of eggs, though it's not Thursday. She can see it on me from across the lane. She holds the basket very still.

"He asked if you still do the eggs," I say.

"Thursdays," says Joss's mother. "And every other day, since. In case."

"He's thin," I say, because she should know. "He coughs. We coughed at each other till Sedge got cross." I swallow. "When they asked him who wrote him, he said, My mam. And Thursdays."

She looks down into the basket. Then she takes out two brown eggs, the brown ones she keeps for Gran, and puts them in my hand, one and then the other, and closes my long fingers over them.

"For your gran," she says, and goes inside.

Old Saul is in his east rows, where the wind's coming in hardest, setting every tomato frame ticking against its stake. He's got his stick on a leaf.

"Saul," I say.

"Blight," says Saul. "Look at it. Wind. Wind, now. On my tomatoes. Two hundred years of good still air."

"Saul. Does the name Fenn mean anything?"

His stick stops.

"Ruth Fenn," he says, after a long while. "Lived where the bakehouse woodstore is now. Had a girl. Hollis. Bit me once, at a feast." He looks at the leaf, not at me. "Nobody of theirs left. Ruth went the winter after the girl was called. Some of them do."

"Hollis keeps the graves," I say. "On a tree by the sea. She's got hands like bark now. She plants them with something that smells of thyme."

Old Saul looks at the leaf a long time. Then he looks again.

"Well," he says. "Well."

Which from Saul is practically a speech.

◆ ◆ ◆

I find Elder Marrow at the foot of the bell tower.

He's just standing there, a big slow man with a bad hip, leaning on the tower wall in the wind, looking up. He's the one who read my letter at the Calling, in his weddings-and-funerals voice. And everyone cheered.

He's also the one who climbs up there every Thaw, with that hip, and rings the bell. Once.

Everybody knows. Nobody says.

He turns when I come. He came up the tower with a rope once, after Jory's dare, and fetched me down.

"Bram Hale," he says. "You've grown a pair of gills, I hear."

I can't answer yet. The wind's coming past him to me, and under the wool and the pipe smoke there's a smell I know. I've smelled it once. On a branch at the top of the tallest tree in Highwater, in the dark, under the salt and the crab and the bark, on a man who couldn't say whose he was.

Warm, and close, and a little sour. Like green apples in a bowl.

"You smell like him," I say.

Toby Marrow goes still.

"Davin," I say. "Son of Tobias Marrow. Of the house of Marrow. Number Nine." My own voice. It's shaking. "He's alive. He sleeps in the high branches of an orchard in the sea, and his skin goes like leaf-shade. He'd forgotten his name. And whose he was." I breathe in. "I smelled you on him. That's how I knew."

The old man doesn't fall down. He just puts his hand flat on the wall of the tower, the way I put mine on the ground to listen.

"He smelled his own wrist," I say. "After. Like he'd never smelled it before."

Toby Marrow looks up at the bell.

Then he starts to climb.

Jory, who's turned up from nowhere, takes a step to help, and Toby says, "No," without turning round, and goes on. One step. Then the hip. Then the next. The village is coming out into the lanes by then, because news goes through Firstfield the way it goes down the threads in the Undercroft. Warm thing at the door. Pass it on. They stand in the square and watch an old man go up the inside of the tower, slow, with the stair creaking under him.

It takes a long time.

Then the bell rings.

Not twice, for a Calling. Not once, for Thaw.

It rings and rings. Over and over. Clumsy, and loud, and much too fast, and nobody in Firstfield has ever heard it rung like that, like a child's been let at it. It goes out over the thatch and up into the dome, and through the open door, out across the hills, further than that bell has ever gone.

You can hear the bell from the ridges, Lenna told me, if the wind's right.

The wind's right.

◆ ◆ ◆

The meeting house fills, because when the bell rings, you go.

Dark beams. The benches worn smooth as river stones. Beeswax and old wood. The eleven brass plaques on the far wall above the elders' bench, polished like coins. REFERENCE No. 1. Called. Retired. Numbers. Only numbers.

And after Eleven, four drilled holes.

I stand under them. The whole village is on the benches and in the aisle and outside the open shutters. Hask fills the doorway. Through the high windows, the north walk is crammed with Shining folk with their faces on the glass.

I put my hand up to the first plaque, and I say the name. And whose. And what house.

Then Two. Three. Four. I know them off Lenna's boards, burned into wood in her slanting hand. At Four, Old Saul says, "Ah," very quietly from the back bench, as if someone's put a weight in his hands.

"Number Five," I say. "Lenna, daughter of Sal, of the house of Brook. She's alive."

The room makes a noise like wind in the thatch.

"Number Six. Hollis, daughter of Ruth, of the house of Fenn. Alive." Saul doesn't say anything this time. He's looking at his hands.

"Seven," I say, and his name. "He died. Lenna found him alive, and he lasted nine days, and she was with him."

"Eight. Pim Ashby. Alive. He sees in the dark and he hates noon." At the back, a white-haired woman I've only ever seen at the bakehouse says, "Pim," as if she's just remembered where she put something.

"Nine. Davin Marrow. Alive." Toby's at the end of the elders' bench with his hip out straight. He nods once, slow. His face is wet. He lets it be.

"Ten. Mae Cotter. Alive. Eleven. Joss Tanner. Alive."

I look at the four drilled holes.

"Twelve," I say. "Bram, son of Wenna, of the house of Hale. Alive."

Nobody cheers. It isn't that kind of room any more.

Below the plaques, in its frame, is the page of the Founders' Charter. Articles Two to Thirteen, about grain and roofs and bees. And Article Fourteen, one line, painted over on the inside of the glass in the same pale brown as the wall.

Master Aldous stands up from his bench. He's got his penknife out. His hands are shaking.

"No," says Odile, from the door, quite gently. "I'm sorry. That's evidence under an open case. It may not be altered."

Master Aldous looks at the knife as if he doesn't know whose it is.

"Then what does it say?" Tilly, on Ma's lap. Loud. Everybody turns. "Somebody say it."

Odile comes down the aisle. She takes out her slate, the cracked one, with the dead backlight, and holds it out to me.

"It's in my filing," she says. "Grounds for proposal fifty-nine-P. In the words I was given them. You read it. It's yours."

So I read it.

The screen's hard to read in here, grey on grey, and I take my time, the way Master Aldous taught us, with his watch ticking.

"And at the coming of age," I read, in my own voice, flat and a bit through the nose, the voice my sister does to make Jory laugh, "each shall choose: to keep, or to go and see. And the choosing shall be theirs."

It's a very short thing. It doesn't take any time at all.

The meeting house is so quiet you can hear the ribs tick.

And on the end of the front bench, Gran's lips are moving on the last part, a beat behind me, with no sound. And the choosing shall be theirs. As if it's a song somebody taught her a long time ago, and she never had anybody to sing it with.

At the end of the elders' bench, Dr. Ommery stands up.

Her face has gone the colour of the wall.

◆ ◆ ◆

She doesn't say much. Not tonight. I can smell what it costs her: the stuff she cleans cuts with, gone sour, and something like a burn, and under both, steady as a floor, the honey cake.

"I knew enough," Dr. Ommery says. "Not all of it. Enough. For twenty-two years I came down the stair, and I ate at your tables, and I taught your children where their livers are, and I took their blood once a year and labelled it in my best hand, and I carried it up. And I said nothing." She looks at the plaques. "I told myself that was kindness."

Jen Cotter stands up so fast her bench scrapes.

Toby Marrow puts his hand on her arm, and she sits down again. But it's there in the room. I can smell it on them all: a stove door, slammed. Ten stove doors. Forty.

"Tomorrow," says Dr. Ommery, "there's a hearing. Here, under the dome, with them watching. I'll say all of it there. Every vial. What you do with it is yours." She takes her spectacles off and holds them. "I'm not asking for anything tonight."

She sits back down. Nobody goes near her.

Except Gran, who doesn't get up, because her chest won't have it, but who reaches out along the front bench as the room starts to empty, and puts her old hand flat on the bench, a little way from Dr. Ommery's. Not touching. Very nearly.

◆ ◆ ◆

The stars come out while we're planting.

Nobody thought of it. Odile had the weather switched off this morning; a hearing ought to be held under the real sky, she said, and nobody upstairs could find the regulation that says otherwise. All day the glass has been plain, with real clouds going over it any way they liked, and Tilly kept saying the sky was broken, and Saul kept saying, That's weather.

And now it's going dark, and the dark isn't blue.

Saul and I are on our knees in the last bed of his east rows, by the wall, where his oldest plant used to be, the summer the sprinklers stuck. The Preserve men have taken two panels out of the end of the east walk on Odile's order, so the far door opens straight onto the rows: a gap in the dome you could drive a cart through, and the wind coming in through it smells of the whole world.

"First knuckle deep," says Saul. "And not a hair more."

I make the hole with my long grey finger. First knuckle, though my knuckles are further down than they were. One seed. Then the next. Then the last.

Saul pats the soil down over them with the flat of his hand.

"They'll not come up," he says. "Too cold. Too much wind. Bound to be blight."

"Look twice," I say.

He looks at me. Then he looks up.

Because they're coming out. Over the hills through the open door first, low down, one, then three. And then through the glass of the dome itself, between the ribs, past the skywalks, where for eight generations there's been nothing but a soft dark blue like the inside of a mussel shell. One. Ten. A hundred.

All over Firstfield, doors are opening.

People come out into the lanes and stand with their heads back. Mistress Thorne with her floury hands over her mouth. Jory on the meeting-house roof, where he is not allowed to be. Ma in our doorway with both hands on Tilly's shoulders. Sedge up the apple tree, of course, and Odile under it, looking not at the sky but at the people looking at it. Hask in the square with his top stone tipped all the way back. Gran in her chair, which Ma and Jory have carried out onto the step.

Master Aldous is at the edge of Saul's rows with his silver watch in his hand, not looking at it.

"I'm sorry, Bram," he says, not to me exactly. "I'd read the word. I didn't know either. Not really."

"The stars like dust," I say.

He laughs, a cracked little sound, and wipes his eyes with the hand that's holding the watch.

"Well," he says. "Now we know what dust is like."

◆ ◆ ◆

Dr. Ommery takes me up the long stair that night, when the village is in bed.

The first time, she didn't say one word the whole climb. This time she doesn't stop. That her first year at Firstfield she cried every night in the room at the top of the stair, because nobody had told her the children would be so kind. That Gran looked at her across our table that first winter, and she looked back, and neither of them ever said.

The Upper House smells the way it did: cedar and cooked ink, and bitter coffee from somewhere far away. Odile's waiting at a door that says AUTHORISED, and the man in green who opens it doesn't look at any of us.

The room is amber. The tanks go back further than the light. Most are empty. The first one isn't.

He's curled on himself in the fluid like a question. A long way to go before he's a boy. Webbed fingers. Closed eyes. A cable from his middle to the base of the tank, and the pump going, slow and wet, every few seconds, like something breathing for him.

A strip of tape on the glass. 13.

Low in the side of the tank there's a port, a round soft window rimmed with brass, warm, where the nurses take their samples. Dr. Ommery shows me, and stands back.

I kneel and put my palm on it.

And I listen.

Through my palm, through the bones of my arm, into my jaw. Under the pump, which is huge in here: something small and very fast. Tk-tk-tk-tk. A sparrow in a hand.

Lightly, I tell the voice behind my eyes.

Lightly, it agrees.

It doesn't go in. It only tastes, the way you'd taste a spoon of something to see if it's ready. And I feel it go quiet.

He's very new, it says. Nobody's ever said anything to him. Not one word. A pause. Nobody's anybody's, in here.

The pump sighs. The little heart goes on, tk-tk-tk, in the amber, on its own.

I lean in close. The glass smells of nothing at all, which is the worst smell I know now.

"Hello," I say. In my voice. "My name's Bram. I'm the son of Wenna, who keeps the bees and the books for the mill. I'm the brother of Tilly, who is eight and the worst person alive. I'm the grandson of Ada Hale, who has the best chin in Firstfield."

The pump sighs.

"What do you call a tomato that won't sing?" I tell him. "A tomato. That's my sister's. She's told it about four hundred times. And here's mine. What do you call a tomato that hums? One that's forgotten the words."

Behind me, Dr. Ommery makes a small noise and turns away.

"There's a lot more out there than anyone ever told us," I tell Thirteen. "I'm going to go and see it. But I'll come back. I promise. I'll keep coming back, and I'll keep talking, and when you come out of there, you'll already know my voice. You'll hear it and think, oh, him." I take a breath. "You won't be nobody's. I won't let you."

Under my palm, the little heart goes tk-tk-tk-tk.

And then, once, very slightly, it skips.

He heard, says the voice behind my eyes. Something did. It's quiet a while. I didn't know they could, so small.

"Neither did I."

Odile's in the doorway when I get up, looking out of the corridor window past my shoulder.

"Look," she says.

Out past the Preserve and the dark hills, something is coming down the sky.

A light. Too slow for a star, and going the wrong way, down, with a long pale tail behind it. A sound comes in after it, through the floor, through my feet: a low far roar like the mill race heard from under the water.

Then smaller ones, grey, with blue nose lights. Four. Six. More.

"My Chief Examiner," says Odile. "On the hop, from Margin Station." She watches the grey ones settle behind the Preserve's roofs. "And the Associate. All of him."

◆ ◆ ◆

I don't sleep much.

I lie in the loft above the kitchen and listen to my family breathe. Tilly snuffling, like a small engine that's run out of fuel. Ma turning over, and over, and then not turning. Under it all, the dome, humming, the ribs ticking as they cool. And through the open door at the end of the east rows, a whole other night coming in: a chirring in the grass that rises and falls like breathing, and something a long way off going tok... tok..., like a spoon on a pot.

The dark isn't dark. My eyes make it silver. The rafters, the thatch, Tilly's foot sticking out of the blanket.

At three in the morning I go down the stairs, past the mother crock in its dark corner, which tastes slightly of otter now and doesn't seem to mind.

Gran is awake.

She's in her chair by the dead stove, wrapped in her shawl, and she doesn't seem surprised to see me.

We look at each other for a long time.

"If you're going to the privy," Gran says finally, "take a coat."

"I've got a coat."

"So you have." She looks at it. Brown, long, too big, the pockets bulging. "It's not yours."

"It's Odile's."

"Then give it her back before the morning," says Gran. "She'll want to look smart."

I sit on the floor with my back against her knees, the way I did when I was small enough to sit on her lap and watch the skywalks pass over. She puts her hand on my head.

Out of the window, up in the dark, a light is moving along the north walk.

Not the night guard; I can hear him snoring a whole skywalk away. Someone in a long coat, alone, walking slowly over our roofs. He stops above the square and stands there, looking down at Firstfield in the starlight. At the open Window. At the bell tower, and Saul's rows, and the Hale house with its blue door, and a boy in its window looking back.

His coat, while I watch, goes from grey to violet to a sort of gold.

I lift my hand. And I wave. A waver, I'd have written, once.

The man on the skywalk looks startled. Then he looks something else. Then something I don't have a category for yet.

He doesn't wave back.

But he doesn't move on, either.

"Tomorrow, then," says Gran, above my head, with her eyes shut.

"Tomorrow."

Up in the ribs of the dome, very softly, the audio guide clicks. The wind's tripped something. There's a hiss.

"Here we see," says the warm dead voice, to the whole sleeping village, "the Baseline Humans at — at —"

It searches. There's no script for this. There's a long, long hiss of dead air.

Gran opens one eye at the ceiling.

"Home," she tells it.

And for once, it doesn't argue.
