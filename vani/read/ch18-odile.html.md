I didn't sleep. I lay in the Hales' loft on a straw tick that smelled of lavender and boy, and listened to a village learn what wind does to a shutter.

At first grey I went down the stairs past the mother crock, which smelled faintly and unmistakably of otter, and found my coat folded on the bottom step. Brushed. Every pocket buttoned down over what was in it. On top of it, a slice of bread and honey on a saucer, and a note in a big careful hand with the B's bottom loop too big: Gran says you'll want to look smart.

I ate the bread standing up. Clover. Then I put the coat on, and it was heavy in the way only my coat is heavy, and I went out to meet my Chief Examiner.

The far door stood open at the end of Saul's east rows, where the Preserve men had taken out two panels of the walk. Through the gap the hills were going from grey to the colour of weak tea, and the air coming in tasted of dew and dust and, very faintly, of the burn of a hop's engines settling: hot metal, ozone, a smell like a struck match left in a drawer. The tomato frames ticked against their stakes. In the last bed by the wall, three small mounds of earth sat patted flat by an old man's hand.

Halloran came up the path from the Preserve at the pace of a man who has never once in his life been made to hurry and does not intend to start on a hill.

In eleven years I had seen him perhaps a dozen times, always behind his desk. Outdoors he was bigger. Broad as a door, grey all over: grey hair cropped like a field after harvest, grey stubble, a coat the colour of wet slate that had been good once. A heavy face, folded, patient as a horse's. Under his left arm was the shell tin, which I had heard every week for eleven years: dented, a biscuit tin once, with a faded lighthouse on the lid.

He stopped at the edge of the rows, cracked a seed between his front teeth, and spat the shell into the tin. Tock.

"Pastern."

"Sir."

"You look terrible."

"Thank you, sir."

He looked past me at the smoke of forty-one chimneys standing up and then, for the first time in their lives, leaning over.

"I've signed things about this place for thirty-one years," he said. "Never been." Tock. "I brought you some witnesses."

I hadn't heard them, because nobody hears Lenna Brook unless she wants them to. She had been leaning on the hop's landing strut at the bottom of the path and she had gone grey as it, and now she came up into the morning with her stick, small and brown and white-cropped, her skin going the colour of the path as she climbed. Behind her came the others. Hollis Fenn with her bark-hard hands folded in her sleeves. Pim Ashby, wrapped to the eyes in a sailcloth hood against a sun that wasn't up yet. Mae Cotter, scales silver at her wrists. Joss Tanner on Mae's arm, coughing. And last, keeping off the path, moving along the grass edge the way a fox moves along a hedge, mottled like leaf-shade, Davin Marrow.

"You filed three hundred and three persons on the twenty-first," said Halloran. "As witnesses, among other things. A witness can be summoned." He spat. "The hop put down on the coast at two this morning. Very irregular. I appear to have lost the flight plan."

"Sir," I said, and found I couldn't say anything else.

"Don't," said Halloran. "It's early."

* * *

Gran was at the far door before anyone had thought to fetch her.

I don't know who told her. Nobody, I think. She had come down the east rows on Wenna's arm in her shawl and her boots, stopping every few frames for her chest, and she stopped now at the edge of the gap, where the dome's floor ended and the outside began, and did not step over.

Lenna stopped on the other side of it.

They looked at each other across a line on the ground for what I'd guess was the better part of a minute. Two old women in the first sun. One tall, square-jawed, set like a gatepost. One small and brown, one eye clouded, with her skin going slowly, as she stood there, the colour of the far door's brass rim.

"You took your time," said Gran.

"I was busy," said Lenna.

"Sixty-two years."

"I know how long it was, Ada. I was there for all of it."

Gran's chest crackled like paper. "You never could leave a proverb alone."

"And the seed remembers both," said Lenna. "Somebody had to."

Then Gran put out her hand across the line, and Lenna took it, and leaned on it. And her skin went, from the fingers up, a soft worn grey-brown with a fleck in it: the exact colour of Ada Hale's shawl.

Neither of them said anything else. I looked away, because the Survey teaches you that some things are not yours to watch, and because my eyes had begun to sting in the dry air.

Behind me, I heard Halloran's tin go tock, much too loudly, and then a small scuffle, and then the Chief Examiner of Margin Station saying, in a tone I had never once heard from him, "That's mine."

Biscuit was sitting on the tin. It had its whole front end inside it and its four back legs braced on Halloran's forearm, and it was eating his sunflower seeds out of the shells with an expression of pure religious joy.

"Is that animal evidence?" said Halloran.

"It's Bram's, sir."

"That isn't what I asked."

Bram came down the rows at a run, in his own face, barefoot on plated soles, and stopped dead in front of Halloran with his gill-slits shut tight under his ears.

Halloran looked down at him. A long, slow, folded look, the one he gives a form before he signs it.

"You're the goat," he said.

"I'm the boy," said Bram. "The goat's outside." He held out his long grey fingers. "Biscuit. Out."

Biscuit came out of the tin with a shell stuck to its nose and its throat lit amber, and went up Bram's arm and into his shirt. Halloran looked into his tin. Then at the boy. Then, for the third time in the eleven years I have known him, he made the sound I have decided to call a laugh.

Up the edge of the rows, by the wall, Davin Marrow had stopped. Elder Marrow was standing at the foot of the bell tower in the next lane with one hand on its stones. Neither of them moved. Then Davin, very slowly, put his own wrist to his face and breathed in, and looked up, and walked on along the wall toward his father without once looking at the ground. I didn't watch the rest of it. It wasn't mine either.

* * *

The meeting house filled at the second bell.

Half of Firstfield had brought cushions: flour sacks stuffed with chaff, a hen-house pillow leaking feathers. The shutters stood open, and the wind came in one side and out the other, carrying beeswax and woodsmoke and bread. Faces filled every window. Hask filled the main doorway entirely, his top stone tipped in under the lintel like a man listening at a keyhole.

Up through the high windows, the north walk was solid with the Shining folk. And among them, near the middle, four people in company grey who were not pressing their faces to the glass at all. They stood with their hands folded, the way people stand at the back of a funeral to see who has come. The board of Varro-Tandem Bioreference had come to watch its executive.

The executive sat on the end of the left-hand bench in his long coat, and the coat was the dull dark of old brass.

Director Penhallow sat across the aisle from him in Heritage Service green with her silver hair set and her slate on her knee, smelling of flowers bought somewhere expensive and a long night spent looking for a regulation.

Dr. Ommery sat at the end of the elders' bench where she always sat, with a cloth bag on her lap. Nobody had sat next to her. The bench beside her was bare, worn smooth as river stone.

And the Associate sat everywhere else.

Twenty-three of him, tall and thin and very neat, company grey from collar to shoe, silver hair combed flat, slim ledger cases on twenty-three knees, filling the front three benches on the right like a row of identical pencils in a box. The room smelled of cedar and copier toner. Pell-7 was at the end of the second row in shoes two sizes too large. Pell-12 still had a stung puffiness under one eye. Pell-22 caught my eye and looked, very briefly, at the launcher on my shoulder, and wrote something down.

Halloran lowered himself onto the elders' bench under the eleven brass plaques. It creaked. He set the tin beside him with Biscuit's teeth-marks in the lid.

"This is a hearing under an open Discrepancy," he said, without raising his voice, and the room heard every word of it. "Reference Twelve, and the cases that have come off it like shoots off a potato. The Examiner will state it. Anyone named in the record may speak. Anyone who throws anything leaves." He looked up through the high windows at the north walk. "That includes you."

On the north walk, several hundred Shining folk looked guilty at once.

"First," said Halloran, "who speaks for the Associate?"

Twenty-three Associates stood up.

Halloran cracked a seed. Tock.

"One of you."

"Chief Examiner," said Pell-4, from the end of the front row, in the cedar baritone they all have. "I was issued first of those present. Seniority—"

"Four has been in company stock as a crate since the seventeenth," said Pell-7. "Materials may not address a hearing."

"I was logged as a crate," said Pell-4, with real heat. "I have never once been a crate."

"The licence," said Pell-19, "is clear that every copy is the Associate."

"I lodge a grievance," said Pell-12, "against Seven, for the crate remark."

"Noted," said eleven of them, and wrote it down.

Halloran let it run, the way you let a dog run off its first excitement in a field. Then he said, "The Associate will sit down," and all twenty-three of them sat down at once, and somebody on the back bench laughed so hard he had to be thumped. I think it was Jory.

"You'll speak in order of grievance," said Halloran. "Fewest first."

There was a pause of real horror along the front three benches while twenty-three men silently counted.

* * *

The company spoke first, through a Pell with no visible number on his pin: the lead from Highwater. I knew the voice off the hailer. He opened his ledger case and read the board's complaint into the record, in a pleasant baritone, as if it were a menu. Assault on an Associate. Damage to a door. Theft of a compliance wand. And removal of materials by livestock.

"The board," he said, "further asks that the Examiner's undertaking be struck, as obtained from an executive acting beyond his authority, under the pressure of a dinner."

"The pudding was never served," said Sedge, from the third bench, darkly. Several of the village children nodded.

"Examiner," said Halloran.

I stood up.

I had never stood up in a room where I could smell every bench: bread, honey, wet wool, a goat, which was me, and from twenty-three grey shoulders, cedar.

"The assault," I said. "Pell-Twenty-two, were you hurt?"

Pell-22 considered. "It was very neatly done."

"The door failed open, as every door on Moriel is required to by the fire code. I have the code number if the board wants it. The compliance wand." I took it out of my inside pocket, black, the brass cap scorched, and laid it on the elders' bench beside the tin. "Returned. Logged. It's been in a pocket with a moonpear stone, so it may smell of fruit."

Pell-22 took it back with both hands and looked as if he wanted to thank me and had found nothing in the licence that allowed it.

"Which leaves the livestock," I said.

"Read me the materials," I said to the lead Pell. "The ones removed. As the board described them in its resolution of nine fifty-two that night."

He found the page and read it as written. They are all careful men.

"Archival reader, juvenile, with contents."

"Thank you." I took out my slate, the cracked one, readable only by the light from the high windows and the glow under my own nails, and turned it round to the room. "That reader has a row in the Register. Table PERSON. Entered on the twenty-first, at Highwater, at her own answer. Name: Sedge. Maker, line: us. And since dawn on the twenty-second she has been the reader on record for archival wetland AW-3." I put the slate down. "The board resolved at nine fifty-two to recover a registered person as company stock. It was prevented by a woman, two grazers and a girl who whistles. And at four the next morning, it filed a complaint about being prevented."

I looked up at the north walk, at the four in grey.

"I'd like the board's complaint entered as evidence," I said. "Theirs."

It went round the benches the way a joke goes round a schoolroom, a beat late and then all at once. The four in grey on the north walk did not move. Halloran took a seed out of the tin, looked at it, and put it back.

"So entered," he said.

* * *

Then I emptied my pockets.

Bram had buttoned every one. I laid it all along the elders' bench in front of Halloran, one thing at a time, plain, with the time. A reed stem with eight knots in it. A scrap of grey shirt with a label corner. An ear of the Pale's wild grain. A moonpear stone. The flask, with its four dents and two tooth-holes, which I set down and did not open, though I wanted to. The survey of the Library of Moriel in its sailcloth, and the inky thumbprint on its first page. My slate, with the drawers of every open case behind it: the Spill, UC-114, the Index, Highwater, AW-one to seven, and Twelve.

And from the pocket over my heart, three folded papers.

"A consent," I said, and unfolded the first. "Signed at the Fifth Pond at dawn on the twenty-second. Sedge. Big letters, leaning backwards." Sedge, on the third bench, went red to the ears and stared hard at the ceiling. "An undertaking. Signed in the glass room at the top of the High Gardens, at nine forty-one that evening, in my hand and his. I undertake to fund the waking of the seven ponds of the Library of Moriel, with willing partners, in place of the draw. L. Varro."

On the end of the left bench, the coat did nothing at all. Old brass.

The third paper had been folded like a sheet off the line, corner to corner. It had a smear of black pond-mud along one edge.

"And this," I said, "was written by the subject of the open case, at dawn on the twenty-third, on the morning after he was poisoned and lost his face, when he was asked who he was and couldn't say."

I didn't look at Bram. I read it.

"Bram. And under it." I went down the list, and then up the side, where the pencil had run out of room. "Wenna's. Ada's. Tilly's. Saul's. Jory's. Master Aldous's. Dr. Ommery's."

I heard that one land. I didn't stop.

"Lenna's. Teasel's. Hask's. Odile's. Sedge's." I turned the paper. "Biscuit's, probably."

A small, cracked noise from the end of the elders' bench. I didn't look at that either.

In the doorway, Hask's stack ground faintly. "I attest," he said, "that he wrote it. I was a roof at the time, but I was facing the right way."

"The Register," I said, "has him as RETIRED. It has had him so for fifteen days. It has every one of these people," I touched the list, "as having nothing to do with him. They're all here. You can ask them."

From the back of the room, very clearly, Tilly Hale said, "He spelt mine right."

* * *

The Pells spoke in order of grievance. It took most of the morning, and they were very good: the Heritage Acts, the licence, a recovery precedent involving a herd of heritage swine. Every one of them wrote down everything every other one said, and filed against it.

The grace came from the copy with the most grievances, which meant he spoke last.

Pell-26 stood up holding his ledger in both hands, the way he'd held it on the back of a grazer. He had a fading bruise across the bridge of his nose in the exact shape of the glass-room roof.

"I was asked," he said, "to read the company's record of the night of the twenty-second. As the copy present." He opened the ledger. "I have it. Nine-oh-eight, subject sighted, gallery level. Nine-oh-nine, settling agent administered. Breathing ceased. Count begun." He turned a page. "Nine-thirteen, subject breathing. Subject's voice: not the subject's voice."

He stopped.

The next half page was blank. I could see it from where I stood: clean, ruled, cream, the only empty paper I had ever seen in an Associate's hand.

"There is a gap," said Pell-26. "I've checked it several times. I appear not to have written anything down." He looked at the blank half page as though it were a door he had found in his own house. "I'm unable to account for that."

"What happened in the gap?" said Halloran.

Pell-26 looked across the room, at a boy on the second bench with a square chin and silver at the back of his eyes in the shadow of the beams.

"I looked at him," said Pell-26. "And there wasn't a subject. There was a person, who was very hungry and very cross with me. I knew him by the expression." He closed the ledger. "I'd like the gap entered. As it is. I don't think I should fill it in now. It would be a forgery."

Twenty-two Associates turned their heads at once and looked at him.

"Grievance," said Pell-19, faintly, "against Twenty-six. Failure to document."

"Yes," said Pell-26, and sat down. "That's fair."

* * *

Dr. Ommery stood up when the Pells had done, and the room changed temperature.

She'd brought the cloth bag. Out of it she took notebooks, one after another, cheap school ones, until there were twenty-two of them stacked on her knees and on the bench beside her where nobody was sitting.

"Every vial," she said. "Every child, once a year, for twenty-two years. The name, the house, the date, the arm. I labelled them in my best hand and carried them up the long stair, and I kept a copy of every label, because I was afraid that one day somebody would ask, and I wanted to be able to answer properly." She put her hand flat on the stack. "Nobody asked. So I'm answering now."

She told it plainly, the way a doctor does. What she knew and when. What she guessed and decided not to know. Bram's calling, and what she said to him at his mother's table, and where she stopped.

"I loved you," she said to the benches. "That's true. And I said nothing. That's also true. I've had twenty-two years to make the second one smaller by the first, and it won't go." She looked up at the plaques. "I'm not asking for anything."

Jen Cotter stood up.

She didn't shout. She stood up with her hands in fists and her mouth working, and Toby Marrow didn't put his hand on her arm this time, because he was standing too. And then Mistress Tanner, with her basket on her lap. And then, row by row, not all of them, but a great many, the people of Firstfield stood up and said nothing, which was worse. The wind came through the shutters and moved their hair and their aprons and the corners of twenty-two notebooks. On the north walk, the Shining folk had gone very still.

Then Ada Hale got up.

It took her a long time. Her chest wouldn't have it, and she made it have it, one hand on the bench end and the other on Lenna's shoulder. Lenna came up with her like a stick under a vine, and her skin went shawl-brown again where they touched. Gran crossed the four steps of bare floor to the end of the elders' bench, and stopped in front of Dr. Ommery, and looked down at her. And her face, which was set like a gatepost, did not change at all.

"I looked at you across my table," said Gran, "your first winter. And you looked back. And neither of us said." She breathed, gravel in a pail. "I've been carrying that too. So that's two of us."

Dr. Ommery's mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.

"I'm not saying it was right," said Gran. "It wasn't. I'm saying I'm putting it down. I'm old and my chest's bad and I want my hands back." She put one of them, the old one that had shaken Bram's chin at the pump, flat on top of the stack of notebooks. Then she took Dr. Ommery's hand off it, and held it. "There. That's done. It'll want doing again tomorrow, I expect. I'll do it again tomorrow."

There isn't a form for that. I'd told Dr. Ommery so, the first day, at the outer gate. I'd meant it as an apology. Standing there, I found I'd been right in a way I hadn't understood: there isn't a form, because there's nothing to file. Gran just did it, in front of everyone, and it cost her a breath she didn't have.

Lenna, still propping her, looked Dr. Ommery up and down with her one good eye.

"I'm the one it happened to," said Lenna. "So I'll say it too. You're forgiven, you great fool." She jerked her chin. "Now shift up. Ada's got to sit down and you're on the only bit of bench without a cushion."

Dr. Ommery shifted up. Gran sat down beside her, hard, and Lenna sat beside Gran, and that was the bare bench beside Dr. Ommery gone.

Toby Marrow sat down. Mistress Tanner sat down. Jen Cotter stood for a long time after everyone else, with her fists shut, looking at the two old women and the doctor between them.

"Not today," she said at last, to the doctor, quite steadily. "I can't today."

"No," said Dr. Ommery.

"Then tomorrow, maybe," said Gran, with her eyes shut. "It keeps."

Jen Cotter sat down. Halfway along the bench her daughter Mae, who had been in the room the whole time, reached across and took her hand, and Jen Cotter looked at the silver scales on the wrist and began, quietly, to sing under her breath, the way she does when she's adding up.

There was a silence of the kind that wants something to happen in it. What happened was Biscuit waking up in Bram's shirt, smelling Halloran's tin on the elders' bench, and going for it straight down the aisle at a gallop with its throat lit like a lamp.

Halloran put his hand flat on the lid without looking.

Biscuit sat down in front of him and gave him a look of such betrayal that the whole front bench of Associates leaned away from it.

* * *

"The undertaking," said Halloran, when the room had finished laughing, and the laughing had turned into the kind of breathing people do after crying. "The board says it's struck. Mr Varro."

Varro stood up.

He didn't look at me. He looked up through the high windows, at the north walk, at the four people in grey with their hands folded, and they looked back down at him through the glass. And his coat, as I watched, went the colour I'd seen it go once before at a dinner table: slate, going down to the blue at the bottom of a flame.

Everybody in the room could see it. It was the first thing the whole of Firstfield had ever learned to read about a Shining man, and they read it at once. He was frightened.

He stood there in it. He didn't try to hide it. You can't, in that coat.

"The board is right," said Lisle Varro, in his soft unhurried voice. "My authority was set aside at nine fifty-two. They're entitled to do that. They hold the Tandem half of the company, and a good deal of the Varro half besides." His square clean hands hung at his sides, open. "They can't set aside my signature. The Examiner pointed that out to me. It's taken me two days to see what she meant."

He took a grey disc out of his pocket and held it up, so that the board could see it from the walk.

"At six this morning," he said, "I signed what remains of my own holding to the Library of Moriel. Not the company's. Mine. It will pay for the waking of seven ponds, slowly, with willing partners. It won't pay for anything else." He put the disc down on the elders' bench, next to the scorched wand and the moonpear stone. "Item, one undertaking. Kept."

The coat stayed blue. All the way through. He said every word of it in the colour of a man who expects to be cut off at the knees by lunchtime, and he said it anyway.

On the north walk, one of the four in grey turned and walked away along the glass. Then the other three.

Varro watched them go. And then, slowly, from the collar down, the coat began to go gold. Not the gold of a man pleased with himself. Something paler and stranger, like honey held up to a window. I don't know what it was. I don't think he did.

"You've cost me something," he said to me, quietly, under the noise the room was making, "I can learn from."

"I know," I said. "I'm sorry it was all of it."

"It wasn't," said Varro, and looked at Sedge, and sat down.

* * *

Then it was mine.

Director Penhallow had one more thing to say first, and she said it well. She stood with her slate and read out Article One of the Founders' Charter from the frame on the wall, in a clear, lovely, visiting voice, as if she were introducing a fountain. That the Original shall be kept, in honour and in health, that we may never forget what we were.

"Kept," she said. "Not consulted. The Heritage Service has kept them, Examiner. In honour. In health. For two hundred years. The Register agrees."

"Yes," I said. "It does. That's the trouble with it."

I looked at the frame. At Article One, curly capitals. And below it at the one pale-brown line painted on the inside of the glass, that Master Aldous had wanted to take his penknife to last night, and that I'd told him he couldn't.

"Article Fourteen uses the same word," I said. "To keep, or to go and see. The founders weren't careless with words. They had a surveyor writing them who measured in chains. Keep is what the children choose. It's what Ada Hale's family did with a crock of starter under their stairs for two hundred years, because they wanted to. It isn't a thing you do to a person. It's a thing a person does." I turned back to the room. "The Preserve kept the word and dropped the person doing it. A clerk did that, a hundred and forty years ago, tidying a table. There's a tick in the margin."

Penhallow sat down. She didn't argue. I think she had already read the tick.

"Before I make the ruling," I said to Halloran, "the company asked by what authority an Examiner walks the plan-room of the Register. The Reading. It's a fair question."

He waited.

There's a gully on a moor in my head, and the ground was still soft. I felt the water start to run that way when I opened my mouth. I let it run. Then I said the name, out loud, in a meeting house, for the first time in seven years.

"I was taught it by Examiner Fallow," I said. "Of Margin Station. In my first winter. He taught me to sit down first."

Halloran's hand stopped on its way to the tin. It stayed stopped. In eleven years I have never seen it stop.

It was just a name. It went out into the room and nothing happened to it. The wind came through the shutters and took it with everything else.

"So I'm going to sit down," I said.

* * *

I took Pell-4's empty place on the end of the front bench, because he had stood up to object to something, and I put my slate on my knee and my hands flat on the slate, and I shut my eyes.

The oak door. Iron-bound. Beeswax and cold stone, my palm on it, and in.

I went through REFERENCE, the short hall, twelve drawers, every one with the same four drilled holes in its front where a brass plate had been unscrewed a hundred and forty years ago and never put back. Every corridor ending in a wall, and beyond six of the walls, faintly, through the plaster, the living people they belonged to, breathing on a bench in a meeting house. And Twelve. RETIRED. A corridor to a wall of damp plaster fifteen days old, still smelling of lime.

Then HERITAGE RESIDENT, two hundred and eleven living drawers and eight generations of shut ones behind them, every one with four drilled holes in its face.

The plan-room, round, under its dome. The book of revisions on its lectern, its brass chain cold. Revision 31 with its little tick. And in the black japanned tray marked PROPOSED, where it had been sitting since a night in the orchards, alone, the best tray, the one nobody ever uses: 59-P.

I took it out of the tray.

I made the ruling aloud, so that the meeting house heard it too. I know I did, because afterwards Tilly could say it back to me.

"Under the open case, I find that the column CHOOSING was removed from the Register at Revision 31 without cause, and with no data migrated, and that its removal is the Discrepancy from which every other in this case descends. I rule that it be restored to REFERENCE and to HERITAGE RESIDENT, as it stood at Revision 30. Default, open at coming of age." I breathed. "And I find that the old plan had no way to record a person who had used it. That they were held, or retired, and nothing else, because the people who wrote the plan never expected anyone to go. So I rule that the column take a second value. Not open. Exercised. A person who has chosen and gone to see." I put my hand on the chained book. "Revision fifty-nine. For adoption."

The Register doesn't have opinions. It waits for a seal.

And from somewhere far away and outside, in a meeting house that smelled of bread, I heard it come: a heavy man's hand, the creak of an elders' bench, a seal pressed on a cracked slate. And then, quite distinctly, through all the halls of the Register at once, a small dry sound, like a shell falling into a tin.

Tock.

The book of revisions turned its own page.

And all down the long halls, every drawer at once, I heard the plates go back.

Small brass plates coming home into four drilled holes each, screws turning, a sound like a thousand watches being wound at once by a thousand careful people with keys on strings. Down REFERENCE. Down HERITAGE RESIDENT, the living and the shut, eight generations of children who had never been told they had a field. The sound went away into the dark of the halls like rain going over a roof. On every plate the same words, in the Register's tidy hand. CHOOSING: open at coming of age.

On the dead it wrote nothing. Rows one to four, and seven: CHOOSING, and an empty plate. A drawer that admits it is empty. I had decided that at Highwater and I didn't change it now. They were never asked. I wasn't going to answer for them.

I went to Twelve.

The plate was on. CHOOSING: EXERCISED. New letters, a little brighter than the rest, the only ones of their kind anywhere in the Register of Moriel. And the status slide beside it, with its two notches, HELD and RETIRED, the only two places a child of Firstfield had ever been allowed to be: the slide had come off its track. It lay on the floor of the hall like a dropped bolt.

And the corridor. The next line, the one that had ended in fifteen-day-old plaster.

The plaster had cracked from top to bottom. While I watched, it fell, in big damp slabs that broke on the floor and smelled of lime and rain. And behind it there was no room.

There was outside.

The corridor ran straight on out of the Register and into open air. I could feel wind coming down it. Dry grass, dust, the green musk of the Spill, something warm and yeasty that I'd smelled on a cloud forest at night. A corridor that didn't end in a wall or a drawer, but pointed out into the world at a person who was standing in it, and would go on standing in it, wherever he went, and the Register would hold him by the pointing.

Nobody had ever seen a corridor like it. Until that moment there had been no way to build one. The Register didn't care. It had simply found that it could now hold a new kind of thing, and it held it, serenely, the way it holds everything: instantly and without doubt.

It went down the other rows after that, because I'd asked it to. RETIRED struck off five, six, eight, nine, ten and eleven, and their corridors broke through into PERSON, each to the name they'd given at their own answer. On the dead, Died, with dates from boards burned in a slanting hand on a platform over the sea. AW-1 to AW-7: REINSTATED, confirmed under seal, three readers on record and four ponds sleeping, waiting to be asked. The REFERENCE hall closed at twelve. No thirteenth drawer. There will never be a thirteenth drawer in that hall.

And down in PERSON, a new row, very small, that I'd made at the end and hadn't told anyone I was going to: Thirteen. In the care of the keeper of Firstfield. Maker, line: to be answered by him. CHOOSING: open at coming of age.

I left by the oak door. It was warm under my palm, for the first time.

* * *

I came out cold and stiff and so hungry I could see it, with a drawer slammed on the inside of my skull, into a noise I didn't understand.

It was chiming.

Every slate in the meeting house was chiming at once: the Register's double note, the one it gives on a followed record. Halloran's. The director's. Varro's disc. Twenty-three ledger cases, so that the front benches sounded like a shelf of clocks striking. And up on the north walk, where the Shining folk had been following the case on the crawl since the Index went UNDER DISCREPANCY, every slate chimed. The sound came down through the glass of the dome, muffled, hundreds of small double notes going off up there among the ribs, like bells in a far tower, like a whole sky full of something agreeing.

Halloran was looking at his slate. He read it twice. He put it face down on the bench, and put his big grey hand flat on top of it, and sat like that a moment with his eyes shut.

"Revision fifty-nine," he said, "is adopted." Tock. "Reference Twelve is not retired. He is not held. He is not a specimen. Write him down as what he is."

I couldn't speak. My mouth had nothing in it. So the boy said it for me, from the second bench, in his own voice, flat and a bit through the nose.

"Bram Hale," he said. "Choosing exercised."

And a bit of bread went into my hand. I don't know where from. Wenna Hale's, I think. I ate it.

That's when the village looked up.

Not at me. Up, through the high windows, at the north walk. For two hundred years Firstfield had been looked down on through that glass, and narrated, and noted. Now every head in the meeting house tipped back, and they looked up at the people looking down: gill-necks, four arms, fur like spilled ink, and skin going every colour there is in a wave along the walk.

And the Shining folk, who could not get any closer, and could not be heard through the glass, did the only thing they could. They tapped.

One at first. Then twenty. Then the whole of the north walk, knuckles and claws and soft pads and the end of somebody's tail, rapping on the glass, right under the sign that says DO NOT TAP THE GLASS, until the dome rang with it like a roof in hail.

Jory stood up on his bench.

"Tappers!" he yelled, joyfully, at the sky.

And then Firstfield, which had never in eight generations done such a thing, waved back.

* * *

"Now, Master Aldous," I said.

He'd had his penknife in his hand since the second bell. He opened the frame's little brass catch, lifted the glass out onto the elders' bench in front of the Chief Examiner, and then, very carefully, with the flat of the blade, the way he'd peel the skin off an apple for a child, he began to scrape.

The paint came off the inside of the glass in pale brown curls. It smelled of old linseed. Nobody spoke. Under it, letter by letter, in big curly capitals, the line came up into the light, and it started with a C, the way Bram had told me it did, the night his whole village cheered and he looked at it because it was easier than looking at his mother.

Master Aldous didn't read it out. He didn't have to. Half the room said it with him, a beat behind, the way you say something you've only just learned and already know.

And the choosing shall be theirs.

Then he wiped the blade on his waistcoat, and passed the knife to Lenna Brook.

The plaques went up the wall in a row above the elders' bench, eleven, brass, polished like coins, numbers only. Lenna went to the fifth. She was too short to reach it; Jory, without being asked, dragged the end of a bench across the floor, and she stepped up onto it like a mounting block, and with her stick hooked under one arm and her tongue in the corner of her mouth, she scratched her own name into the brass under the number, slowly, in letters that leaned forward like a woman walking into wind.

LENNA BROOK.

"Sixty-two years," she said, stepping down. "I'd like the credit."

Hollis did six with her slow bark-hands. Pim did eight with his owl's eyes screwed shut against the light, laughing. Mae did ten. Joss did eleven between coughs, with his mother's hand on his back. Lenna did one, two, three, four and seven from her boards.

Nine waited a long time.

Davin Marrow came up the aisle at the end, along the edge of the benches, not touching anything. He took the knife from Lenna and looked at the plaque, and at the number on it, and at the knife, the way a man looks at a road he isn't sure is his. Behind him, his father had stopped breathing.

Then Davin wrote. It took him longer than it took anyone. His letters were big and uneven and they went uphill.

DAVIN. MARROWS.

He'd put the S on because that's how he answers. Nobody corrected it. Nobody ever will.

There were still four drilled holes in the wall after the eleventh.

I took the last thing out of my coat. I'd found it the day before, in a drawer in the director's stores when I went looking for the key to the Window: brass, plaque-sized, in a cloth, already engraved, already polished. REFERENCE No. 12. Called. A date. Retired. And a second date, left blank, waiting. They had made it before they had drilled the holes.

I gave it to Toby Marrow.

He read it, and his mouth went thin. Then he turned it over to the plain brass back and held out his hand to his son for the knife.

He wrote slowly, leaning on the elders' bench with his whole weight in the point of the blade. Then he took four brass screws out of his own waistcoat pocket (he'd been carrying them, I think, since the night the bell rang) and put the plaque up with the old words turned to the wall, where they belong.

BRAM HALE. CHOSE.

Bram didn't say anything. He went red to the gill-slits, which slammed shut, and Tilly, on Wenna's lap, shouted "THAT'S MY BROTHER," at people who already knew.

Toby Marrow put the knife back in his son's hand, and went out of the meeting house without a word, and a minute later, the bell.

* * *

Hask had been very still in the doorway the whole time. When the bell started, I looked over, and his top stone was tipped down toward the joint between his seventh and eighth stones, where the bud sat among the four leaves, a small hard green fist.

"Hask?"

"I had thought it might," he said. "With the bell." A long, gravelly pause. "It has not."

"Is that a discrepancy?"

"No," said Hask. "It is a bud." He considered it. "I find I am content to wait. That is also new."

* * *

It was Director Penhallow who stopped the guide.

The afternoon tour started at three, as it always had, because nobody had told it otherwise, and as the village came out of the meeting house into the wind, high in the ribs over the square, the speakers clicked and hissed, and the warm dead voice of a woman who had sold the rights two hundred years ago began, sweetly, to the whole of Firstfield:

"Here we see the Baseline Humans at their daily tasks. Note the absence of any modification. Note the single set of l—"

It stopped.

Not a stall. Not a flicker. Gone, mid-word, the way a candle goes when you pinch it.

Penhallow was standing by the meeting-house steps with a small brass key in her hand, the kind that turns a panel off, that she must have carried on her ring every day of her directorship. She looked at it as if she'd never seen it before.

"Regulation three hundred and six of the Heritage Acts," she said, to nobody, in a voice that was not her visiting voice at all. "The director may suspend the interpretive narration during works." She put the key back in her pocket. "There are going to be a great many works."

I liked her, then, for about four seconds, which I'll admit is four more than I'd planned.

Halloran wrote the works down by tea-time on a single page, which he gave to Dr. Ommery and not to the director. The Preserve reclassified: a museum still, with WE KEEP WHAT WE WERE SO WE KNOW WHAT WE ARE in the stone and the Wall of the Eleven left where it was, and under it, cut new, an account of what retired had meant. A museum of its own wrongs, that people would pay to walk through, and should. The village stays, if it chooses. The skywalks stay, if the village chooses. The genome kept with consent, from the Library and not the arm. The draw-room on level nine closed and its chairs inventoried, which Pell-22 asked to do personally. And tank thirteen in the care of the keeper of Firstfield, until he was ready to be a baby, and then to whoever chose him.

"Ready," said Dr. Ommery, reading it. "Who decides when he's ready?"

"You do," said Halloran. "That's what care of means." Tock. "Don't make me write it down."

* * *

At dusk I went back to the far door.

Halloran was asleep in a chair on the Hales' step with his tin on his stomach and Biscuit asleep on the tin. The Pells had gone back to their skiffs in a grey line, filing as they went. Varro had walked out alone along the north walk an hour before and turned at the far end to look down at the square; Tilly had waved at him from the top of the apple tree, and he'd lifted one hand, just to the shoulder.

The gap at the end of Saul's rows was full of people.

Not going through. Standing in it. Jory with his toes on the line where the dome's floor stopped and his hands in his pockets. Wat from the mill. Mae Cotter and her mother, singing under their breath. Old Saul, watching the three flat mounds in his last bed as if they might do something rash. The wind came in through the gap and went down every lane in Firstfield, banging doors, rattling the tomato frames, carrying grass and dust and the dark green musk of the Spill. And very faintly, from the north, so far off you had to want to hear it, sky-grazers piping in the last of the light.

Bram stood beside me in his own face with his hands in my coat pockets. He'd taken the coat again. I'd let him.

He didn't say anything for a long time. Neither did I. I'd learned that from a man eating lunch.

Behind us the ribs began to tick as they cooled. Through the plain glass the first stars were coming out over Firstfield for the second night in its history, and nobody had gone indoors.

"Who's going?" said Tilly.

She was standing between us. I hadn't heard her come. She had hold of the back of the coat, and she was looking out through the gap at the dark hills, and the stars low over them, and the road.

"I don't know," I said. "That's rather the point."

Jory, at the line, took one hand out of his pocket. He looked at it. Then he looked back over his shoulder, straight at Bram, and something went across his face, a dare, the oldest one in Firstfield, that he didn't say.

He didn't have to. Bram had heard it a long time ago.

"What's out there?" said Tilly, to me. "Really."

I thought about a battered notebook in a schoolhouse cupboard, with columns of numbers in a careful hand, and on the last page a drawing of the dome from outside, the way nobody in there could have seen it, and an arrow pointing out into empty paper.

"More," I said.

Nobody went through. Not that night.

But nobody shut it, either.
