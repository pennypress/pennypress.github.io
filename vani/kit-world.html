Starter kit: the world

Spoilers ahead.

The setting in brief

Centuries from now, travel between worlds is easy, so distance is never the obstacle; permission, politics, cost and paperwork are. The galaxy is crowded with kinds of people, and humans are one voice among many. The book is set mostly on Moriel, a planet where genetic engineering went too far, and on Margin Station, a frontier station where the official record and reality disagree constantly.

These are our working rules. The book shows them at work and almost never states them as a list; you can state them, change them, or break them on purpose.

Founding rules of the universe

  • Travel is easy. Faster-than-light hops and folds are ordinary. Whoever controls the lanes, the permits and the tolls has the power.
  • Many kinds of people. Aliens are never one-dimensional: no species has a single fixed nature. Each has factions, dissenters and individuals who break type, and its strangeness comes from culture, biology and technology, never from sameness.
  • Every species tells stories, in its own form. Each tells them in a way very different from the others.
  • Every species has its own idea of "AI". There is no single galactic machine mind. Each people has its own answer to how it automates things, how it augments minds, how it pairs minds (as dyads), and how its feedback loops work.
  • Humanity's thing is forgiveness. Where other kinds keep long memories and settle old scores, humans can let a thing go, at a cost, and choose to do it again tomorrow.
  • Tone. Serious, thought-provoking ideas broken up by funny ones, and the funny ones used to explain. Hopeful at the core; never grim for its own sake.
  • The never-list. No cardboard: every place, species and institution needs specific texture (food, slang, smells, how people argue). Running gags need retirement dates. Nothing is repeated until it goes stale.
  • The respect line. A story a living tradition keeps oral, ceremonial or sacred is not retold. If in doubt, leave it out. Living traditions are never mocked, and no deity is costume-swapped for a joke.

Laws of the living world

Life on Moriel was engineered to be easy to edit, and that design choice is the root of everything else.

  1. Nothing is free. Every trait has a running cost in food, heat or time. Find what a creature is paying and you know what it needs.
  2. Every fence leaks. Designed things escape their design: seed blows, spores drift, edit-friendly life swaps traits.
  3. Life drifts without a reference. Easy-to-edit lines pick up changes every generation, like a copy of a copy, and wander off spec. Checking against an original pulls them back.
  4. Everything talks. Life signals by scent, sap and touch.
  5. Everything feeds something. Nothing lives alone; fix one thing and three others change.
  6. A gift both ways. The law over all the others: every place gives travellers something, and they give something back. Nothing in a living system only takes. The villains' way is extraction; the heroes' way is exchange.

The Register and the Inspectorate

The Register is the great record of what exists and who counts. The Inspectorate of Discrepancies handles the places where the Register and the world disagree. An Examiner recognises six ways a record can be wrong:

  1. Absent: recorded, but not there.
  2. Unrecorded: there, but not recorded.
  3. Drifted: there, but no longer what the record says.
  4. Misfiled: recorded as something else.
  5. Forged: made wrong on purpose.
  6. Lapsed: true once, no longer applies.

An Examiner's ruling changes legal existence. Registering something gives it standing; striking it takes standing away. An open discrepancy freezes action on everything entered as evidence in it: evidence under an open case may not be destroyed, altered or cleared.

The Third Schedule

Hask, a walking cairn of carved stones, may act only to prevent a harm on the Third Schedule: a list of 4,112 enumerated harms. Being shot at is listed; being shot is not. If it is not on the list, he may not act. He is a mind of rules carved in stone, with no weights to shift, which is why he can neither forgive nor forget.

The symbiont's grammar

Life is a language: genomes are text and chemistry is speech. The symbiont that lives in Bram can read both and write small changes into him, within limits that make it a story and not a superpower:

  1. It can only change what he can name. It needs his understanding to hold a shape, so learning the world is his power.
  2. Every change costs (hunger, cold, sleep), and none fully goes back.
  3. It needs his yes. It asks first ("Shall I fix it?"), and he may refuse.
  4. Roughly one new "word" per place he passes through.

Minds and dives

Two kinds of inner journeys appear at important points only. They are immersive and sensory, scientifically honest in shape, and they cost something:

  • Biological dives. Inside a cell, as a lit city; DNA as a library of four letters; proteins that fold like laundry.
  • The Reading. An old Inspectorate method (a method of loci) in which an Examiner builds a structure in her mind and walks it. The Register becomes a building: every kind of thing is a hall of drawers, every entry a row with a key, every pointer a corridor. The six discrepancies are failures she can see.

Rule for both: show, never lecture. The reader learns the structure by walking it.

Rules of craft this world was written under

  • The book is entertaining and many other things, but not a lecture. An explaining passage stays when a character wants something by it, or someone reacts, or it is ironic, or its voice is a pleasure in itself.
  • A system may solve problems for the author only as far as the reader understands it: set it up early and lightly, pay it off later.

Rules, as the book shows them

A gift both ways

Also called: the core law, exchange, not extraction.

Bram restarts the falling grazers with his own old fermenters, and the grazers carry him over the ridge.

  • Costs: Something from each side: Bram gives his baseline, his sweat, his breath; Odile gives a correct record and is marked by every biome.

An open case freezes its evidence

Also called: Evidence under an open case.

'SUSPENDED. Conflicts with open Discrepancy. Area contains evidence.' A whole valley, saved by being entered as evidence.

  • Costs: A formal Discrepancy goes to the Chief Examiner and stays on the Examiner's record; Odile has opened 'perhaps six' in eleven years.

Drift and the chassis

Also called: Law A3: life drifts without a reference, the drift, copies of copies.

A failing grain stalk read like a book: top pages intact, bottom pages worn like the meeting-house steps.

  • Costs: Calibration needs an untouched copy. The cheap path drew it from Firstfield's children until they were used up; the founders' path draws it from the Library.

Every fence leaks

Also called: Law A2, The fence is a suggestion.

Where the gold grain stops at a line as straight as a knife, the wild begins anyway.

  • Costs: Designed things escape their design: seed blows, spores drift, edit-friendly life swaps traits.

Everything talks; everything feeds something

Also called: Law A4, Law A5.

The grass says 'warm here'; the fur-trees talk about rain; the grass reads grazers as 'weather, sky, nothing'.

  • Costs: Fix one thing and three others change; and a body that hears it all can never switch it off (root-listening, scent-speech).

Nothing is free

Also called: Law A1, Nothing floats for free.

Sky-grazers pay for their lift by fermenting seed into gas, so they must graze constantly.

  • Costs: Every trait has a running cost in food, heat or time; every change to Bram costs hunger, cold or sleep, and none of it fully goes back.

One reader, one Librarian

Also called: a Librarian sleeps without a partner.

The Pale's Librarian tastes Bram and lets go: 'taken'.

  • Costs: A Librarian needs a willing partner who can name things; a reader becomes, in the company's eyes, 'materials' (a living reference).

The Choosing

Also called: Article Fourteen, CHOOSING, CHOOSING: EXERCISED, Rumspringa clause.

'And at the coming of age, each shall choose: to keep, or to go and see. And the choosing shall be theirs.'

  • Costs: Leaving costs a family (Bram's leaving is a real loss to a loving village); keeping must be chosen, or it is captivity.

The Reading

Also called: Fallow's method.

Leave by the door you came in, or you'll leave by a window.

  • Costs: Hours that feel like minutes; she comes out cold, stiff and starving, with a headache 'behind the eyes like a drawer slammed shut'. The Manual warns against doing it on yourself: 'no drawers there, only weather.'

The Register's discrepancy classes

Also called: Layer B, the six ways a record can be wrong.

'Population: none' is a value and a lie; Lenna's empty 'Died' slot is an honest empty drawer.

  • Costs: Each class is a harm someone pays for: an unrecorded valley can be burned, a misfiled network blasted, a forged index starves the plains, a retired child has no protection.

The Third Schedule

Also called: Hask's mandate, the Schedule, Appendix C.

Being shot AT is listed; being shot is not.

  • Costs: Hask may act only to prevent an enumerated harm to the bearer; everything else is a gap he must decide about, and measurement first ('an unmeasured beast is weather').

The symbiont can only change what is named

Also called: Layer C, Name it and you can hold it, the symbiont's grammar.

He names his leg from Dr. Ommery's chart of the flayed man, the one Jory drew a moustache on.
