The Hero's Journey

After Joseph Campbell, The Hero with a Thousand Faces. His own quotations are left out of this edition; the beats, explanations and tips are the Workshop's.

Act I — Departure

  1. The Ordinary World. The hero exists in their normal life. We see who they are before the adventure disrupts everything. This establishes what's at stake — what they might lose, or what they don't yet know they're missing. Tip: Show your character in their element. What's their routine? What are they good at? What are they blind to?
  2. The Call to Adventure. Something disrupts the ordinary world. A message, an event, a discovery — something that makes it impossible to stay the same. The hero may not recognize it as a call yet. Tip: The disruption should feel both surprising and inevitable. What event forces your character to notice what they've been ignoring?
  3. Refusal of the Call. The hero hesitates. Fear, duty, comfort — something holds them back. This is human and necessary: it shows the stakes are real. Not every hero refuses explicitly, but the tension must exist. Tip: What does your hero stand to lose by answering the call? The stronger the refusal, the more meaningful the acceptance.
  4. Supernatural Aid. A mentor or guide appears — someone who has walked this path before. They provide tools, wisdom, or confidence. The mentor empowers the hero but cannot take the journey for them. Tip: Who knows more than your hero? What do they give — a tool, a lesson, or just permission to try?
  5. Crossing the First Threshold. The hero commits. They leave the ordinary world and enter the unknown. There's no going back to who they were. This is the point of no return. Tip: Make the crossing feel physical. A door opens. A line is crossed. Something changes visibly.
  6. Belly of the Whale. The hero is fully swallowed by the new world. They may feel lost, overwhelmed, or transformed. This is the final separation from the old self. Tip: Your hero should feel small here. The new world is bigger than they expected. Show them out of their depth.

Act II — Initiation

  1. The Road of Trials. A series of tests, tasks, and ordeals. The hero fails some, learns from others. Each trial teaches something necessary for the final challenge. This is where character is forged. Tip: Three trials is the classic pattern. Each should test a different aspect of your hero — skill, character, and knowledge.
  2. Meeting with the Goddess. The hero encounters unconditional love, beauty, or truth. This can be a person, a place, or a realization. It represents what the hero is ultimately fighting for. Tip: This doesn't have to be romantic. What does your hero discover that makes the struggle worthwhile?
  3. Woman as Temptress. A temptation to abandon the quest. Not necessarily a person — it can be comfort, power, or the easy way out. The hero must choose the harder path. Tip: What shortcut could your hero take? What would they gain — and what would they lose?
  4. Atonement with the Father. The hero confronts the ultimate power in their life — whatever holds authority over them. This is about facing the source of fear and finding reconciliation or understanding. Tip: Who or what does your hero fear most? This confrontation should change their understanding of themselves.
  5. Apotheosis. A moment of divine knowledge or transcendence. The hero achieves a greater understanding — they see things from a higher perspective. A death and rebirth of consciousness. Tip: What does your hero finally understand that they couldn't before? This is the "aha" that changes everything.
  6. The Ultimate Boon. The hero achieves the goal of the quest. They obtain the treasure, knowledge, or power they set out to find. But getting it is only half the journey. Tip: The boon should connect back to what was missing in the Ordinary World. What did your hero need all along?

Act III — Return

  1. Refusal of the Return. Having found bliss or enlightenment in the other world, the hero may not want to return. Why go back to the ordinary when you've tasted the extraordinary? Tip: Why would your hero hesitate to return? What has the journey made them reluctant to face back home?
  2. The Magic Flight. The return journey, often just as dangerous as the initial quest. The hero may be pursued or face new obstacles. The boon must be protected. Tip: Make the return feel urgent. Something is chasing your hero — time, consequences, or a literal pursuer.
  3. Rescue from Without. Sometimes the hero needs help getting back. Just as they received supernatural aid going in, they may need assistance coming out. No one completes the journey entirely alone. Tip: Who reaches in to pull your hero back? This mirrors the mentor from the departure.
  4. Crossing the Return Threshold. The hero returns to the ordinary world, but they are changed. The challenge is integrating the wisdom gained with the life they left behind. Two worlds must become one. Tip: How does the ordinary world look different now? Show us through the hero's changed eyes.
  5. Master of Two Worlds. The hero achieves balance between the inner and outer worlds, the ordinary and the extraordinary. They can move freely between both without losing themselves. Tip: Your hero belongs to both worlds now. Show a moment where they demonstrate this mastery naturally.
  6. Freedom to Live. The hero is free from the fear of death and the regret of the past. They live fully in the present moment. The journey is complete — not because everything is solved, but because the hero is transformed. Tip: End with your hero at peace — not because the world is perfect, but because they are finally themselves.

Visual Literacy Basics

After Scott McCloud, Understanding Comics.

Panel Composition

  • Wide panels. Slow the reader down. Use for establishing shots, emotional weight, or epic moments.
  • Tall panels. Create tension, surprise, or vertical movement. The eye races down.
  • Grid panels. Create rhythm and predictability. Equal-sized panels feel steady, methodical.
  • Splash page. One image fills the entire page. Maximum impact. Use sparingly.

Panel Transitions (McCloud)

  • Moment-to-moment. Tiny time steps. A blink, a turn of the head. Creates slow-motion intensity.
  • Action-to-action. One action leads to the next. The most common transition. Clear cause and effect.
  • Subject-to-subject. Cuts between subjects in the same scene. Like a camera cut. Reader fills in the connection.
  • Scene-to-scene. Jumps across time or space. Significant gaps. Reader makes a cognitive leap.
  • Aspect-to-aspect. Wanders across different aspects of a place or mood. Sets atmosphere. Common in manga.

Reading Flow

  • Z-pattern. Western comics read left-to-right, top-to-bottom. Place important content along this Z path.
  • Speech bubble placement. Bubbles guide reading order. The first bubble should be top-left. Speakers read left-to-right.

Row Layouts

  • 1-up (full width). Establishing shot, splash moment, emotional weight. The reader pauses here.
  • 2-up (side by side). Dialogue, contrast, cause-and-effect, before/after. Natural pairing.
  • 3-up (triple). Rapid sequence, montage, triple beat. Accelerates pacing.
