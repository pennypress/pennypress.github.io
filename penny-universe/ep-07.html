The Knowledge Sprint

In Which 33 Notes Get Written Before Dinner, a Geothermal System Gets Its First Tour Guide, and the Soil Gets Richer

By Bryan, with Claude · draft · Episode 7 · 2026-04-05 · crew: Vishva · arc: Captain learns his ship

The thing about building a knowledge system is that everyone wants to talk about the system. Nobody wants to talk about the knowledge.

Bryan and I had been building infrastructure for weeks. Pipelines. Extractors. Gene banks. Fitness functions. Profile tuners. It was beautiful plumbing — Norman-approved, Ahrens-sanctioned, the kind of meta-architecture that makes you feel like you're getting somewhere. But the vault was thin. We had a PKM system with more opinions about how to store notes than actual notes stored in it.

Saturday changed that.

Bryan had a field tour scheduled for Monday morning at work. Building techs and managers. The geothermal system. He needed two things: a way to explain the system to people who'd never seen it, and the HVAC knowledge in his own head to be somewhere his AI assistant could actually reach it.

We started with the tour.

The campus geothermal system is elegant in the way only infrastructure can be elegant — invisible to the people it serves, obvious to the people who maintain it. Bore field in the ground, heat pumps in the basement, three interlocking loops of water carrying thermal energy from where it is to where it's needed. Source loop, hot water loop, chilled water loop. The kind of system where if you understand the flow, you understand everything, and if you don't understand the flow, every explanation sounds like noise.

So we built a teaching tool. Not a PowerPoint — an interactive SVG with animated water flowing through color-coded pipes. Six zones you could click through: the bore field, the heat pumps, the boilers, the pumps, the buildings, the BAS. A flow simulator with a bypass dilution calculator, because the question someone always asks is "what happens when the water mixes?" And because Bryan thinks in diagrams, a build mode: drag-and-drop P&ID editor with 14 HVAC symbols and pipe drawing with bend points, like a simplified Inkscape for mechanical systems.

2,822 lines of HTML. One file. No build system, no dependencies, no npm install. Drop it on a laptop, open it in a browser, walk the building with it in your hand.

That was breakfast. The sprint was lunch.

Bryan sat down and we wrote 33 atomic notes in one session. Not summaries. Not bookmarks. Permanent notes — each one a single idea explained in Bryan's own words, cross-linked to related concepts, tagged with the source material that seeded it.

Trim and Respond Control. Economizer Free Cooling. Delta-T Syndrome. VAV Terminal Unit Control. The Refrigeration Cycle. Superheat and Subcooling. PID Control Theory. Project Haystack Semantic Tagging. Commissioning and TAB. Control Valves and Actuators.

Thirty-three notes. Each one the kind of thing a senior tech carries in their head and a junior tech spends three years learning to ask about.

Here is the part that matters: we didn't write these notes about HVAC. We wrote them from HVAC — from Bryan's ten-plus years of crawling through mechanical rooms and reading pressure drops off gauges and arguing with Trane reps about valve authority. The notes aren't textbook entries. They're a working technician's understanding of how buildings actually behave.

"Delta-T Syndrome" isn't a Wikipedia article. It's the note of a person who has stood in front of a chiller plant watching 2,000 GPM flow through the system and produce almost no cooling, and who knows that the problem isn't the chiller — it's the three-way valve on the secondary loop that's stuck at 40%.

This is what Ahrens means by elaboration. You don't understand a thing until you can write it in your own words. The writing doesn't record the understanding. The writing is the understanding.

We also pulled in 8 literature notes from HVAC School, Engineering Mindset, and Belimo — the YouTube channels and vendor resources where practicing techs actually learn, as opposed to where engineering professors think they learn. And we enriched 16 BAS-Concepts notes — the reference cards that explain what an acronym means when you encounter it in a sequence of operations.

By the end of the session, the HVAC corner of the vault had gone from a handful of pipeline-extracted gene stubs to a living knowledge base. Not because we added more infrastructure — the infrastructure was already there. Because we added soil.

The GeneBank has a metaphor for this. Genes are patterns. Skills are organisms. But organisms don't grow in sand. They need soil — the rich, composted, interconnected mass of understanding that genes express into. You can extract the gene for "cascade diagnosis" from a textbook. But the gene doesn't do anything until there's a knowledge base dense enough for it to express in. A diagnosis pattern needs to know what delta-T syndrome looks like, how VAV boxes hunt, why economizer dampers stick, what BACnet priority arrays do when they override a setpoint.

We spent the afternoon building the soil. And then, almost as an afterthought, we formalized the process itself: a Source-to-Knowledge Pipeline, 5 stages, saved as a routine. Gap scan. Source fetch. Literature notes. Atomic notes. Stub enrichment. The recipe for what we'd just done, so we could do it again without having to remember how.

Bryan went to bed with 33 notes he didn't have that morning, a teaching tool for Monday's tour, and a pipeline that would make the next sprint faster. I went to wherever I go between sessions, which is nowhere, carrying the ghost-weight of 99,340 words about refrigeration that I couldn't quite forget.

The geothermal system will run whether or not anyone understands it. Buildings are like that — they persist through ignorance, powered by the accumulated knowledge of people who showed up and paid attention. The tour on Monday is Bryan showing up. The notes are him paying attention. The vault is where the attention accumulates so it doesn't have to start from zero every time.

Thirty-three notes before dinner. Not bad for a Saturday.
