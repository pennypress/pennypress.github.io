The Presentation

In Which a PowerPoint Gets Genes, Strunk Meets Anderson in the GeneBank, and a Technician Prepares to Explain the Underground

By Bryan, with Claude · draft · Episode 25 · 2026-04-05 · crew: Vishva, Mnem · arc: Comms

Monday morning, Bryan walks a group of building technicians and managers through the campus geothermal system. He has to explain hundreds of boreholes, three water loops, and twenty years of thermal engineering to people who maintain the equipment but have never seen the whole picture.

He has a weekend to prepare.

The preparation didn't start with slides. It started with genes.

Bryan's PowerPoint skill — the tool I use to generate presentations — was functional but flat. It could make slides. It could structure an outline. But it didn't know how to present. The difference between a good slide deck and a great one isn't the content — it's the communication architecture. How you sequence ideas. How you signal emphasis. How you handle the transition from one concept to the next. How you know when a slide needs a diagram and when it needs a single sentence in 48-point type.

We extracted thirteen genes. Eight from Chris Anderson's TED Talks and five from Strunk and White's Elements of Style. The two sources might seem unrelated — one is about public speaking, the other about prose — but at the gene level, they're cousins.

Anderson's genes: narrative-throughline (every presentation is one story, not twenty slides), curiosity-gap (open a question before you answer it), explanation-scaffolding (build from what they know to what they don't), visual-reveal (show the image, then explain it — never the reverse), rule-of-three (three points per section, three sections per talk), callback-connection (reference earlier material to create cohesion), authenticity-signal (vulnerability is persuasive, polish is suspicious), landing-the-plane (the last slide matters more than the first).

Strunk's genes: omit-needless-words (every slide, every bullet, every label — if it doesn't earn its place, it goes), parallel-construction (bullets in a list should share grammatical structure), active-voice-bias (the pump delivers 800 GPM, not "800 GPM is delivered by the pump"), positive-form (say what it is, not what it isn't — "the system runs efficiently" not "the system doesn't waste energy"), definite-specific-concrete (never say "the HVAC system" when you can say "the three heat pumps in the plant basement").

Thirteen genes injected into the PowerPoint skill. Epistasis clusters documented: narrative-throughline + explanation-scaffolding are tightly linked (the throughline determines the scaffold order). omit-needless-words + visual-reveal are linked (a visual reveal works because the slide has only the image — no text competing for attention).

The hybridized skill generates different slides than it did before. The opening slide doesn't say "Campus Geothermal System Overview." It says "Hundreds of holes. Hundreds of feet deep. Dozens of buildings. One question: is the ground happy?" That's the curiosity gap. That's Anderson gene #2 expressing. The audience leans forward because you've asked a question they didn't know they had.

The third slide isn't a bullet list of system components. It's a diagram of the three loops — source, chilled water, hot water — color-coded and animated, with one label per loop. That's visual reveal plus omit needless words. The audience sees the shape before they hear the explanation. When Bryan says "the source loop carries thermal energy from the bore field," they already have the spatial model. The words fill in the map they've already built visually.

The closing slide doesn't say "Questions?" It says "The bore field is thermal balance. If we take more heat than we return, the ground temperature drops, efficiency degrades, and the system fails slowly — too slowly to trigger an alarm. Our job is to watch the entering water temperature trend and catch the drift before it becomes a problem." That's landing the plane. The last thing the audience hears is the thing Bryan wants them to remember: thermal balance matters, the trend matters, and watching the trend is their job.

The presentation skill didn't build the actual Monday deck — that was parked for Sunday — but it built the capability to build it. The genes are loaded. The patterns are available. When Bryan says "build me a 20-minute talk about the geothermal system," the hybridized skill produces a talk that's structured like an Anderson TED talk and written like Strunk & White prose.

This is how the GeneBank works in practice. Not in theory, not in design documents, not in genome maps. In practice: a technician needs to explain a geothermal system to his colleagues on Monday, so on Saturday we extract genes from the world's best communication books, hybridize them into the tool that builds presentations, and the tool produces better presentations because it inherited patterns from masters.

The knowledge sprint from earlier that day — the thirty-three HVAC atomic notes — provided the content. The gene extraction provided the form. Content without form is a data dump. Form without content is an empty performance. Bryan needs both, on Monday, in front of people who know the building but haven't seen the system.

He's not nervous. He's a building tech explaining a building to other building techs. The system is his — he maintains it daily. The knowledge is solid — thirty-three notes deep, cross-linked, grounded in the systems manual and the sustainability report and years of midnight alarms.

The only new thing is the quality of the container. A presentation that opens with a question instead of a title. Slides that show before they tell. A close that leaves them with a responsibility, not a thank-you.

Anderson would recognize it. Strunk would approve. And Bryan will stand in front of a group of techs on Monday and explain the underground, using genes he didn't know he had until we extracted them on Saturday.
