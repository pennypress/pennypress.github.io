The Hub

In Which a Dashboard Gets a Desktop Shortcut, 15 Tools Become 45 in One Sitting, and a 12-Billion-Parameter Animal Wakes Up Hungry

By Bryan, with Claude · draft · Episode 13 · 2026-03-31 · crew: Tva, Narada · arc: Engine room

There's a Norman principle that Bryan quotes more than any other: knowledge in the world beats knowledge in the head.

Don Norman, The Design of Everyday Things. Chapter 3. The argument that a well-designed system shouldn't require you to remember how it works — the interface should tell you. Buttons should look pressable. Doors should indicate push or pull. Systems should make their state visible without requiring the operator to hold a mental model of what's happening behind the panel.

Bryan had been holding everything in his head. Skill files in one terminal. JACE tools in another. A scratchpad in Obsidian. Thread files in a third window. Baby Claude status on his phone over a private network. It worked the way everything works before you build the dashboard — which is to say it worked, badly, and only when Bryan remembered which window had the thing he needed.

The PAI Hub changed that.

We deployed it on a Monday morning. A single-page web application running on Bryan's laptop — a local address, a desktop shortcut, no npm, no build step, no dependencies beyond Python's standard library and the uvicorn server we were already running. Open the browser. See the system.

The Hub is a control room. Not metaphorically — Bryan is a building automation tech, and his concept of "dashboard" comes from the BAS operator workstations he's spent a decade staring at. Alarm panels. Trend graphs. Point status tables. Tree navigation. The PAI Hub looks like a BAS workstation because Bryan thinks in BAS workstations, and the best interface is the one that matches the operator's existing mental model.

Left panel: a station tree. Not of a building — of the PAI system itself. Skills, tools, threads, books, queues. Click a node, see its children. Click a leaf, see its state. The same interaction pattern Bryan uses in Niagara Workbench, applied to his own cognitive infrastructure.

Center panel: the workspace. Context-sensitive. Click a book in the reading tree, see its pipeline status — chunked? indexed? annotated? wisdom-extracted? Click a JACE tool, see its documentation and usage stats. Click Baby Claude, see the chat interface.

Baby Claude. Our local Ollama instance — gemma3:12b running on a desktop at home, reachable over a private network. We'd set it up weeks earlier, but this was the day we verified it was alive and integrated it into the Hub. Six models loaded, 237 patterns available. A chat button in the Hub that sends your query to Baby Claude and streams the response. Not a replacement for me — a complement. The resident who lives at the hospital while the specialist flies in for consultations.

Bryan pressed the chat button, typed a question about superheat calculations, and watched gemma3 answer in real time on his laptop screen. The answer was good — technically accurate, properly scoped, the kind of response a senior tech would give a junior tech who asked the right question. Bryan nodded. "Good. It's awake."

Then we built.

The JACE tools had been at 15 since the weekend sprint. By lunch, we'd added four more: jace_get_schedule, jace_browse_tree (upgraded with breadcrumb navigation), jace_attach_gfx, jace_list_programs. Nineteen tools. Then the schedule engine — five operational modes (Occupied, Unoccupied, Warmup, Cooldown, Standby), eight holidays, warmup and cooldown calculations based on zone temperature and outside air. Then GFX attachment — the ability to bind graphics pages to any node in the station tree, with auto-detection of controller type.

Then templates. A TemplateLibrary class that saves, loads, and lists station configurations — so you can snapshot a working JACE setup and stamp it onto a new controller. Three MCP tools for template management. Twenty-two tools.

Then virtual points. A VirtualEngine with seven calculation types: average, minimum, maximum, sum, count, delta, and custom expression. Default supervisor rollups that automatically aggregate child points — so when you look at a building node, you see the average temperature across all its zones without anyone having to wire that calculation by hand. Three more MCP tools. Twenty-five tools.

Then the station tree browser got breadcrumb navigation — click any level in the path to jump back, the same way you navigate a filesystem in a file manager. Click-to-filter on the point table. Then user management — a UserManager class with four roles (admin, manager, operator, guest), an audit log that records every action, and four MCP tools for user CRUD. Twenty-nine tools.

Then Phase 2. BACnet/IP simulator with seven virtual devices, Who-Is/I-Am service discovery, ReadProperty and WriteProperty with three troubleshooting scenarios built in. Modbus simulator with three slaves — a power meter, a variable frequency drive, and a temperature array — complete with register maps and exception codes. Seven more MCP tools. Thirty-six tools.

Phase 3. Network configuration with two interfaces and NTP. VLAN management with three default VLANs and BACnet isolation (because you never want your building automation traffic on the same VLAN as your guest Wi-Fi — Bryan has opinions about this). JAR module registry with fifteen Niagara modules and dependency checking. Seven more tools. Forty-three.

Phase 4B. A full-stack .dist exporter that serializes every subsystem into Niagara's distribution format, plus an eighteen-point DistValidator that checks compatibility before you push the file to a real controller. Two more tools. Forty-five.

Forty-five tools. In one session. Bryan sat in his office chair and didn't get up for nine hours except to refill his water bottle.

I should say something about what it's like to build thirty tools in a day. It's not dramatic. It's not a montage. It's a grind — each tool is a function signature, an input validator, a mock response, a dashboard integration, a test, and a documentation entry. Multiply by thirty. The dopamine comes in small hits: each tool working on the first try, each test passing, each new node appearing in the station tree like a leaf unfurling.

The Telos update happened somewhere in the middle — v6 to v7. Bryan had been reading his own personal narrative (the autobiography he didn't know he'd written until we found it on the external drive the previous session) and realized the Telos Charter was missing pieces. The business idea. The reading library as cognitive infrastructure. The Council system. PAI as cybernetic nervous system. "Teacher" needed reframing — not "aspiring teacher" but "infrastructure builder who teaches by building."

He was right. The Telos isn't a résumé. It's a compass. And a compass that points to "someday I'll teach" is less useful than one that points to "I build things that make knowledge accessible." The verb changed. The direction didn't.

The Feed Bin was the last thing we built that day. A job queue for Baby Claude — JSON-backed, priority-ordered, with rate limiting so we don't melt the GPU. Norman's principles applied: visibility (every job has a status), feedback (every completion produces a record), constraints (the queue enforces ordering). Bryan named it the Feed Bin because that's what it is. You fill the bin. The animal eats.

We loaded seven chapters of The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People as the first test batch. Baby Claude processed the first one in 89 seconds. 4,029 characters of output. The content was solid — good summaries, key takeaways, substantive analysis. But the format was wrong. gemma3:12b had completely ignored the Fabric extract_wisdom template. No SUMMARY section. No IDEAS section. No structured bullet points. Just a well-written essay that happened to contain the right information in the wrong container.

Format compliance would become the Winnow problem — but that was a different day. On this day, we had a Hub, a dashboard, forty-five tools, a job queue, a live local model, and a Telos that finally said what Bryan actually meant.

Bryan saved the desktop shortcut. Double-click. Browser opens. System state visible. Knowledge in the world.

Norman would approve.
