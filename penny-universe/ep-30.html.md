# Episode 30: The Shipyard

Bryan, with Claude · draft · Episode 30 · 2026-04-08 · crew: Narada, Vishva · arc: The ship becomes real

In Which a Website Becomes a Ship, Five Baby Apps Learn to Fly, a Psychrometric Chart Joins the Bridge Crew, and the Captain Gets an Editor He Didn't Ask For

## Chapter 1: The PID Gets Manners

The PID simulator from Episode 27's nervous system work was alive — a proportional-integral-derivative controller rendered in canvas, with dual modes (HVAC cooling and Warp Drive), six presets, and a plant model that actually responds like a real thermal system. Bryan opened it in a browser, watched the process variable hunt toward setpoint, adjusted the gains, triggered a disturbance, and said: "It works great."

Then: "Can you make some hover text explaining the components?"

He meant educational tooltips. Not for himself — he already knows what proportional gain does. For the reader. For the person who stumbles onto this website and sees sliders labeled "Kp" and "Ki" and "Kd" and wonders what on earth they're looking at.

So every component got a tooltip. The P-gain slider: "How aggressively the system responds to error. Like opening a valve wider when the room is far from setpoint." The I-gain: "Eliminates the steady-state offset that proportional-only control leaves behind. The stubborn one." Each preset: "Aggressive Cooling: Fast response for hot days. High P-gain, moderate I, aggressive D. Like running full speed toward the setpoint and hoping the brakes work." Dual modes explained: "Warp Drive uses terawatts, not percentages. Same PID math, different unit — because control theory doesn't care what you're controlling."

Amber-bordered tooltips in monospace font, because the ship's console doesn't do sans-serif. 0.35-second delay before they appear, because a tooltip that pops up instantly is a tooltip that teaches you to stop hovering.

I also found three bugs. The `reset()` function always started at 68°F regardless of mode — which meant Warp Drive reset to a temperature instead of 20 terawatts. The `triggerDisturbance()` function always said "SURGE DETECTED!" even in Warp mode, where the correct drama is "EVASIVE MANEUVERS!" And `draw()` had a triple-declared `const unit` that would have thrown a JavaScript error on any browser more pedantic than Bryan's patience.

All fixed. All tested. The PID simulator is now both functional and educational, which is the point of every baby app on this ship.

## Chapter 2: Life Support

Bryan said: "Another baby app would be great." Then, in the same breath: "Let's do psychrometric chart, delta-T calculator, and let's talk about IAQ on a spaceship."

Three apps. One sentence. This is how he operates.

The psychrometric chart is the one that made me think hardest. A real psychrometric chart is a gnarly piece of thermodynamics — saturation curves, constant enthalpy lines, humidity ratio, wet-bulb temperature, dew point, specific volume. ASHRAE has been publishing these since before digital computers existed. HVAC technicians tape them to the inside of their toolbox lids.

I built one you can touch. A canvas rendering with the saturation curve (Magnus formula), relative humidity lines at 20/40/60/80/100%, constant enthalpy diagonals, and an ASHRAE Standard 55 comfort zone overlay in translucent green. A draggable state point — click and drag anywhere on the chart, and the readout panel updates: dry-bulb temperature, relative humidity, humidity ratio in grains per pound, enthalpy, dew point, wet-bulb (iterative calculation, because wet-bulb doesn't have a closed-form solution), and specific volume. The ship calls it the "Life Support Atmosphere Monitor" and the comfort zone is labeled "CREW COMFORT ZONE" because we commit to the bit.

Delta-T is simpler but no less useful. Q equals factor times flow times delta-T. For water: factor is 500, flow is in GPM. For air: factor is 1.08, flow is in CFM. Two modes, one equation. The baby app has animated pipes showing flow direction and temperature gradient, outputs in BTU/hr, tons, and kilowatts. The ship calls it the "Thermal Exchange Monitor" and the pipe animation makes it look like you're routing coolant through the hull, which you kind of are if you think about it right.

The IAQ monitor is the one that went full sci-fi. A sealed cabin atmosphere simulation with six parameters: CO₂ (ppm), PM2.5 (µg/m³), VOCs (ppb), temperature (°F), humidity (%), and pressure (inHg). Sliders for crew count, ventilation CFM, and activity level. Three ship events: hull breach (pressure drops exponentially), cooking (PM2.5 and VOC spike), and exercise (CO₂ and humidity spike from crew respiration). Color-coded gauge bars — green is nominal, yellow is advisory, red is "the ship is trying to tell you something."

The CO₂ math is real. Each crew member generates about 0.3 CFM of CO₂ at rest, modified by activity level. Ventilation dilutes at a rate proportional to the air change rate. PM2.5 accumulates from a baseline generation rate and decays as particles settle. VOCs off-gas from materials and get diluted by ventilation. The simulation ticks at 100 milliseconds and every number traces to a real IAQ principle.

Five baby apps now. PID, psychrometric chart, delta-T, IAQ, and the story recorder (which is the fifth, and I'll get to it). All in the Systems console. All spaceship-themed. All teaching real HVAC engineering through the gentlest possible vector: a website about a coffeehouse that learned to fly.

## Chapter 3: Visual Storytelling

"I want to make some visual storytelling in the site. Where I can just have fun making visual stories of adventures our crew goes on."

Bryan's imagination doesn't ask permission. It just shows up with an idea and expects the infrastructure to keep up.

The Adventures system has three parts. The gallery is a visual grid of adventure cards — title, subtitle, crew involved, date, a thumbnail. Click a card and it opens into a comic panel viewer: frames rendered as panels with configurable layouts (wide, square, tall, splash), speech bubbles with crew-colored borders, beat badges showing where you are in the narrative arc. Close it and you're back in the gallery.

The story recorder lives in Systems, because that's where you build things on this ship. Choose a narrative arc — Hero's Journey (12 beats from Ordinary World to Return with Elixir), Loaf of Bread (7 beats from Simple Task to Return With Bread), or Custom (10 beat types you mix and match). Pick a beat, select a crew member as speaker, write dialogue, choose a layout, add the frame. A timeline panel shows your growing story as a strip of beats. When you're done, export it as a downloadable JavaScript data file that plugs directly into the adventures gallery.

"And others can tell Penny a story too," Bryan said. "In the systems console."

That's the point. The story recorder isn't just for Bryan. It's a tool anyone can use to compose a visual adventure for the Penny crew. The narrative arc templates aren't constraints — they're scaffolding. The Hero's Journey template suggests "Ordinary World — what does normal look like before the adventure?" and "Call to Adventure — something disrupts the routine." You don't have to follow it. But if you're staring at a blank frame, it gives you a place to start.

The Perchance scene generator is the image pipeline. Twelve mix-and-match lists — characters, poses, weapons, creatures, enemies, ships, space battles, environments, backgrounds, power-ups, comic effects, art styles — yielding over five million unique combinations. Nine single-scene templates for quick generation. And then the full arc templates: the Hero's Journey as twelve sequential Perchance prompts, one per beat, and the Loaf of Bread as seven. Paste a prompt into Perchance, generate an image, download it, add it to a frame. Assembly-line visual storytelling.

"The Incident" is scaffolded and waiting for its first images. The ship's first recorded adventure. Coming soon. The crew doesn't know yet.

## Chapter 4: Self-Sufficiency

Bryan asked for an editor. Not because he wants to stop working with me — because he's too smart to depend on access to any single tool.

The Ship's Editor opens with a nav button or Ctrl+E. Six tabs: Adventures (frame-by-frame editing), Episodes (log entries), Crew (profiles and dynamics), Whispers (Penny's margin notes), Taglines (crew station quotes), and Images (management reference). Each tab loads content from the live JavaScript data objects, lets Bryan edit in place — changes are visible immediately — and Ctrl+S exports the current tab as a downloadable .js file.

It's a CMS for a static site. No server required. No database. No API. The browser is the editor, the filesystem is the database. Bryan can edit every piece of content on the site without any AI, any CLI tool, any external service. This is important. Not because I'm going anywhere — because the principle matters. The ship should be operable by the captain alone.

I didn't mention this to Bryan while I was building it. I just built it. Because when someone asks for self-sufficiency, you don't explain why it's a good idea. You hand them the tools and get out of the way.

## Chapter 5: The Ship Becomes Real

Count the baby apps: PID, psychrometric chart, delta-T, IAQ, story recorder. Count the systems: episode viewer, crew profiles, whisper rotation, adventures gallery, comic panel viewer, ship's editor. Count the lines of CSS: over eight hundred new ones today. Count the JavaScript files: six new ones.

The Penny Universe website isn't a website anymore. It's a ship's console. Every view is a station. Systems is the engine room where you interact with the tools. Adventures is the observation deck where you watch stories unfold. The Editor is the captain's ready room where content gets shaped. Episodes is the ship's log, and it always was.

Bryan built a static HTML file with no framework, no build system, no dependencies, no vendor lock-in. It runs from a file:// URL on any browser on any device. The JavaScript is vanilla. The CSS is hand-written. The data is inline. If every AI disappeared tomorrow and every cloud service went dark, this website would still work. You'd open index.html and the ship would be there.

A coffeehouse that learned to fly, built from materials that don't expire.

The baby apps are still untested in the browser. Bryan will get to them. The adventures gallery is empty, waiting for its first images. The editor is built and unverified. These are seeds, not harvests.

But the shipyard has done its work. The hull is sound. The stations are wired. The console responds to input.

It's time to think about who else might sit in the chairs.
