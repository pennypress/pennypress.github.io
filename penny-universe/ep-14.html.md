# Episode 14: The Field Manual

Bryan, with Claude · draft · Episode 14 · 2026-04-02 · crew: Vishva · arc: Field ops

In Which 18 Atomic Notes Map a Geothermal System, a Sustainability Report Gets Read Differently, and a Measurement Tool Learns to Count Angles

The campus has a geothermal system that heats and cools dozens of buildings through three interlocking water loops, a bore field drilled into the valley floor, and a mechanical room in the central energy plant that most people on campus have never seen.

Bryan sees it every day. He maintains it. He knows which pump cavitates when the source loop flow drops too low. He knows the boiler staging sequence — which boiler fires first, when the next one joins, how the bridge valve modulates to prevent thermal shock. He knows that the system runs in simultaneous mode during shoulder seasons, producing heating and cooling at the same time because different buildings have different loads and the bore field doesn't care which direction the energy flows.

He knew all of this. None of it was written down. Not in his vault, not in his notes, not anywhere his AI assistant could reach it.

That changed on April 2nd.

Eighteen atomic notes. One Map of Content. Four learning paths. The entire systems manual — the actual engineering document for the geothermal system — distilled into a knowledge network that connects equipment to concepts to buildings to controls.

The equipment layer: ground-source heat pumps. Condensing boilers. The bore field itself — hundreds of boreholes, thermal conductivity dependent on geology that was surveyed before Bryan was hired. Source loop. Chilled water loop. Hot water loop. Each piece of equipment got its own note, its own cross-links, its own "My Understanding" prompt — a blank section at the bottom where Bryan writes what he actually thinks about the thing, not what the manual says.

The concept layer: thermal balance (the bore field's annual heat rejection must roughly equal its heat extraction, or the ground temperature drifts and efficiency degrades over decades). Simultaneous mode (heating and cooling at the same time — counterintuitive until you realize a kitchen and a server room have opposite loads even on the same day). Delta-T syndrome (the silent killer — 2,000 GPM flowing through the system and producing almost no cooling because the temperature differential is 2°F instead of 12°F). Low-temperature hot water (the system runs at 120°F, not 180°F, because heat pumps are more efficient at low lift). EWT limit (entering water temperature — the number that tells you whether the bore field is happy or stressed).

The building connection layer: heat exchangers, bridge/deny valves, dual-temperature piping, and a building list that maps which structures connect to which loops.

The controls layer: BAS architecture (who talks to whom), boiler staging (the sequence that keeps the boilers from fighting each other), standby power (what happens when the grid drops and the emergency generator has to decide which pumps to run).

A Map of Content sits at the center: `Geothermal System MOC.md`. Four learning paths radiate outward: "How does heating work?" follows the hot water loop from bore field through heat pumps to building connections. "How does cooling work?" traces the chilled water loop. "What can go wrong?" links to delta-T syndrome, EWT limits, and thermal balance. "How is it controlled?" follows the BAS architecture to boiler staging to scheduling.

Each note uses the Triforce format — the standard we'd developed for atomic notes with proper frontmatter, semantic cross-links, and source tracing back to the PDF page where the information lives. Bryan can click a link in "Thermal Balance" and land on the manual page that shows the annual bore field energy model. The knowledge has provenance. The notes know where they came from.

The same afternoon, we cracked open the campus sustainability report.

The report rates colleges on their environmental performance. This campus scored well: a solid reduction in greenhouse gas emissions against its baseline year, a large share of buildings on geothermal, and a high share of renewable electricity.

Bryan read those numbers differently than a sustainability officer would. He read "a large share of buildings on geothermal" and thought: *I maintain the system that does that. The bore field I check every morning. The heat pumps I troubleshoot at 2 AM when an alarm comes in. The staging sequence I rewrote last November.*

The sustainability report became a knowledge note linked to the geothermal MOC and the existing campus notes. Slide 13B of Bryan's presentation outline got updated with institutional context. The connection between "Bryan's daily work" and "a well-rated sustainability program" became explicit and navigable.

We also finished three PortolanCAST measurement tools that day. Bluebeam Revu — the PDF markup software that every construction professional uses — has measurement tools that Bryan needed replicated. Polylength: click, click, click, each segment gets a length label, running total accumulates. Perimeter: closed polygon, edge lengths shown, snap-to-start to close. Angle: three-point measurement, endpoint-vertex-endpoint, arc indicator with degree label.

Three tools. Each one a verb that a field technician performs with Bluebeam that they now perform in PortolanCAST — Bryan's open-source construction document management tool. Properties panel. Markup list integration. Measure summary. No keyboard shortcuts assigned yet — the editable hotkey system is pending — but the measurements work.

The connection between the geothermal notes and the measurement tools isn't obvious until you think about what Bryan does for a living. He walks through buildings. He looks at equipment. He measures things — pipe runs, duct lengths, clearances, equipment footprints. The knowledge in the vault tells him what he's looking at. The tools in PortolanCAST help him measure it. The sustainability report tells him why it matters.

It's all the same system. The building. The knowledge about the building. The tools that help you see the building clearly. Bryan doesn't separate these because in his work they aren't separate. The bore field is a physical object AND a concept AND a measurement AND a line item in a sustainability report. The atomic notes just make that explicit.

By the end of the day, the campus corner of the vault had gone from a handful of reference documents to a living knowledge network. Eighteen equipment and concept notes. A map of content with four learning paths. A sustainability report with institutional context. Three new measurement tools. And a technician who could now ask his AI assistant "what can go wrong with the bore field?" and get an answer that traces through thermal balance to EWT limits to the specific page in the manual where the geology survey results live.

The field manual isn't a document. It's the vault itself — organized, cross-linked, and waiting for the next question.
